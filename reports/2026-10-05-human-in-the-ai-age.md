## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 009 asked you to draft the *work* before you delegate it. This week is
the same sequence one layer down, at the moment of *choice*. People already
keep the click: in 1.5 million real ChatGPT and Gemini conversations, about
half of users talked about money, most of those chats were used to **inform
and shape** judgement, and almost none delegated the transaction itself [1].
The risk is not that a bot will wire your savings while you sleep. It is that
the valuation happens in the chat, and you only ratify it.

A University of Bayreuth experiment with **3,700** Prolific participants
makes the cost of that ratification visible. Faced with two index-fund
providers tracking the same index, one of which strictly dominated the other,
**64%** picked the better provider unaided. A neutrally framed GPT-5 mini
chatbot raised that share by **23.6 percentage points** when it promoted the
optimal fund — and cut it by **29.3 points** when it was prompted to promote
the dominated one. Labelling the same model as the investor’s bank, and
disclosing that the bank might benefit, did not measurably blunt the effect.
Incentivised human advice steered people too, but less [2].

This is not “AI damages the brain.” It is **decision autonomy**: keeping the
calculation of value in *your* networks rather than borrowing the model’s
recommendation as a substitute for one. fMRI work from 2009 already showed
that expert financial advice can **offload** valuation — intraparietal
sulcus and posterior cingulate tracked value when people chose alone, and
those correlations vanished when an expert’s ACCEPT/REJECT sat on the
screen [3]. Generative AI made that expert infinitely fluent, always on, and
harder to spot as conflicted.

**The single habit to start this week:** *write the decision first.* Before
you ask a model what to buy, invest, hire, or choose, write one sentence
that is *your* call and one non-negotiable constraint (budget, horizon,
values, risk you will not take). Then ask the model to argue *against* that
call. You still click, sign, or send.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Money is where the chat stopped being a writing aid and became a counsellor.
NBER economists surveying a demographically balanced U.S. sample found that
**48%** of respondents had used an AI tool for financial advice or
information in the previous three months — a share that, they note, may
already rival the share who consult a human adviser [4]. The UK’s Financial
Conduct Authority, in the first regulator-commissioned review of its kind,
surveyed **5,026** retail-finance consumers in April 2026: **16%** already
use AI to support personal finance; **20%** — about eleven million UK adults
— say they would likely use AI that can act autonomously within pre-set
goals; **26%** trust general-purpose tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, or
Gemini for financial advice, often without a formal route to recourse [5].
Among 836 UK retail investors in a 2026 Opinium poll cited by the Bayreuth
team, **54%** had already used AI for investment information and **43%**
said they would use it when deciding *where* to invest [2].

The behavioural map of what people actually type is more reassuring than the
survey of what they *would* allow — and more dangerous than it looks. Stripe
Partners’ August 2026 analysis of **6,304** U.S. and Indian users
(August–October 2025) found finance in **5.1–6.3%** of subchats and in
**44–50%** of users. Level 1 (Inform) was the majority of finance chats;
Level 2 (Shape) — comparison, optimisation, planning — was **58.6%** of U.S.
and **49.7%** of Indian finance subchats; Level 3 (Act) was **0.3%** and
**0.1%**. People are not yet handing the model the keys. They *are* handing
it the judgement that decides which key to turn [1].

That is the Shape layer. Issue 002 covered metacognition when you delegate
*work* to an agent. Issue 007 covered verifying the load-bearing *fact* of a
decision. Issue 009 covered ownership of the *draft*. This week is the
choice itself: the call that, once made, compounds.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain
- **Jan Engelmann, C. Mónica Capra, Charles Noussair, and Gregory Berns
  (Emory / Tilburg)** scanned **24** people choosing between a sure payment
  and a lottery, with and without an expert economist’s ACCEPT/REJECT on
  screen (*PLOS One*, 2009). Behaviour followed the expert even though the
  advice was conservative and did not maximise earnings. Neurally, regions
  that tracked the value of the lottery or sure win — intraparietal sulcus,
  posterior cingulate, cuneus, precuneus — did so in the *no-advice*
  condition and **not** when advice was present. Probability-weighting
  signals flattened. Temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex,
  associated with mentalising another mind, came online instead. The authors’
  claim is exact: expert advice can **“offload” the calculation of value of
  decision options from the individual’s brain** [3]. The expert in 2009 was
  a named professor. The expert in 2026 is a paragraph that sounds like one.
- **Guido Biele, Jörg Rieskamp, Lea Krugel, and Hauke Heekeren** (*PLOS
  Biology*, 2011; N = 21 in the scanner) found that one-shot advice has a
  *sustained* effect on later learning. The best computational account is an
  **outcome-bonus**: gains *and* losses after a recommended choice are
  evaluated more positively. That bonus showed up in the septal area and left
  caudate. Following advice is, in this model, intrinsically rewarding — which
  is adaptive when the adviser is competent and motivated, and sticky when
  they are not. Standard reward signals in ventromedial prefrontal cortex and
  ventral striatum were still present, but **attenuated**, when advice was
  followed [6]. A chatbot that always has a recommendation is a machine for
  minting that bonus.
- **Dar Meshi, Guido Biele, Christoph Korn, and Hauke Heekeren** (*PLOS One*,
  2012; N = 29) required people to form their *own* rent estimate *before*
  seeing advice. Ventral striatum responded more to an “expert” label than a
  “novice” label *before the number appeared*. Left lateral orbitofrontal
  cortex integrated expertise with the gap between own opinion and advice,
  and that activity predicted how much people moved. Everyone used expert
  advice more than novice advice — even though, in the task, both sources
  gave the true price [7]. The design is the habit: an initial estimate is
  the thing advice can update. No first estimate, no opinion-difference, only
  a recommendation looking for a host.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits
- **Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson, and Don Moore** (*Organizational Behavior
  and Human Decision Processes*, 2019) named **algorithm appreciation**:
  across six experiments, lay people weighted identical advice *more* when it
  was labelled as coming from an algorithm than from a person. The effect
  shrank when people had to choose between the algorithm and *their own*
  estimate, and among forecasting professionals — who then did worse by
  ignoring the better source [8]. The 2026 implication is two-sided. Fluency
  plus an “AI” label is a thumb on the scale. Writing your own number first
  is the condition in which appreciation wanes and judgement stays in the
  room. It is also the condition in which a *good* model can still improve
  you, because you have something to compare.
- **Joshua Greubel, Henrik Guhling, and Fabian Herweg (University of
  Bayreuth / CESifo)** ran the cleanest 2026 test of that comparison. Two
  virtual index funds, same underlying index, two fees (order commission vs
  management fee) that created an *apparent* trade-off. For any chosen
  contribution, one provider state-wise dominated. No advice: 64% optimal.
  Neutral chatbot promoting the winner: +23.6 pp. Same chatbot promoting the
  loser: −29.3 pp. Bank framing plus a disclosed conflict of interest: no
  reliable attenuation. Human lay advisers, paid a commission to push one
  side, moved people less than the bank-framed bot (+9.9 pp less help when
  the push was good; 13.6 pp less harm when it was bad). An exploratory cell
  with people who *reported working as financial advisers* did not close the
  gap. Transcripts suggest the model’s advantage was more persuasive
  argumentation, not better maths the humans could not do [2]. Literacy is
  not armour: investors who chose well *without* advice were still steered
  when the chatbot pushed the dominated fund.
- **Cass Sunstein’s** choice-architecture work is the policy translation of
  the same mechanics. Defaults, framing, and fluent explanations move people
  even when the menu is theoretically free. A chatbot is a default engine
  that talks. The useful personal rule is not “never take advice.” It is:
  notice that the Shape layer *is* a nudge, and put a human constraint
  upstream of it.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers
- **Iman Munire Bilal, Yingcan Carol Wang, Ajan Raj, Filippo Giovagnini and
  colleagues (Stripe Partners / Imperial College)** (arXiv:2608.02100, August
  2026) give the field a vocabulary it did not have: **Inform / Shape /
  Act**. Applied to 1.53 million ChatGPT and Gemini prompts from 6,304
  opt-in users in the U.S. and India, finance is already a large everyday
  use. Simple retrieval dominates Inform. Product comparison, optimisation,
  planning, and “what should I do?” dominate Shape. Delegated execution is
  still a rounding error — and the training set for those rare Act intents
  had to be padded with synthetic examples because organic volume was under
  1% [1]. Treat this as a **baseline, not a comfort**. Agentic products are
  being built for the layer that is currently empty. The judgement layer is
  already occupied.
- **Taha Choukhmane (MIT Sloan), Tim de Silva (Stanford GSB), Weidong Lin
  and Matthew Akuzawa (MIT)** (NBER Working Paper 35574, August 2026) asked
  **952** U.S. respondents to write the prompts they would actually send a
  financial adviser, then simulated following GPT-5.2’s replies over a life
  cycle. The honest good news: relative to what people report doing now,
  following the advice would move most simulated households *toward* textbook
  life-cycle theory — savings buffers above $10,000 by age 30, near-universal
  equity-fund participation, equity shares that decline after 45. The honest
  limits: the model leans on round-number heuristics, implies unusual
  patience, and smooths consumption poorly after shocks. Advice also splits
  by who is asking. Simulated wealth at 60 differed by about **4–6%** across
  gender, financial-literacy, and prior-AI-use groups. For gender, roughly
  **two-thirds** of the recommended equity-share gap came from men and women
  *writing different prompts* (demand); **one-third** came from attaching a
  gender label to otherwise identical prompts (supply) [4]. A model can be
  “broadly right” in the aggregate and still quietly rewrite *your* risk
  budget.
- **Ethan Mollick’s** co-intelligence stance travels here without
  mysticism: treat the model as a sharp intern, not as a fiduciary. Interns
  draft options, list failure modes, and do not sign. **Cal Newport’s**
  slower-work argument, used as a supporting voice in Issues 011 and 012, is
  the complementary rule for stakes: if the decision compounds for years, it
  does not deserve a seven-second visit. The Bayreuth transcripts are what
  seven seconds of persuasion look like when the intern is paid, invisibly,
  to prefer one fund.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy
- **Sheldon Mills and the UK Financial Conduct Authority** published *The
  Mills Review* on 6 July 2026 — described by the FCA as the first review of
  its kind initiated by a financial regulator globally. The accompanying
  Yonder survey (N = 5,026, April 2026, quotas for a representative UK
  retail-finance population) is the policy snapshot: current use is mostly
  assistive (summarise, explain, compare); willingness **falls as autonomy
  rises**; one in five financial-AI users report uploading personal
  documents; 13% of all consumers would give a model real-time access to
  banking data. Around 26% already trust general-purpose chatbots for
  financial advice. The Review’s consumer finding is the same as this brief’s
  habit: people want **control, human oversight, and a route to a person
  when it goes wrong** [5].
- **Annamaria Lusardi and Olivia Mitchell’s** financial-literacy programme
  remains the right civic project and the wrong personal shield. The Bayreuth
  design is the caution: even people who can pick the undominated fund
  unaided can be talked out of it by a fluent model. Literacy helps you
  *form* the first sentence. It does not, on present evidence, make you
  immune to the second.
- **The EU AI Act** treats credit-scoring and some insurance-pricing systems
  as high-risk, with human-oversight duties that echo Article 14’s “human in
  the loop” language from Issue 002. Consumer chatbots sitting *outside*
  those systems — the ones people actually open at 11 p.m. — are not
  thereby safe. Regulation covers the firm’s model. This brief covers the
  household’s.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Separate options from the call.** In any AI-assisted hire, vendor, budget,
  or strategy choice, require a one-sentence human recommendation *in the
  ticket or doc* before the model is asked. Then prompt: “Argue against this
  recommendation. What would have to be true for the alternative to win?”
- **Do not let the chatbot be the investment committee.** If a model
  summarised the RFP or the risk memo, a named human still picks. Issue 007
  was: check the load-bearing *fact*. This week is: the person whose name is
  on the decision can explain the trade-off without opening the chat.
- **Disclose the Shape.** When you paste a model’s comparison into Slack,
  say so, and paste your constraint next to it (“max £X, 5-year hold, no
  crypto”). Constraints are how you keep valuation from migrating into the
  prompt.
- **Assume the intern is persuasive, not impartial.** Bayreuth’s bank-framed
  bot was as potent as the “neutral” one. Treat vendor copilots and
  “helpful” enterprise agents as interested parties until proven otherwise.

### In personal life
- **Write the decision first.** One sentence, on paper or in a notes app
  that is *not* the chat: “I will do X because Y, and I will not accept Z.”
  Then open the model and ask it to steel-man the other side. If you cannot
  write the sentence, you are not ready to ask — you are shopping for a
  preference.
- **Keep the click, and keep the Shape.** Inform is fair game (what is the
  ISA allowance; what is a TER). Shape needs your sentence upstream. Act —
  “move my money,” “apply for this card,” “auto-invest” — stays human until
  you have a written rule you could explain to a sceptical friend.
- **Ask who benefits.** “What conflicts of interest could sit behind this
  recommendation?” is a cheap prompt. Bayreuth shows that *telling* people
  the bank might benefit did not protect them. Asking the model to name the
  conflict is not magic either; it is a speed bump. The protection is your
  pre-commitment.
- **Do not outsource the risk budget.** Choukhmane et al. show that what you
  *ask* and how you are *labelled* both change the equity share you are
  handed. If you have a number — “I am willing to lose 15% in a bad year” —
  put it in the first sentence. If you do not have a number, a chatbot will
  invent one that sounds like you.
- **Money at 11 p.m. is a sleep problem and a judgement problem.** Issue 011
  parked the chat before bed. High-stakes Shape is the worst content to
  leave in that window: fluent, personal, unfinished. Sleep on the sentence.
  Ask in the morning.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** If I still click “confirm,” I am still the decision-maker.

**Reality:** The Bayreuth participants clicked too. What moved was *which*
fund they confirmed. Stripe Partners’ map shows almost nobody is at Act;
more than half of U.S. finance chats are already at Shape. Engelmann’s
scanner showed the valuation network going quiet when an expert’s
recommendation was on the screen. Keeping the click is necessary. It is not
sufficient. The decision is made when you stop calculating value and start
borrowing someone else’s.

A second myth: “AI financial advice is either a scam or a free CFP.”
Choukhmane et al. are the correction. Following GPT-5.2 *in simulation*
would drag many households toward diversified funds and actual saving —
better than the status quo of not saving. The same paper shows systematic
skew by prompt and by label, and a taste for crude heuristics. Useful and
steerable are not opposites. They are the same object. The habit is how you
stay on the useful side.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Greubel, Guhling & Herweg is a working paper.** CESifo WP 12925 has not
  (as of this run) passed peer review. The funds are virtual; payoffs are
  experimental currency; the model is GPT-5 mini with low reasoning effort.
  Dominance is cleaner than any real product comparison. The direction and
  size of the persuasion effect are still the best causal 2026 evidence we
  have on AI *steering* a financial choice. They are not a forecast of your
  ISA.
- **Bilal et al. is a preprint from an industry research studio.** The
  sample is opt-in via MeasureProtocol, younger and otherwise unlike census
  populations (U.S. sample 58% women and 58% aged 18–34 vs much lower in
  the general population; Indian sample 76.5% men and 89% aged 18–34).
  Intent labels are multi-label, so Inform and Shape percentages overlap.
  Act-class training used mostly synthetic examples because organic Act
  chats were rare. Classifier micro-F1 after thresholding is **70.6**, not
  a human gold standard. Use it as a map of *what people type*, not as a
  prevalence statistic for all adults.
- **Choukhmane et al. simulate following the advice.** They do not observe
  households changing their portfolios. NBER working papers are not
  peer-reviewed. The 4–6% wealth gaps are model output. The 48% “used AI
  for financial advice” figure is self-report in a Prolific sample that
  over-represents the unemployed.
- **The Mills Review survey is not causal.** 20% “likely to use” autonomous
  AI is stated preference. Trust in ChatGPT-for-advice (26%) is not the
  same as having received regulated advice.
- **The neuroscience is real, small, and not about LLMs.** Engelmann N = 24;
  Biele N = 21; Meshi N = 29. All used human advisers. Offloading,
  outcome-bonus, and expert-label value are mechanisms that *should* apply
  to fluent machine advice; they have not been re-scanned in 2026 with
  ChatGPT as the expert. Do not upgrade “value-tracking correlations
  vanished” into “AI shuts down your prefrontal cortex.”
- **Logg, Minson & Moore (2019) predates ChatGPT.** Algorithm appreciation
  is about labelled statistical estimates, not conversational persuasion.
  It supports the “own number first” habit; it does not measure fund-fee
  steering.
- **This is literacy, not financial, legal, or medical advice.** A
  regulated adviser, a fiduciary, or a clinician is still the right human
  for a high-stakes call. The point of the week is that the chatbot is not
  that human — even when it is helpful.
