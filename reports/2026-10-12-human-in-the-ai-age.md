## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 006 asked you to *generate* before you store — protecting memory
encoding when the model becomes an external brain. This week is one layer
upstream, at the moment of *reading*. People are not mainly using AI to
annotate a page they have already seen. In an eight-week field study of
college reading, **30%** of prompts were “summarise this,” comprehension
requests were **60%** of the log, and students described a new pattern the
authors named **reading through AI**: the summary became the primary
material, and the original was consulted only to triage what might be worth
a look [1]. That is not a study habit. It is a change of object. The thing
being understood is no longer the author’s text.

A pre-registered crossover with **195** 18- to 22-year-olds makes the cost
of substitution visible. When an AI summary *replaced* a standardised ACT
passage, people who already read well dropped from **82.5%** to **66.5%**
on the closed-book quiz — the largest hit of four tools tested, Cohen’s
**d = 0.83**. People who had struggled unaided *gained* from the same
summary (44.9% → 55.4%), and gained more still from a Socratic chatbot
that sat *beside* the full passage (→ 63%) [2]. Summaries are not poison.
They are a trade: they lower the load of a hard text, and they strip the
work that builds a **situation model** — the mental model of what the
author is actually saying — from a text you could have handled.

This is not “AI damages the brain.” It is **reading comprehension**: keeping
enough of the original in front of you that your networks, not the model’s
paragraph, construct the meaning. Classic comprehension science already
separated a fluent *feeling* of understanding from the detection of
contradiction [3], and a surface *textbase* from the situation model that
lets you infer, apply, and argue [4]. Generative AI made a fluent,
complete-looking textbase available in one prompt.

**The single habit to start this week:** *read the page, not the summary.*
Before you ask a model to summarise a paper, memo, article, or thread, read
enough of the original to write one sentence in *your* words of what the
author is arguing. Keep the original on screen. Then the model may outline,
quiz, or compress — as a map, not as the territory.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Summarise is the default verb of the AI age. Chatbots, PDF readers, inbox
copilots, and meeting tools all offer to turn a long thing into a short
thing. That is often the right move for triage. It is the wrong move for
the documents that are supposed to change what you know. Walter Kintsch’s
construction–integration account is blunt about the difference: a
reproduction of the wording is not comprehension. Comprehension is building
a situation model — integrating the text with prior knowledge so you can
answer questions the author did not literally write [4]. A summary is, by
design, someone else’s macrostructure. If you never visit the
microstructure, you cannot tell what was dropped.

The field study from the University of Washington and collaborators, now
in *Human–Computer Interaction*, is the behavioural map of that drop. Over
eight weeks, **15** undergraduates logged **838** prompts in **239** reading
sessions. Most sessions (**72%**) contained exactly three prompts — the
assignment minimum. Metacognitive prompts (monitoring, evaluating) were
**8.5%**. Students knew that good prompting took effort; they rarely did
it. Efficiency, deadlines, and a new **illusion of coverage** — the belief
that the summary *is* the reading — filled the gap [1]. Issue 013 was about
whether a citation exists and says what the model claims. This week is
whether *you* have a model of the page at all.

The two-sided finding is the one to keep. Jing Liu and colleagues, recording
EEG during reading with and without AIGC summaries, found that summaries
**reduced** the theta/alpha ratio (a load marker), **shortened** reading
time, **improved** comprehension accuracy, and cut subjective load — more
so on difficult texts [5]. Falk Huettig and Morten Christiansen, writing in
*Cognitive Science*, put the civic stake without the scare: literacy is
already under pressure in many countries; LLMs might accelerate the slide
*or* help reverse it, depending on whether they replace reading or
scaffold it [6]. The habit below is the scaffold: original first, summary
second, one sentence of your own as the test.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain
- **Walter Kintsch** (*Psychological Review*, 1988) remains the load-bearing
  theory. Word meanings and propositions are constructed bottom-up; a
  spreading-activation *integration* step then settles a coherent network.
  Microstructure plus macrostructure make the **textbase** — what the text
  actually said. Deeper comprehension requires a **situation model**: the
  text content integrated with prior knowledge, goals, and often imagery.
  A reader who can only reproduce the wording has a textbase. A reader who
  can infer, apply, and argue has a situation model [4]. An AI summary is
  a finished macrostructure. It can *help* you locate the situation. It
  cannot *be* the construction work, because that work is the integration
  of *this* wording with *your* knowledge.
- **Jing Liu and colleagues** (*The Electronic Library*, 31 July 2026)
  provide the first EEG evidence we have for AIGC summaries as a reading
  nudge. In a 2 × 2 within-subjects design (easy vs difficult text ×
  summary present vs absent), summaries lowered the **theta/alpha ratio**,
  shortened reading time, raised comprehension accuracy, and reduced
  subjective load, with a stronger load-reduction on hard texts [5]. That
  is the honest neurophysiological result: summaries can *help*. Cognitive
  load theory predicts exactly this when the text exceeds working-memory
  capacity. The implication is not “never summarise.” It is: use the
  summary where the original would swamp you, and do not use it where the
  original is the workout your situation model needs. The public abstract
  does not report N; treat the direction as peer-reviewed and the size as
  not yet inspectable from the open record.
- **Christian Gaviria and Javier Corredor** (*Memory & Cognition*, 2025)
  tested whether cognitive load inflates the **illusion of explanatory
  depth**. The load manipulation itself had **no direct effect** on IOED;
  exploratory analyses found that high load predicted worse concurrent
  memory-task performance, which in turn tracked a larger IOED, and that
  more analytic processing tracked a smaller one [7]. That is a thinner
  claim than “load causes overconfidence,” and it is the claim this brief
  will keep. A summary that cuts load can still be a double agent: it can
  free resources for a hard text, and it can also skip the effort that
  would have revealed the gaps. Liu’s EEG finding and this IOED paper are
  not in conflict. They describe two uses of the same relief.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits
- **Arthur Glenberg, Alex Wilkinson, and William Epstein** (*Memory &
  Cognition*, 1982) named the **illusion of knowing**: the belief that
  comprehension has been attained when it has failed. They planted
  contradictions between adjacent sentences and *told* readers to find
  them. Illusions were more common when the contradiction sat at the end
  of a longer text and when it was marked as new information. Readers
  chasing local coherence can feel finished without having checked that
  the pieces fit [3]. A fluent AI summary is an industrial version of that
  local coherence. It is designed to feel complete.
- **Leon Rozenblit and Frank Keil** (*Cognitive Science*, 2002) showed that
  the illusion is especially strong for *explanatory* knowledge — how a
  zipper works, why there are leap years — as opposed to facts, procedures,
  or narratives. People discover the gap only when they have to generate
  the explanation [8]. That is the test this week’s habit borrows: one
  sentence in your own words, from the original, before the model is
  allowed to compress.
- **Ivan Aslanov, Patricio Felmer, and Ernesto Guerra** (OSF / PsyArXiv,
  4 October 2025; N = 102 university students) asked whether a *chatbot
  interface* inflates IOED even when the words are identical. Three groups:
  a custom ChatGPT that returned pre-controlled answers; the same texts on
  screen with no bot; and a no-materials control. The drop from predicted
  to post-explanation self-rating was **largest in the GPT group**. Coders
  scored explanations higher in the no-GPT group (mean 2.75) than the GPT
  group (2.47) on a 4-point scale; GPT answers were also shorter (~44 vs
  ~60 words) and less lexically diverse [9]. Same content, different
  wrapper: retrieving an explanation *from a chatbot* inflated confidence
  and thinned the explanation people could later produce. This is a
  **preprint**. The materials appear to have been processed with Spanish
  stop-words, so do not assume an English-only sample. The design is still
  the right caution for anyone who “understands” a topic because they
  asked ChatGPT to explain it.
- **Matthew Fisher, Mariel Goddu, and Frank Keil** (*Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: General*, 2015) already showed that searching the internet
  for explanations inflates estimates of internal knowledge, including in
  domains the search never touched [10]. The chatbot result extends that
  misattribution story: the feeling “I could explain this” leaks from the
  tool that just did.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers
- **Yue Fu, Joel Wester, Niels van Berkel, and Alexis Hiniker** (University
  of Washington / Copenhagen / Aalborg; *Human–Computer Interaction*,
  published online 18 August 2026) logged real reading, not a quiz. Summary
  prompts were **30.3%** of 838 turns; inference 23.2%; explanation 21.8%.
  Together, three-quarters of what students asked the model to do was
  *process the text for them*. Locating prompts (“does the article
  mention…”) let students test their existing interests against the AI’s
  account without reading. Vocabulary questions were asked about words in
  the *summary*, not the original. The authors distinguish an **illusion of
  coverage** (the summary stands in for the source) from the older illusion
  of competence (I understand). Coverage feeds competence. N = 15, five
  interviews, no comprehension test: this is a map of behaviour, not a
  causal estimate of learning [1]. It is the best map we have of how
  “summarise this PDF” actually gets used.
- **Muhan Xu, Xiaomei Li, Rebecca Fiebrink, and colleagues** (*Interactive
  Learning Environments*, 1 March 2026; N = 29 HCI graduate students)
  compared conversational summarisation (ChatGPT-style) with **structural**
  summarisation (a map of the paper’s sections) against a plain PDF, on
  10-minute categorisation tasks. Structural overviews helped people
  *navigate* and locate information, with better user-experience ratings
  and lower perceived load than a conversational summary. Participants
  still reported verification as slow and over-reliance as a risk. The
  authors’ design recommendation is the practitioner version of this
  week’s habit: combine a structural map with the original, clarify the
  reading *intent* before summarising, and shift from answer-giving to
  guided questions [11]. Bounded by gist-categorisation, two different
  LLMs across conditions, and an HCI sample. Do not treat it as a
  comprehension RCT.
- **The Etkin tools themselves** are a design lesson. In the crossover
  below, a Socratic chatbot *beside* the full passage was the kindest tool
  for struggling readers and the least harmful for strong ones. A summary
  *in place of* the passage was the unkindest tool for strong readers [2].
  If your product can only do one thing, keep the source visible.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy
- **Hudson Etkin, Kai Etkin, Ryan Carter, and Camarin Rolle** (*Frontiers
  in Education*, 2025; Stanford / Alto Neuroscience / San José State)
  ran the cleanest causal test we have of GPT reading aids. Pre-registered
  4 March 2024; 228 Prolific 18- to 22-year-olds; 33 removed for chance
  performance or time outliers; N = 195. Five conditions in random order:
  AI summary *instead of* the passage, AI outline of the passage, Q&A
  tutor, Socratic chatbot with the passage, and no AI. Quizzes were
  closed-book. Split on control-passage accuracy: low performers improved
  with every tool, most with Socratic (**d = 0.86**); high performers
  declined with every tool, most with the summary (**d = 0.83**). Means
  for high performers: control 82.5%, summary 66.5%, Socratic 76.3%,
  tutor 75.1%, outline 72.4%. An SAT/ACT split replicated the summary
  harm for high scorers, with smaller effects [2]. The honesty move is in
  §7: replacing the text is not the same as putting a map on top of it.
- **Falk Huettig (MPI) and Morten H. Christiansen (Cornell / Aarhus)**
  (*Cognitive Science*, August 2024) argue that cognitive science should
  not leave literacy-under-LLMs to U.S. tech firms. Literacy is already
  declining in many countries, with knock-ons for associated cognitive
  skills and susceptibility to fake news. LLMs might worsen that *or*
  help — especially for SES-disadvantaged pupils, if they scaffold
  turn-taking that *promotes* literacy rather than short-circuiting it
  [6]. Their “turn-taking” recommendation rhymes with Etkin’s Socratic
  condition and with this week’s one-sentence test.
- **Maryanne Wolf** has been a voice in this series since the pilot:
  deep reading is a circuit the brain *builds* through practice with
  demanding text, not a default app. This week’s evidence is the
  empirical update. The circuit does not fire on a substitute paragraph.
  It fires on the page.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Original stays on screen.** For any paper, spec, contract, incident
  report, or strategy memo that you will be asked about, do not paste
  “summarise this” as the first move. Scroll the source. Write one
  sentence of the argument or the claim. *Then* ask for an outline, a
  comparison table, or a quiz.
- **Use summaries as maps, not substitutes.** Structural overviews
  (section headings, “where is the method / the risk / the number”) are
  the practitioner-supported use [11]. Conversational “so what does this
  say?” is the use that most resembles Etkin’s replacement condition.
- **Name the load-bearing paragraph.** After the model returns, go back
  to the original and highlight the sentence that actually bears the
  decision. Issue 007 was: verify the load-bearing *fact*. This week is:
  verify that you have *seen* the sentence, not only the paraphrase.
- **Socratic, not oracular, for onboarding.** If a colleague is new to a
  dense doc, a chatbot that *asks them questions with the PDF open* is
  closer to Etkin’s helpful condition than a summary that lets them skip
  it. Close the chat and have them teach back one claim.

### In personal life
- **Read the page, not the summary.** News, Substack, papers, school
  readings, the terms you are about to click: two or three minutes in the
  original, one sentence in a notes app that is *not* the chat, then
  (if you still want it) a summary as a check, not a replacement. If you
  cannot write the sentence, you have not read it yet.
- **Ask the model to quiz, not to digest.** “Give me three questions I
  should be able to answer from this article, then hide the answers” keeps
  the situation-model work on your side of the table. “Summarise this”
  moves it to the model’s.
- **Watch the illusion of coverage.** If you find yourself asking “does
  this article mention X?” of the chatbot rather than of the text, you
  are already reading through AI. Open the PDF. Search *there*.
- **Hard text is the exception, not the rule.** If a paper is above your
  current vocabulary or a second language, a summary-as-scaffold matches
  the EEG result [5] and Etkin’s low-performer gains [2]. Use it to *enter*
  the original, then stay.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** “AI summaries make you a faster reader of the same material —
same understanding, less time.”

**Reality:** Sometimes they make you a faster reader of a *different*
material. Etkin’s high performers lost sixteen quiz points when the
summary replaced the passage [2]. Fu’s students treated the summary as
the object of study and the original as a lookup table [1]. Aslanov’s
participants felt they understood more, and produced thinner explanations,
when the identical text arrived through a chatbot rather than on the page
[9]. The speed is real. Liu’s EEG even shows lower load and *better*
accuracy when the summary rides along with the task, especially on hard
text [5]. The myth is the “same understanding” clause. A situation model
built from the author’s sentences is not the same object as a situation
model built from a model’s paraphrase. Faster can be better. Faster is
not equivalent.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Etkin replaced the text.** The summary condition did not put a map on
  top of the ACT passage; it *withheld* the passage. High-performer harm
  is therefore a mix of (a) losing the original, (b) possible regression
  to the mean from a median split on the control quiz, and (c) closed-book
  questions that punish anyone who never saw the wording. It is *not* a
  clean estimate of “summary + original.” The Socratic condition, which
  kept the passage, still slightly hurt high performers (82.5% → 76.3%) —
  a smaller, still real, dent. Sample is 18–22 on Prolific, one session,
  US English. Do not generalise to expert professional reading without
  that grain of salt.
- **Fu is a behaviour log, not a learning trial.** N = 15, one elective
  course, a required three-prompt minimum that truncated sessions, five
  interviews. No comprehension or retention test. “Reading through AI”
  is a qualitative pattern with quantitative prompt counts. It is
  peer-reviewed as of August 2026; it is still a small, specific sample.
- **Liu’s EEG is two-sided and incompletely reported in the open
  abstract.** Summaries *helped* accuracy and reduced load. That is the
  opposite of a scare finding, and it is the one this brief will not bury.
  We do not have N, effect sizes, or channel details from the public page.
  Until the full paper is in hand, cite the direction, not a magnitude.
- **Aslanov is a preprint.** Identical content, different interface is a
  strong design; coder N and language of testing need the published
  version. Do not treat IOED-via-chatbot as settled.
- **Xu is navigation, not comprehension**, N = 29, ten-minute tasks, two
  different LLMs across conditions.
- **Kreijkes et al.** (*Computers & Education* 243, 2026; N = 405, ages
  14–15) already appeared in Issue 006: note-taking, alone or with an
  LLM, beat LLM-alone on comprehension and three-day retention, even
  though students *preferred* the model. It corroborates this week; it is
  not new evidence. Generation still beats consumption. This week adds:
  consumption of a *summary* is not consumption of the *page*.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing here is a lesion, a
  longitudinal IQ drop, or a claim about permanent change. The evidence
  is about *patterns of use*: which object you understand (page vs
  paraphrase), and whether your confidence matches what you can later
  say.
