## 1. Executive Summary

Last week we looked at how AI shapes the *diversity* of our ideas. This week we go one layer
deeper — into **memory itself**. AI is quietly becoming our external memory: it summarizes the
meeting, keeps the notes, recalls the thread, and hands back a tidy answer the moment we ask.
That is genuinely useful. But a striking new brain-imaging study suggests a cost we rarely feel
in the moment. Scarampi, Sachdeva, Tsai and Gilbert (UCL) used fMRI to watch what happens when
people **offload** something to an external store instead of committing it to memory. Their
finding: the brain's "offload" signal looked **almost identical to a deliberate "forget"
signal**, and the neural trace of the to-be-remembered information **faded until it was
statistically absent** — the same as when people were told to forget it [1]. When you decide the
machine will hold it, your brain, quite sensibly, stops holding it too.

That neural picture lines up with two fresh randomized controlled trials. In a pre-registered
experiment with 405 secondary-school students (a Cambridge–Microsoft Research team),
**note-taking — alone or combined with an LLM — beat using the LLM alone** on both comprehension
and three-day retention, even though most students *preferred* the LLM and found it more helpful
[2]. In a separate RCT (n = 120), undergraduates who studied with ChatGPT scored **57.5%** on a
surprise test 45 days later versus **68.5%** for those who studied traditionally (d = 0.68) —
and their forgetting curve was **steeper**, pointing to weaker encoding at the outset, not just
weaker recall later [3]. The common thread is one of the most durable findings in cognitive
psychology: the **generation effect** — we remember what we *produce* far better than what we
merely *read* [4].

**The single habit to start this week:** *generate before you store.* Whenever AI hands you
something you'll actually need later — a decision, an explanation, the three takeaways from a
meeting — **close the tab and say or write it in your own words, from memory, first.** Sixty
seconds of effortful recall converts a passing glance into an actual memory. Let AI hold the
archive; make your brain do the encoding.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The default is shifting from *retrieval* to *generation-and-storage on our behalf.* The old
"Google effect" (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, *Science*, 2011) already showed that when we expect a
fact to remain available on a computer, we remember **where to find it rather than the fact
itself** [5]. AI raises the stakes: it doesn't just store the information, it performs the
**thinking** — the summarizing, connecting, and explaining that used to be *our* job, and that
happens to be exactly the effortful work that lays down memory. Meeting-notetakers, "recall"
features, always-on assistants, and auto-summaries mean the moment of encoding is increasingly
skipped by design.

Why care, if the archive is always one query away? Two reasons. First, **judgment runs on what
you actually know.** You cannot notice that an AI answer is wrong, connect two ideas across a
week, or think on your feet in a conversation using knowledge that lives only in a document you'd
have to go re-open. Retrieval you can *feel* is the raw material of expertise and good decisions.
Second, and easy to miss: the students in these studies **preferred** the AI and rated it as
more helpful — while learning *less* from it [2][3]. That gap between how learning *feels* and
how much actually sticks is the trap. The reassuring part, repeated across every study here, is
that this is about the **pattern of use**, not damage: the same tools help a lot when you let
them handle storage and retrieval while *you* keep doing the generating.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Offloading looks like deliberate forgetting (Scarampi, Sachdeva, Tsai & Gilbert, UCL; fMRI;
  preprint, Oct 2025).** Participants saw pictures and were cued to either *remember* them (a
  test was coming), *offload* them (the test would come with a reminder), or *forget* them (no
  test). Two results stand out. (a) **Offload cues produced brain activity that closely resembled
  forget cues.** (b) Using multivariate pattern analysis, the neural trace of the information
  **persisted only in the "remember" condition** and **faded to statistical absence in both the
  offload and forget conditions**, which did not differ from each other [1]. Sam Gilbert is one of
  the field's leading offloading researchers, and the team frames this as likely **adaptive** — a
  limited-capacity brain sensibly declining to store what it's been told it won't need. The
  lesson isn't "never offload"; it's that offloading is a real decision with a real memory cost,
  so *choose* what you hand over.

- **Why generating beats reading — measured in the pupil (*Memory & Cognition*, Sep 2025,
  peer-reviewed).** Across four experiments using pupillometry (an
  independent, physiological index of mental effort), self-*generated* items were remembered
  better than *read* items, and generation reliably **dilated the pupil more** — i.e., recruited
  more effort — with that extra effort partially *accounting* for the memory boost [4]. This is the
  mechanism under the classroom results: producing information forces the effortful, attentive
  processing that builds a durable trace; passively receiving a fluent answer does not.

- **Supporting signal from the lab.** A 2026 Cognitive Neuroscience Society poster reports that
  when people **offloaded mnemonic generation to ChatGPT** (typing out the AI's mnemonic) rather
  than making their own, both **recognition and cued-recall were significantly impaired** — whether
  or not study time was equated [6]. It's a small conference study, but it isolates the exact move
  this brief is about: letting AI do the *generating*.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **The generation effect is old, large, and directly actionable.** First described by Slamecka &
  Graf in 1978 and replicated for decades, the generation effect is a workhorse of memory science:
  a meta-analysis (Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel, 2007) put the within-subjects effect at
  about **d = 0.50** [4]. Behaviourally, it converts into cheap habits — turn a summary into a
  question you answer from memory; explain the idea to a colleague; write the takeaway in your own
  words before you copy the AI's. Each move swaps a few seconds of *effort* for a real gain in
  *retention*.

- **Fluency is not learning — and it points the wrong way.** In both RCTs, learners **preferred**
  the easier, AI-mediated path and judged it more helpful, yet remembered **less** [2][3]. This is
  the classic "desirable difficulties" warning (Bjork): the conditions that make studying *feel*
  smooth are often the ones that make it *stick less*. Practically, treat "that was easy and I
  feel like I've got it" as a **cue to add a quick self-test**, not as evidence you've learned it.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Let AI hold the archive; keep the encoding human (Kreijkes et al., incl. Microsoft Research
  "Tools for Thought"; *Computers & Education*, 2026, peer-reviewed).** In a pre-registered RCT
  with 405 English secondary students (344 analysed), each student studied history passages under
  different conditions and was tested three days later. **Note-taking alone, and note-taking
  combined with the LLM, both significantly beat the LLM-alone condition** on comprehension and
  retention — and combining the two beat using the LLM by itself [2]. The practitioner takeaway is
  not "avoid AI," it's **workflow design**: use the model to make hard material accessible and to
  spark interest, but keep a step where *you* produce the notes, the summary, the answer. AI for
  access and scaffolding; human for the effortful part that becomes memory.

- **AI offloads the *process*, not just the *storage* (cognitive-crutch RCT, *Social Sciences &
  Humanities Open*, 2025, peer-reviewed).** The authors argue that unlike a calculator or a
  notebook — which offload a narrow task or a fact — generative AI can offload **comprehension,
  synthesis, and explanation** wholesale [3]. That's why they see not just a retrieval dependency
  but **weaker initial encoding** (the steeper forgetting curve), and why the hit was **largest
  for technical, conceptual material** (d ≈ 0.92) — precisely where AI feels most helpful [3]. The
  design principle: offload the *typing and formatting*; never fully offload the *understanding*.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Preference and learning can point in opposite directions.** The most policy-relevant finding
  across these studies is not the average deficit but the **preference–performance inversion**:
  students consistently rated the AI condition as better while it taught them least [2][3]. For
  schools and training programs, that means **satisfaction and usage metrics are not proxies for
  learning**, and "students like it" is not evidence a tool works. It argues for keeping
  retrieval-based activities — writing, self-testing, explaining — structurally in the loop rather
  than optional.

- **Design for "AI-after-attempt," not "AI-first."** Both RCT teams converge on the same
  remedy: have learners **attempt and self-quiz first**, then bring in AI to check answers, surface
  gaps, and give targeted feedback — using the model as a **retrieval coach**, not an answer
  engine [2][3]. Retrieval practice remains one of the best-evidenced study techniques we have;
  the task for the AI era is to sequence the tool *after* the effortful attempt, so the tool
  amplifies encoding instead of replacing it.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Generate before you store.** After an AI answer you'll need later, close the tab and write the
  key point(s) in *your own words, from memory* — then reopen to check. The producing is what
  encodes it [1][4].
- **Turn summaries into questions.** Instead of re-reading an AI summary of a doc or meeting, write
  1–3 questions with checkable answers and answer them yourself first. AI is great at *drafting*
  the questions; it must not *answer* them for you [2][3].
- **Attempt first, AI second.** For anything you're trying to learn, take a real swing and a quick
  self-quiz *before* asking the model. Use AI to grade, fill gaps, and give a follow-up — a
  retrieval coach, not an answer engine [3].
- **Notes stay human where memory matters.** Let an AI notetaker capture the transcript, but write
  your *own* three takeaways and next actions. The one who writes the summary is the one who
  remembers the meeting [2].
- **Watch the fluency trap.** "That felt easy, I've got it" is a cue to add a 30-second self-test —
  not proof you've learned it [2][3].

### In personal life

- **Keep a few things in your own head, on purpose.** Learn the new colleague's name, the recipe,
  the route, a couple of key numbers — decide what's worth *knowing*, and let AI hold the rest [1][5].
- **Explain it to a person.** Just read a great AI explainer? Teach it to a friend or partner from
  memory. Explaining is generation; it's one of the strongest ways to make something stick [4].
- **Retrieve before you re-look-up.** Catch yourself re-Googling / re-asking the same thing — try to
  recall it first. Each successful retrieval strengthens the memory; each instant lookup weakens the
  reason to have one [5].
- **Choose what to offload.** Offloading isn't the enemy — mindless, blanket offloading is. Before
  you file something with the machine, ask: *is this something I actually want to know?* If yes,
  spend the 60 seconds to encode it [1].

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "AI remembers everything for me, so I don't need to — the archive is always a query
> away."
> **Reality:** An external archive and a personal memory do different jobs. AI is excellent at
> *storage and retrieval*; only *your* memory powers real-time judgment, noticing when an answer is
> off, connecting ideas across time, and thinking fluently in a live conversation. And the act of
> deciding "the machine will hold this" appears to make your brain **stop holding it**, with neural
> correlates resembling deliberate forgetting [1]. Crucially, this is **not** "AI rots your brain":
> the deficits in these studies came from letting AI do the *generating*, and they largely
> disappear when you keep the effortful step — attempt first, note in your own words, self-test —
> for yourself [2][3][4]. Use AI as your archive and your coach; keep being your own memory.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **The headline neuroscience study is a preprint.** Scarampi et al. [1] is not yet peer-reviewed,
  uses a lab picture-memory task (not real AI use), and its authors explicitly read the effect as
  *adaptive* prioritisation — not harm. Treat it as a mechanism, not a verdict.
- **The two RCTs have real limits.** Kreijkes et al. [2] tested 14–15-year-olds on short history
  passages with a 3-day delay; the cognitive-crutch RCT [3] had **29% attrition** (120 enrolled,
  85 tested), a single Brazilian business-program sample, one model (GPT-4, web), and a
  "learning-by-teaching" task — so exact magnitudes shouldn't be over-generalised. Both are
  causal and pre-registered, which is a genuine strength.
- **This is about learning tasks, not all AI use.** Much offloading is entirely fine — you don't
  need to memorise a phone tree or a boilerplate email. The evidence is about the material you
  actually want to *know* and reason with later.
- **"Better memory" isn't the only goal.** As the cognitive-crutch authors note, AI may build other
  real skills (synthesis, prompt design, workflow speed, drafting) their retention test didn't
  measure [3]. The aim is balance, not abstinence.
- **The generation effect is robust but bounded.** It's larger *within* individuals than *between*,
  and lab effect sizes vary with how "generation" is manipulated [4]. It's a reliable lever, not a
  magic one — which is why the habits above are small and repeatable rather than heroic.
