## 1. Executive Summary

Last week’s brief went to the night the brain uses to finish the day’s work. This week
goes to the *day itself*: the **attention** that has to stay on one working sphere long
enough for thinking to happen. Knowledge workers in a 2026 telemetry study switched
applications a **mean of 908 times per person-day**. The typical spell in any one app
lasted **27.8 seconds** on average — and the **median was 3.3 seconds**. Nearly nine in
ten of them also opened a generative-AI tool, a **median of 13 times a day**, usually for
about **seven seconds** a visit [1]. That is not “multitasking.” It is a workday made of
handoffs.

The surprising part, and the reason this is not a scare brief, is what happens *after*
the model answers. On days with more AI use, overall switching was higher — but the
window following an AI visit packed into **fewer apps, longer (if still brief) dwells,
more predictable sequences, and more time in learning and developer tools** [1]. AI did
not look like an extra slot machine. It looked like a **structuring episode sitting on
top of an already-fragmented day**, whose main engine was **communication load**, not
the chatbot. Weekends and holidays **reset** the fragmentation; it then climbed again
toward Friday [1].

Sustained attention is not a personality trait you either have or lost in 2012. A
preregistered RCT that blocked mobile internet for two weeks improved objective
sustained attention by about **d_z = 0.23** — small, real, and in the same ballpark as
a decade of age-related change on that particular task [2]. The lever is the
**workday’s design**, not a diagnosis of a broken brain.

**The single habit to start this week:** *stay after the answer.* When the model
returns, **do not open Slack, email, or another prompt**. Stay in the destination file
or problem for **one named 25-minute block**. If you must switch, spend fifteen seconds
on a **ready-to-resume** note — where you were, what is left, what you will do when you
come back [5]. Agents can run concurrently. Your attention cannot.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Generative AI did not invent fragmentation. Gloria Mark’s logging already showed screen
focus falling from about **2.5 minutes in 2004** to about **47 seconds** (median
**40 seconds**) in the late 2010s [6]. What AI changed is the *tempo of the handoff*.
The model answers in seconds. Each answer is an invitation to switch roles: prompter,
reviewer, editor, colleague-in-chat, then prompter again. The machines are concurrent.
The human attentional system is sequential, and every incomplete task leaves
**attention residue** on the next one [4].

That residue is now cheap to manufacture. Thirteen seven-second AI visits, interleaved
with Teams and email, is a day with dozens of unfinished loops. Issue 007 covered
*automation complacency* (trusting the output). Issue 008 covered *empty gaps* (the
default-mode network). This week is the complementary cost: even when you *intend* to
think, the **switch itself** taxes working memory and goal maintenance. The unit of
intervention in the 2026 data is not “the employee who is bad at focus.” It is **today’s
workday** — 44.6% of fragmentation variance was **within the same person, day to day**,
more than the 35.8% that lived between people and far more than the 19.6% between
organizations [1]. You are not doomed by temperament. Tomorrow’s day can be a different
shape.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain
- **Melina Uncapher (UCSF) and Anthony Wagner (Stanford)** reviewed a decade of media-
  multitasking work in *PNAS*. The literature is mixed — many nulls, almost no positive
  associations with working memory — but when a relationship appears, it is usually on
  tasks that need **sustained, goal-directed attention**. Their working account is an
  **attentional-lapse** hypothesis: heavier media multitaskers have more trouble
  *staying* on task and *returning* after a lapse. They are explicit that **causality is
  not established**; people who already lapse more may also juggle more streams [3].
  The ancestor finding is **Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009)**: heavy media multitaskers were
  more distracted by irrelevant stimuli on a filter task [7]. Use this as a caution
  about *habit*, not as a scan of a damaged cortex.
- **Michael Esterman (Boston University / VA Boston)**, co-author of the 2025 mobile-
  internet RCT, has spent years showing that sustained attention fluctuates and can be
  measured objectively with the **gradCPT** (a go/no-go continuous performance task).
  In the RCT, two weeks without mobile internet improved gradCPT *d-prime* (Intervention
  T1→T2 **d_z = 0.24**; pooled ITT **d_z = 0.23**; delayed-intervention replication
  **d_z = 0.28**) [2]. Sleep, social time, and “offline world” activities mediated
  well-being — **none of them mediated the attention gain**. The authors’ reading: fewer
  constant opportunities to switch may itself be practice at staying on one stimulus
  [2].
- **Stephen Monsell’s** classic review of **task-switch costs** is the lab backbone
  under all of this. Even when you know the next task is coming, switching is slower
  and more error-prone than repeating, because the brain must reconfigure goals,
  stimulus–response rules, and working-memory contents [8]. Thirteen AI visits a day
  are thirteen extra reconfigurations, before you count Slack.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits
- **Sophie Leroy** named **attention residue**: cognitions about Task A that persist
  after you have moved to Task B, and that measurably hurt Task B — especially when
  A was **unfinished** [4]. That is the state a half-written prompt, a still-generating
  window, or an unanswered Slack thread leaves you in.
- **Leroy & Theresa Glomb (2018)** tested the fix in *Organization Science*: a
  **ready-to-resume plan**. Before you switch, take about a minute to note where you
  left off, what is left, and what you will do when you return. Across four studies,
  that brief closure reduced residue and protected performance on the interrupting
  task [5]. It is the smallest intervention in this brief, and the most portable.
- **Gloria Mark (UC Irvine)** is the field’s metronome. Average attention on one screen
  fell from ~150 seconds (2004) to ~75 seconds (~2012) to ~**47 seconds** (median
  **40**) in recent logging [6]. After an interruption, it took on the order of
  **25 minutes** to return to the original working sphere, often via a couple of
  intervening tasks. Mark’s applied stance is not monastic abstinence; it is
  **meta-awareness** and **day design** — you will switch, so design *when*.
- **Noah Castelo, Kostadin Kushlev, Adrian Ward, Michael Esterman & Peter Reiner**
  supply the causal personal-life result: blocking mobile internet (calls and texts
  still worked; desktop internet still existed) for two weeks cut iPhone screen time
  from **314 to 161 minutes** (d_z = 2.22) and improved mental health (ITT d_z = 0.56)
  and sustained attention (ITT d_z = 0.23). **91%** of completers improved on at least
  one of the three preregistered outcomes [2]. Completers had better baseline attention
  than dropouts; only **119 of 467** kept the block ≥10 days. Treat the effect as
  **real and modest**, not as a miracle cure, and not as an AI-specific finding.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers
- **Sumer S. Vaid and Ashley V. Whillans (Harvard Business School)** analysed
  **103 million** second-by-second application events from **1,017** employees in
  **eight** knowledge-work organizations (law, finance, accounting, software, and
  others) — **62,693 person-days**, **56.9 million** switches. This is the first
  large-scale, within-person look at **generative AI and digital fragmentation**
  together. Headline numbers: mean **908.3** switches per person-day; median employee
  **814**; typical within-person swing **~383** switches from one day to the next.
  **906 / 1,017 (89.1%)** used at least one AI tool. Median **13** AI episodes per
  using day; median episode **7 seconds**. Same-day AI duration tracked a higher
  Fragmentation Index (within-person β = **0.013 per minute**, *P* < .001). And
  then the honesty clause the abstract leads with: after AI use, networks were
  **more modular** (β = 0.028) and **less dense** (β = −0.025), with fewer unique
  apps, fewer switches, slightly longer dwells (β = **531 ms**), and destinations
  skewed to **learning platforms (rate ratio 4.04)** and **developer tools (2.25)**
  rather than Office (0.93) [1].
- **Cal Newport (Georgetown computer science)** spent March 2026 arguing that we
  need a **cognitive-fitness** revolution analogous to the mid-century fitness
  revolution — not because AI is evil, but because ultraprocessed feeds plus
  on-demand generation are making *deep thought* rarer even when the calendar has
  holes in it [9]. His 2026 update to *Deep Work*: (1) a **hybrid attention**
  model (protect remote days as meeting-and-message-free deep days); (2) **rules
  for AI**, including *don’t let the model write the thing whose writing is the
  thinking*. His five-part cognitive-fitness routine is daily **reading**, seeking
  out **writing** instead of fleeing the blank page, **thinking walks** without a
  usable phone, **parking the phone** at home, and learning a **hard skill** [9].
  Issue 009 already used “draft first”; this week the relevant piece is the
  **attention architecture** around the draft.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy
- **Whillans’s lab** frames fragmentation as mostly **work design**, not character.
  Organization explained only **19.6%** of variance. Communication apps showed an
  inverted-U: more comms, more fragmentation — until comms *dominated* the day, at
  which point switching among the remaining apps fell. Concentrated **mornings**
  predicted more consolidated **afternoons** (afternoon switching β = −54.7,
  surviving a control for the morning rate) [1]. If you manage a team, the
  cheapest policy is not “use less AI.” It is **protect a named morning block**
  and **batch communication**.
- **Australia’s under-16 social-media restriction**, which Newport cites as a
  digital-nutrition analogue of a trans-fat ban, is a *developmental* policy, not
  an adult workplace one [9]. It is noted here as a signal that attention is
  entering public-health language — not as a recommendation to copy it onto office
  Slack. Adult evidence for blunt bans is the Castelo trial: helpful for the
  minority who comply, leaky for everyone else [2].
- **EU AI Act Article 14** (human oversight of high-risk systems) still assumes a
  human who can *notice*. A human switching 900 times a day is not overseeing.
  Oversight is an **attentional** requirement, not a checkbox. (Issue 002 covered
  the legal frame; this week is the cognitive precondition.)

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Stay after the answer.** After each AI return, set a 25-minute timer and remain in
  the *destination* artefact (the code, the memo, the analysis). No new prompt, no
  Slack, no “quick email.” This rides the structuring window Vaid & Whillans actually
  measured, instead of wasting it [1].
- **Name the block before you start.** One working-sphere name on the calendar or a
  sticky (“Q3 forecast, rows 40–80”), not “catch up.” Concentrated mornings predicted
  calmer afternoons in the telemetry [1].
- **Batch the thirteen.** If you currently open ChatGPT/Copilot in seven-second
  snatches, collapse them into **one or two named AI sessions** (for example 11:30 and
  3:30) rather than 13 ambient visits [1].
- **Ready-to-resume, then switch.** When Slack wins, write one sentence: *where I was /
  what’s left / first action when I return.* Then switch fully [5].
- **Batch communication, don’t nibble it.** Communication load, not AI, was the
  workday’s fragmentation engine [1]. Two email/chat windows beat a day of badges.
- **If you lead a team:** pick one morning without meetings and without “quick
  questions” on chat. Organization explained little of the variance; **day design**
  explained more [1].

### In personal life
- **One phone-away block.** You do not need Castelo’s full two-week internet fast.
  Put the phone in another room for one 25-minute reading or thinking block. The RCT
  improved attention without touching desktop internet [2].
- **Read something with a spine.** Newport’s first cognitive-fitness move is daily
  reading as practice aiming the mind’s eye at one internal target — start at 15–20
  pages of whatever you will actually finish [9].
- **A thinking walk without a usable phone.** Phone in a bag, not a pocket, if it must
  come along [9]. This is Issue 008’s empty gap, now serving attention rather than
  incubation.
- **Notifications in batches, not in real time.** The Castelo paper notes prior field
  experiments: batched smartphone notifications beat a drip of them for self-reported
  attentional functioning [2].
- **Do not use “47-second attention span” as a self-diagnosis.** It is a *screen-switch*
  statistic, not a measure of your worth or of irreversible decline [6]. Two weeks of
  fewer switches moved an objective attention score [2].

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** Generative AI is just another tab, and it is shredding our attention the way
social media did.

**Reality:** In the only large 2026 telemetry we have, people used AI on their *already
more fragmented* days, in **brief, frequent** visits (median 13 × 7 seconds). After
those visits, switching **narrowed** toward learning and builder tools — a structuring
pattern, not a slot-machine pattern [1]. The bigger, older engine is **communication
interleaving**, and the thing that looks like a “destroyed attention span” is mostly
**workday design plus unfinished loops**. AI can still *manufacture extra loops* if
you treat every seven-second answer as a reason to start a new thread. The myth’s
grain of truth is the **13 micro-visits**. The myth’s error is blaming the model for
what Slack and the unfinished prompt already do.

A second, related myth: “Humans now have a 47-second attention span.” Mark’s number is
**time on one screen before a switch**, average not destiny, and Vaid’s computer-foreground
median is even shorter (3.3 seconds) because the logging is finer — not because
prefrontal cortex expired in 2012 [1, 6]. Attention is a **practice**, and it moved in
two weeks when the environment stopped inviting a switch every few minutes [2].

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Vaid & Whillans is a preprint / HBS working paper, not yet peer-reviewed.** It is
  observational telemetry from eight organizations in India that largely employ
  knowledge workers. It cannot say AI *causes* fragmentation. The honest reading is
  **co-occurrence plus a post-AI structuring signature**. Reverse causality is live:
  chaotic days may *send* people to the chatbot. The post-AI dwell increase of
  **531 milliseconds** is statistically clear at this sample size and **practically
  tiny** — do not advertise it as “AI creates deep work.” The more meaningful
  post-AI signals are **modularity, density, destination mix, and the hourly peak-
  then-decline** [1].
- **Castelo et al. is causal for *mobile internet*, not for ChatGPT.** Waitlist
  control; high non-compliance (25.5% met the ≥10-day rule); completers already had
  better mental health and attention at baseline; sample was motivated to cut phone
  use (83% scored ≥5/7). ITT attention effect is **small (d_z = 0.23)**. The “ten
  years of aging” line is a **task-norm comparison**, not a claim that phones aged
  anyone’s brain by a decade [2]. Issue 011 already used this paper for *sleep*;
  this week uses the **attention** outcome that had not been the anchor.
- **Media-multitasking neuroscience is correlational and mixed.** Uncapher & Wagner
  warn against saying multitasking *rewires* the brain. About half the working-memory
  studies are null; none show a significant *benefit* [3]. Ophir 2009 is small-N [7].
- **The 2017 “brain drain” mere-presence finding (Ward et al., JACR) did not reliably
  replicate** in later preregistered work. Castelo et al. themselves note that mere-
  presence effects are small and inconsistent in meta-analysis [2]. This brief does
  not lean on it.
- **Ready-to-resume is a lab-tested habit, not an AI trial.** It generalizes to
  prompt-and-Slack interruptions by mechanism, not by direct 2026 evidence [5].
- **This is literacy, not clinical advice.** Persistent inability to focus, suspected
  ADHD, depression, or anxiety belong with a clinician. Do not self-treat with a
  chatbot or a productivity rule.
