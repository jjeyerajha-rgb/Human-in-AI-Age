## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 015 asked you to write the *practical* call first —
which fund, which hire, which purchase — so the model
shapes facts, not the click. This week is one layer
deeper, and it is not the same brief. It is the
**values** call: who is owed, what is fair, who bears
the cost, what you would still defend as right if the
chat disappeared. **Moral judgment and responsibility**
— character, not calculation.

People *say* they do not want machines making moral
decisions. In nine studies, Yochanan Bigman and Kurt
Gray found that aversion — driving, parole, medicine,
the military — and traced it to the sense that machines
neither fully think nor feel [8]. That is the survey
answer. The behavioural answer, once *you* hold the
choice, is the reverse. Nicola Hüholt and Nora Szech,
in the *European Economic Review* (online 8 January
2026), put **5,639** people in front of a real donation
trade-off modelled on the trolley problem. In Study 1
(**N = 800**), **17%** delegated to an AI versus
**6.75%** to another person (*p* < .001). In a
representative German and U.S. sample (Study 2), the
gap was **27.8%** versus **18.32%** (*p* < .001). When
the donation would actually be paid, people delegated
*more* to AI (**29.74%** vs **25.92%** hypothetical);
they delegated *less* to a human. Delegation cut felt
responsibility by **0.76** points on a 5-point scale,
and another **0.24** when the delegate was AI. People
who handed the choice to the model also rated its moral
capability higher — **2.81** versus **2.63** in the
hypothetical AI arm, with no such lift for human
delegates. Opacity, the authors write, is moral wiggle
room [1].

Even when you keep the click, the chat still moves you.
Ethan Landes, Kathryn Francis, and Jim A. C. Everett
(*Cognition*, 2026, in press) ran three studies on
everyday dilemmas — a laptop promised to an uncle, a
birthday versus a concert. Study 1 (**N = 189**):
people were **equally persuaded** by advice labelled as
an LLM or as a human philosopher, even while rating the
human more trustworthy. Study 2 (**N = 490**): three
rounds with a “high-quality” or “low-quality” model did
not change how much the later moral advice moved them.
Study 3 (**N = 663**): a recommendation plus good
reasons was no more persuasive than a recommendation
plus filler; patently absurd reasons *backfired*. The
heuristic is “seems good enough.” That is a fine
standard for a boilerplate email. It is a poor standard
for a values call [2].

Moral judgment is not a lookup. Joshua Greene’s fMRI
work showed that personal moral dilemmas recruit
emotion-related medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate,
and amygdala networks; harder cases recruit the
cognitive-control circuitry that has to wrestle with
those feelings [4][5]. Molly Crockett showed serotonin
can shift harm aversion in both judgment and behaviour
[6]. Shannon Vallor named the long risk in 2015:
**moral deskilling** — character atrophies when the
hard calls are no longer practised [9]. Her 2024 book
*The AI Mirror* is the same warning in the age of
fluent chat: do not treat a reflection of our words as
a substitute for the burden of being a person [10].

**The single habit to start this week:** *keep the
moral vote yours.* Before you type “what should I do?”
about a fairness, loyalty, harm, or honesty question,
write one sentence of the principle you would defend to
a colleague. Then you may ask the model to name
trade-offs, argue the other side, or role-play the
person who is harmed. You still own the call. The
model may stress-test it. It does not cast the vote.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The chat bar is now a place people take “should”
questions. Not only medical triage and hiring scores —
the everyday ones Landes and colleagues used: keep a
promise, split a gain, tell a colleague the truth.
Krügel, Ostermaier, and Uhl already showed in 2023 that
ChatGPT’s *inconsistent* moral advice still moved
people’s sacrificial judgments; a 2026 follow-up found
that adding a justification did not change how much
the advice landed [12][13]. Dillion, Mondal, Tandon,
and Gray reported in *Scientific Reports* (2025) that
people can rate a language model as rivalling an expert
ethicist in *perceived* moral expertise [14]. Fluency
is being mistaken for standing.

Issue 015 already covered the Shape layer of practical
choice: people keep the click and hand the model the
valuation [16]. Do not reread that brief as this one.
A fund that strictly dominates another has a correct
answer. A values call does not. The cost of
outsourcing it is not a worse index-fund pick. It is
a thinner character, a responsibility gap, and a
habit of treating “the model said so” as a reason.
Hüholt and Szech’s most uncomfortable finding is that
this happens *more* when the stakes are real, and that
people then *upgrade* their belief that the AI is
qualified — Dana, Weber, and Kuang’s **moral wiggle
room**, now with a chatbot [1][15].

“Human in the loop” is the regulatory sentence. It
only works if the human wants the loop. If the loop is
a way to feel less responsible, oversight is theatre
[1]. The EU’s GDPR already assumes a person who will
engage. This week’s evidence says that assumption is
the fragile part.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Joshua Greene, R. Brian Sommerville, Leigh Nystrom,
  John Darley, and Jonathan Cohen** (*Science*, 2001)
  put trolley-style dilemmas in the scanner. Personal
  harm cases preferentially engaged medial prefrontal
  cortex, posterior cingulate, and amygdala —
  emotion-linked regions — relative to impersonal
  cases. The 2004 *Neuron* follow-up found that
  difficult personal dilemmas recruited dorsolateral
  prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate, the
  control network that has to compete with a prepotent
  emotional response [4][5]. The point for this brief
  is not “trolleys.” It is that a values call is
  *work* in the same networks that handle conflict,
  harm, and social feeling. Pasting a fluent
  recommendation skips that work.
- **Molly Crockett, Luke Clark, Marc Hauser, and
  Trevor Robbins** (*PNAS*, 2010) showed the chemistry
  is not a slogan either. Enhancing serotonin with
  citalopram made healthy volunteers more likely to
  judge emotionally salient harms as forbidden, and
  less likely to reject unfair ultimatum offers — a
  harm-averse, prosocial shift that was stronger in
  high-empathy people [6]. Moral judgment is embodied.
  A text generator has none of that machinery.
- **Heather Gray, Kurt Gray, and Daniel Wegner**
  (*Science*, 2007) split mind perception into
  **agency** (thinking, planning) and **experience**
  (feeling, empathy). Bigman and Gray later showed
  that the aversion to machine moral decision-makers
  is mediated by the sense that machines lack a full
  mind [7][8]. Hüholt and Szech found that when people
  actually *delegate*, ratings of AI *experience*
  rise in the real-consequence condition. That is
  not a better theory of mind. It is a convenient
  one [1].

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Hüholt and Szech** (*European Economic Review*,
  vol. 184, April 2026; available 8 January 2026) is
  this week’s field experiment. Real donation to
  Against Malaria Foundation versus Helen Keller
  International; 1 in 10 choices implemented; no
  transparency about the AI; a deep net trained on
  non-delegators’ own choices. Study 1, technical-
  university sample, **N = 800**: **17%** vs
  **6.75%**. Study 2, representative DE/US,
  **N ≈ 4,839**: **27.8%** vs **18.32%**. Framing
  (gain vs loss) did not matter. Difficulty did:
  Study 2 delegation rose from **16.8%** (“very
  easy”) to **41.0%** (“very difficult”). Open text:
  **44.12%** of AI-delegators said the AI would make
  a “better decision,” versus **3.70%** for human
  delegates. **Peer-reviewed.** Dedicated to Nora
  Szech. The paper is explicit that the AI–human gap
  is a *lower bound* given how little they told
  people about the model [1].
- **Landes, Francis, and Everett** (*Cognition*,
  2026, in press; University of Kent / University of
  Oxford). Three Prolific UK studies. The persuasion
  is not blind: absurd reasons reverse it. It is also
  not *understanding*: good reasons add nothing over
  a bare recommendation. Trustworthiness ratings and
  attitude change come apart. That is the “seems good
  enough” heuristic, named as such [2]. Everyday
  dilemmas, not trolleys. That is the use case of
  the chat bar.
- **Jason Dana, Roberto Weber, and Jason Xi Kuang**
  (*Economic Theory*, 2007) remain the mechanism
  Hüholt cites: people exploit ambiguity to behave
  self-servingly while keeping a fair self-image
  [15]. Mary Steffel, Elanor Williams, and Jaclyn
  Perrmann-Graham (*OBHDP*, 2016) showed people
  delegate to avoid responsibility and blame. AI
  is a more convenient scapegoat than a colleague,
  because you can tell yourself it is *better*, not
  that you ducked [1].

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Krügel, Ostermaier, and Uhl** (*Scientific
  Reports*, 2023; *AI and Ethics*, 2026). The 2023
  paper: ChatGPT’s moral advice was internally
  inconsistent, and that inconsistency still
  influenced users. The 2026 paper: a screenshot of
  a simple recommendation versus a short
  justification — justification optional. Independent
  corroboration of Landes Study 3 [12][13].
- **Danica Dillion, Debajyoti Mondal, Niket Tandon,
  and Kurt Gray** (*Scientific Reports*, 2025).
  People can perceive an LLM as rivalling an expert
  ethicist. Perceived expertise is not moral
  standing. Combined with Hüholt’s belief-adaptation
  result, it is a machine for licensing the
  hand-off [14].
- **Ethan Mollick’s** working rule still applies, and
  it is not a trial: use the model as a critic, not
  as a priest. Ask it what you might be missing;
  do not ask it what is right. **Cal Newport** is
  not this week’s theme (digital minimalism remains
  unused as a sole issue), but the notebook habit
  is the right physical twin: write the principle
  on paper before the chat can finish your sentence.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Shannon Vallor** (*Philosophy & Technology*,
  2015; *The AI Mirror*, Oxford, 2024). Moral
  deskilling is the academic name for what happens
  when the hard calls are automated: practical
  wisdom is a skill, and skills atrophy without
  exercise. Upskilling is possible — Socratic tools
  that force reasons, not oracles that issue
  verdicts — but market incentives run the other
  way [9][10].
- **Ethan Landes, Cristina Voinea, and Radu Uszkai**
  (*AI & SOCIETY*, 2025). Design rule for an
  artificial moral advisor that *enhances* rather
  than replaces: it must show why, and the user
  must do the understanding. Advice without reasons
  is deskilling even when the advice is “correct.”
  Correct action without moral motivation is not
  character [11].
- **Do not treat “human in the loop” as solved.**
  Hüholt and Szech are blunt: if people use the loop
  to *shift* responsibility, GDPR-style oversight
  fails in the same places it is most needed —
  hiring, healthcare, courts [1]. The organisational
  sentence is: the person who would have to explain
  the call to the person who is harmed still makes
  it. The model may list options. It does not sign.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Keep the moral vote yours.** Once this week, when
  you are about to ask a model what you *should* do
  (a hiring shortlist, a layoff note, a customer
  exception, a conflict of interest, a promise to a
  colleague), write one sentence of the principle
  you would defend in the room. Then prompt. You
  still decide [1][2].
- **Ask for the other side, not the verdict.** “What
  would the person who is harmed say?” and “What am
  I rationalising?” are legitimate uses. “Tell me
  the right answer” is the hand-off Hüholt measured.
- **Name who is accountable in the prompt.** If the
  output will go out under your name, say so in the
  chat: “I am the one who will explain this.” Named
  accountability is a cognitive forcing function
  (Issue 007); here it is also a moral one.
- **Do not let fluency upgrade the model’s standing.**
  Dillion et al. show perceived expertise can track
  a language model. Hüholt shows capability beliefs
  rise *after* people want to duck. Treat both as
  warning lights, not as credentials [1][14].
- **Issue 015 still applies to the practical call.**
  Write the decision sentence first when there is a
  correct-ish answer (budget, risk, constraint).
  This week is the leftover: when there is no
  correct-ish answer, only a values one [16].
- **If the decision is hard, that is a reason to keep
  it.** Delegation in Hüholt rose with rated
  difficulty. Difficulty is the training signal.
  Offload the formatting. Keep the fairness.

### In personal life

- **Do not ask the chat whether to keep a promise.**
  Birthday versus concert, honesty versus comfort,
  money versus a relative: write the principle, then
  talk to a person if you need a second view. Issue
  004 was the emotional confidant. This week is the
  *ought*.
- **Refuse “seems good enough” on values.** Landes
  Study 3: a recommendation without reasons moved
  people as much as a dressed-up one. If you cannot
  restate *why* in your own words, you do not yet
  have a judgment. You have a paste.
- **Keep one weekly decision that costs you something
  and is not optimised.** A donation, an apology, a
  no. Character is practice. Vallor’s deskilling
  argument is about the absence of that practice
  [9].
- **When you do use a model on a values question,
  demand reasons you could reject.** If the reasons
  are absurd, Landes found people move *away*. That
  sensitivity is worth keeping. If the reasons are
  smooth, assume they are filler until you have
  tested them against someone who lives with the
  consequence.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** “People don’t want AI making moral decisions,
so everyday chat is safe. I’ll just get a second
opinion.”

**Reality:** Stated aversion and behaviour split.
Bigman and Gray documented the aversion; Hüholt and
Szech showed that once *you* hold a real moral choice,
delegation to AI exceeds delegation to a person, and
rises with real stakes [8][1]. Landes, Francis, and
Everett showed that a fluent recommendation moves
everyday judgments even when you rate the model less
trustworthy than a human, and even when it offers
no extra reasons [2]. A “second opinion” that you
cannot disagree with in your own words is not an
opinion. It is a vote you did not cast. The habit is
not abstinence. It is refusing to let the model
replace the principle — and using it, if at all, to
make that principle sharper.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Hüholt and Szech is a donation trade-off, not
  your workplace.** The AI was a custom net trained
  on other participants, not ChatGPT. The human
  delegate was a random other participant, not an
  identified colleague or ethicist. The authors call
  the AI–human gap a lower bound. It is still not a
  field RCT of copilots in hiring or medicine. Do
  not claim your team has a 10-point “AI morality
  tax.”
- **Responsibility and capability results are
  associations plus a between-subject stake
  manipulation.** The stake × delegate interaction
  is causal for *delegation rates*. The belief
  upgrade among AI-delegators is not a lottery over
  who already trusted AI. The paper is honest about
  that. So are we.
- **Landes et al. is in press, UK Prolific, everyday
  dilemmas.** Persuasion was modest, not overriding.
  Study 1 was not pre-registered. The bad-reasons
  backfire in Study 3 was not a pre-registered
  prediction. Effects may be short-lived. This is
  not evidence that ChatGPT rewires your ethics
  overnight.
- **Greene and Crockett are not about AI.** They
  justify treating moral judgment as a practiced,
  embodied capacity worth protecting. They do not
  measure a chatbot. Do not claim prompting causes
  a lesion in vmPFC.
- **Vallor is philosophy, not a trial.** Deskilling
  is a coherent risk with a 2015 paper and a 2024
  book behind it. It is not a measured skill curve.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing
  here is a drop in IQ, a moral injury, or a
  diagnosis. The evidence is about *patterns of
  use*: whether you still cast the values vote, and
  whether “the model said so” has become a reason
  you would not accept from a stranger.
