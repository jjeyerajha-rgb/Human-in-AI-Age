## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 006 asked you to *generate before you
store* — to produce the content yourself before
the model files it. Issue 017 asked you to *write
until you surprise yourself* — to keep the
origination of the idea. This week is neither
of those. It is about the **channel**. Prompting
skips the hand. Typing still uses the hand, but
the motor plan for a *T* and a *G* is almost
the same keystroke. Handwriting is different:
every letter is a unique sequence of strokes,
with visual and proprioceptive feedback on every
millimetre. That is **analog encoding**. The
brain network that writes is not a luxury
interface on top of thinking. It is one of the
ways thinking gets into the head.

Two 2026 papers make the network concrete. In
April, Bookyung Shin, Sungeun Lee, and colleagues
at Seoul National University, Northwestern, and
Oxford recorded EEG while **33** undergraduates
took notes from video lectures with an ink pen,
a digital pen, or a keyboard. Both pens produced
higher alpha, beta, and gamma power — and
**lower theta/beta ratios** (a marker of
sustained attention) — than the keyboard,
especially over occipital cortex. Immediate
recognition and comprehension scores did **not**
differ. The medium changed the *process* more
clearly than the *test* [1]. The same month,
Lauriane Véron-Delor, Marieke Longcamp, Jérémy
Danna, and colleagues at CNRS published an fMRI
study of adults writing in the scanner. Spatial
control of the pen recruited the
**cortico-cerebellar** loop; the sequence of
strokes recruited **both** cortico-striatal and
cortico-cerebellar loops. Handwriting is not one
blob of “motor cortex.” It is two coordinated
skill systems [2].

The honest limit is as important as the finding.
The most-shared handwriting EEG paper of the
decade — Van der Weel and Van der Meer, 36
students, 256-channel array — found richer
theta/alpha connectivity for a digital pen than
for typing. It did **not** include a learning
test, and typing was one finger with no visual
feedback. Longcamp, who has spent twenty years
mapping this network, said so in print [3][4].
The case for a pen is not “keyboards damage the
brain.” It is that the unique motor trace of
forming a letter is a cheap, underused way to
keep encoding alive in a year when the default
is to *ask*.

**The single habit to start this week:** *ink
the skeleton.* Before you open the model, write
five to ten lines by hand — ink or stylus — of
the question, the claim, or the outline. Then
let the model expand, argue, or check. After,
add one line you can still say with the chat
closed. The encoding is yours. The archive can
be the machine’s.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The missing step is no longer typing. It is
**prompting**. A decade ago the worry was that
laptops would turn lecture notes into a
stenograph. Mueller and Oppenheimer’s 2014
“pen is mightier” paper made that worry famous;
direct replications did **not** settle it [5][6].
In 2026 the default is more extreme: many people
never write the notes at all. The model
summarises the meeting, outlines the memo, and
returns a page that looks like thinking. Issue
006 already covered what happens when you let
the model *hold* the memory. This week is what
happens when you let it *skip the hand*.

Cal Newport, writing on 10 November 2025, put
the practitioner version in one paragraph. The
problem is not that we lack powerful tools. It
is that we are already so surrounded by them
that there is no time “to really open the
throttle on our brains.” His prescription was
not a new agent. It was a 6×9 stenographer’s
notebook and a hard problem [7]. That is not
nostalgia. It is a bet that the slow extraction
of understanding still needs a surface the
model does not fill for you.

This is not Issue 006 again (generation versus
offloading of *content*). It is not Issue 017
(writing as the origination of an idea). It is
not Issue 009 (felt ownership of output) or
Issue 021 (whether the model owns the calendar).
Those briefs asked *what* you produce and
*when* you open the tool. This one asks *with
which muscles*.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Marieke Longcamp (CNRS / Aix-Marseille)**
  is the academic through-line. In 2008 she
  showed that adults who *wrote* novel graphic
  shapes by hand later recognised them better
  than adults who typed the same shapes —
  and that handwriting, not typing, recruited
  a motor-perceptual network that stayed
  active during later visual recognition
  (*Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*) [8].
  Eighteen years later she is still mapping
  that network. The 2026 *Cortex* paper with
  Véron-Delor and Danna splits handwriting
  into two jobs: **sequence** (stroke after
  stroke, cortico-striatal *and*
  cortico-cerebellar) and **spatial
  adaptation** (keep the pen on the line,
  cortico-cerebellar). The associated
  Aix-Marseille protocol scanned **25**
  right-handed young adults (mean age about
  25) [2]. A prompt does not run those loops.
  A keystroke runs a much smaller one.
- **Shin, Sung, Kiaer, Cho, and Lee**
  (*Frontiers in Education*, 17 April 2026).
  Within-subject EEG, **N = 33** Korean
  undergraduates (36 recruited; three
  excluded for artefact), 32-channel array,
  10-minute video lectures of pseudowords.
  Ink pen, digital pen, and keyboard, crossed
  with verbatim versus question-generating
  notes. After FDR correction, keyboard use
  produced **higher theta/beta ratios** at
  26 electrodes (keyboard means about
  **3.44–4.73** versus ink **2.76–3.98** and
  digital pen **2.65–4.00**) — the direction
  that usually means *less* sustained
  attention. Alpha, beta, and gamma were
  higher for both pens than for the keyboard,
  strongest at occipital sites. Immediate
  word-recognition and sentence-comprehension
  tests: **no significant differences**. The
  authors’ own sentence is the right one:
  devices change the learning *process*;
  they did not, in this session, change the
  score [1].
- **F. R. (Ruud) Van der Weel and Audrey
  van der Meer** (NTNU; *Frontiers in
  Psychology*, January 2024). **N = 36**
  university students, 256-channel EEG,
  cursive on a digital pen versus typing the
  same visually presented words. Handwriting
  showed more widespread theta/alpha
  coherence between parietal and central
  hubs — **16** significant connections
  from **32** clusters. They inferred a
  learning benefit from the frequencies and
  regions involved in memory encoding [3].
  **Svetlana Pinet and Marieke Longcamp**
  (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 8 January 2025)
  then wrote the necessary commentary: there
  was **no learning task** (students copied
  known words); statistics compared the
  *difference* between conditions, not each
  condition on its own; typing was
  **one index finger** with **no visual
  feedback**. Those choices can suppress
  typing-related connectivity by themselves
  [4]. Cite Van der Weel for a connectivity
  difference. Do not cite it as proof that
  pens teach and keyboards do not.
- **Robert Wiley and Brenda Rapp** (Johns
  Hopkins; *Psychological Science*, 2021).
  **N = 42** adults with no Arabic learned
  20 letters by writing, typing, or looking.
  Everyone got to recognition eventually.
  The writing group got there in fewer
  sessions (**M = 3.67**, SE = 0.36) than
  typing (**3.92**) or visual study
  (**4.25**), improved faster on letter
  *naming*, and — the distinctive claim —
  acquired **both motor and amodal symbolic**
  representations. Typing did not [9]. This
  is letter learning, not memo drafting.
  It is still the cleanest behavioural
  evidence that the hand’s unique path, not
  “motor output” in general, is doing
  encoding work.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer**
  (*Psychological Science*, 2014) is the
  paper everyone remembers. Laptop notes
  were longer and more verbatim; longhand
  notes predicted better conceptual answers
  in some studies [5]. **Kayla Morehead,
  John Dunlosky, and Katherine Rawson**
  (2019) could not replicate the performance
  advantage. **Heather Urry and colleagues**
  (*Psychological Science*, 2021) ran a
  direct replication of Study 1 (**n = 74**
  laptop, **68** longhand): more words and
  more verbatim overlap on the laptop, **no
  quiz advantage** for longhand, and a mini
  meta-analysis of eight similar studies
  saying the same. Higher word count tracked
  better scores; verbatim overlap’s harm was
  not robust [6]. The 2014 mechanism that
  *did* survive is not “paper magic.” It is
  **generative paraphrase**. A slow hand
  forces selection. A fast keyboard, and
  now a faster model, make it easy to copy.
- **Michelene Chi and Ruth Wylie’s ICAP
  framework** (2014) names the same split
  without a pen: *active* (select, copy)
  versus *constructive* (generate a question,
  a summary, a claim in your own words) [10].
  Shin et al. crossed device with that
  split. The EEG interaction at occipital
  beta and gamma was real; the test scores
  still did not move. Strategy and medium
  both matter. Neither is a spell.
- **Daniel Reisberg’s “detachment gain,”**
  as Annie Murphy Paul uses it in *The
  Extended Mind* (2021): moving a thought
  onto a page lets you inspect it with the
  senses instead of holding it in working
  memory. Seventh- and eighth-graders who
  *drew* a bicycle pump or a chemical bond
  understood the system better with no
  further instruction — because the drawing
  showed them what they did not yet know
  [11]. A chatbot page that you did not
  make cannot surprise you that way. The
  surprise is the point.

  hands, sketchbooks, and rooms are part of

  exists.
- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton).** In December

  showing Gemini-class models transcribing
  handwriting at or above human level [12].
  That is the practical hinge. Analog notes
  used to die in a drawer because search
  lived on the laptop. The encoding argument
  no longer has to fight the filing
  argument. Write by hand for the brain;
  photograph or transcribe later if you
  need a searchable archive. Do not reverse
  the order.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Brenda Rapp (Johns Hopkins)** has spent
  a career on the writing brain, including
  patients who can write digits but not
  letters. The 2021 training study with
  Wiley is the educational translation:
  handwriting is not old-fashioned motor
  drill. It builds a symbolic
  representation that typing does not [9].
  For adults at work the analog is not
  “learn Arabic.” It is: the first time a
  concept has to live in *your* system,
  give it a stroke path, not only a prompt.
- **Shin and Lee’s 2026 conclusion** is
  the policy-sized one that survives the
  null on immediate tests: if the goal is
  *attention during the hour*, pens (ink
  or stylus) still look different from
  keyboards in the EEG. If the goal is
  *tonight’s quiz*, the device may not
  matter. Design for the process you want,
  not for a score the session cannot see
  [1].
- **Pinet and Longcamp’s 2025 commentary**
  is the academic hygiene this series
  exists to practice. A viral EEG figure
  is not a curriculum. Classroom claims
  need a learning measure, a realistic
  typing condition, and children if the
  advice is for children [4]. We will
  not launder Van der Weel into a school
  mandate. We will keep the narrower
  adult habit: when the work has to be
  *yours*, put a pen on a skeleton of it
  before the model writes the rest.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Ink the skeleton.** Before the first
  prompt on a piece of work that has to
  stay *yours* — a decision, a design, a
  client argument — write five to ten
  lines by hand. Question, claim, or
  outline. Then open the model. Shin’s
  EEG says the pen changes attention
  during the hour even when the quiz does
  not [1]. Newport’s grove says the page
  is where the idea gets past working
  memory [7].
- **Stylus counts.** Shin found digital
  pens at least as “live” as ink in the
  bands they measured, sometimes more so
  [1]. If the workplace forbids paper,
  a tablet stylus is the analog, not a
  blank prompt box.
- **Paraphrase, don’t stenograph.** The
  part of Mueller that survived Urry is
  not paper. It is *not copying* [5][6].
  If you must type, type a question or a
  claim, not the model’s sentences.
- **Photograph later, encode now.**
  Mollick’s 2025 observation removes the
  last honest excuse that handwritten
  notes are unsearchable [12]. Snap the
  page after the meeting. Do not start
  in the chat because filing is easier.
- **One analog pass on load-bearing
  work.** Issue 007 still owns checking
  the load-bearing *number*. This week
  owns the load-bearing *shape*: the
  outline of the argument, in your hand,
  before the model writes the paragraphs.

### In personal life

- **Keep a pocket notebook that is not
  a second phone.** Newport’s 6×9 steno
  pad is the object. Use it for the
  problem you would otherwise dump into
  a chat on a walk [7].
- **Draw the thing you think you
  understand.** Paul’s bicycle-pump
  study is the personal-life version:
  a sketch reveals the gap a fluent
  explanation hides [11]. Then, if you
  want, ask the model to quiz the
  drawing — not to replace it.
- **Write the hard message by hand
  first.** Condolence, apology, a
  request with stakes. Ten lines in ink.
  Then type, or ask the model to tone-
  check. The feeling of the sentence
  should exist before the polish.
- **Do not confuse a beautiful digital
  notes app with encoding.** A perfectly
  tagged vault of AI summaries is Issue
  006’s external brain. This week’s
  test is simpler: can you still say
  the skeleton with the glass dark?

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** Science has proved that writing
by hand is always better than typing, and
that AI will atrophy the writing brain.

**Reality:** The best 2026 EEG study found
a *process* difference (attention-related
bands) and **no** immediate performance
difference [1]. The most famous 2014
laptop study did **not** replicate on the
quiz [6]. The viral 2024 connectivity
paper compared handwriting to an unnatural
typing condition and never tested learning
[3][4]. Wiley and Rapp *did* show that
the unique motor path of handwriting
builds representations typing does not —
in a letter-learning task, in adults, with
a small N [9]. None of this is brain
damage. None of it says you should abandon
keyboards or models. It says the hand is
an encoding device you currently skip for
free, and that skipping it is a *use
pattern*, not a neurological injury.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Shin et al. is N = 33**, one session
  per device, immediate tests only,
  pseudoword lectures, Korean
  undergraduates. EEG differences can
  come from motor artefact as well as
  attention; the authors discuss this.
  Do not quote occipital beta as a grade
  improvement. There wasn’t one.
- **Véron-Delor et al. maps the writing
  network. It is not an AI study and not
  a learning study.** N for the
  associated protocol is 25. It tells
  you *what handwriting recruits*, not
  that prompting shrinks those regions.
- **Van der Weel is real EEG and a weak
  educational inference.** Pinet and
  Longcamp’s commentary is the peer
  record, not a nitpick. We will not
  outrun it.
- **Wiley and Rapp is letter learning
  in 42 adults.** Generalising from
  Arabic graphemes to your quarterly
  strategy memo is a leap. The leap we
  are willing to take is narrow: unique
  motor traces help encoding of *new
  symbols*. Treat a new argument as a
  new symbol.
- **Mueller 2014 should no longer be
  cited as settled.** Urry 2021 is the
  replication. The surviving advice is
  generative notes, not a laptop ban.
- **Newport and Mollick are practice,
  not trials.** Cite them as experienced
  operators, not as N.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.**
  Nothing here is a drop in IQ, a
  lesion, or a reason to panic. The
  evidence is about *patterns of use*:
  whether the first trace of an idea
  is a stroke, a key, or a prompt.
