## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 004 asked you to *route it to a human* when the chatbot became a
confidant. That brief was about emotional life. This week is the other
conversation that keeps a person sharp: the **work conversation** — the
hallway ask, the advice tie, the two-minute “how do we do this here?”
that also happens to be how organisations actually think.

Those conversations are not decoration around the real job. They *are*
a cognitive job. Occupational social interaction across midlife is
associated with lower later-life dementia and mild cognitive impairment
in a Norwegian registry study of **9,248** people [3]. Complex work
*with people* tracks episodic memory and a neuroimaging marker of brain
reserve [4]. And a 2026 field experiment shows the substitution fear is
not automatic. Ralf Büchsenschuss, Irmela Koch-Bayram, Torsten Biemann,
and Phanish Puranam randomly assigned **42** teams (**316** employees
with pre- and post-surveys) at a Central European technology-services
firm to a **grounded** GenAI assistant — RAG on the firm’s CRM,
documents, mail, and meeting recordings — or to business as usual.
After three months, treated employees gained **+7.77** collaboration
degree-centrality versus **+1.12** in control, and **+5.21** knowledge
degree-centrality versus **+0.84** (both *p* < .001). Specialists became
more sought-after as knowledge sources (in-degree **+6.26** vs **+2.28**
for generalists). Generalists translated the tool into more projects
(absolute gain larger; interaction *b* = 2.48, *p* < .01). Grounded AI,
in that shop, made people *more* useful to one another [1].

The other pattern is just as real, and it is the one most of us live
in. Workday’s Human Connection Workplace Index (Hanover Research,
March–April 2026; **2,150** AI-using employees at firms of 3,500+)
found **33%** rarely or never have a non-transactional colleague
conversation in a given week. In the last year, **76%** had used AI for
advice, **52%** to brainstorm, **37%** for companionship. Reduced
human-to-human interaction was the top AI worry (**43%**), ahead of job
loss. The survey is vendor-commissioned and correlational. It is still
the largest current snapshot of the *connection deficit* at work [2].
Microsoft telemetry points the same way: among Copilot users with
**≥100** uses over 20 weeks, productivity-app actions rose **21.2%**
while communication-app actions rose only **7.1%**; small-group emails
fell **4.7%**, unique recipients **1.6%**, conversation rounds **2.1%**
[6]. Generic copilots can answer the question that used to build the
tie.

**The single habit to start this week:** *walk one question to a
person.* When the model answers something you would have asked a
colleague — a process, a judgement, a “how do we do this here” — take
that same question, or the model’s draft of it, to a human this week.
Grounded tools can *increase* the network when they make experts
findable. The hallway ask is how you stay in that network.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Inbox copilots have made it cheaper to resolve a work question without
disturbing anyone. That is a gift for the status update nobody needed
and a tax on the conversations that carry tacit knowledge: who actually
decides, what failed last time, which client is lying. Mark Granovetter
showed in 1973 that **weak ties** — acquaintances, not close friends —
are the bridges that move new information [10]. Ronald Burt later named
the structural holes those bridges span [11]. A model that answers
*your* question from *your* corpus does not walk you across the hole.
It fills it with a fluent paragraph.

The INSEAD–Mannheim RCT is the honest counter-example, and it should
stay in the room. The tool was not a consumer chat. It was grounded in
the firm, used in live customer work, and it *raised* both inbound and
outbound human ties. Interviews (seven generalists, six specialists)
sketched the mechanism: generalists used it to kill easy retrieval so
they could involve people on *harder* projects; specialists used it to
prepare better answers, which made people come back [1]. Yu, Chen, Hu,
Suri, and Counts, writing from Microsoft Research and Arizona, see the
other mechanism in Copilot traces: expanded individual bandwidth,
compressed coordination, and suggested replies that reduce reading.
They warn that a relative shift toward documentation can thin the
diffusion of diverse information [6]. Both can be true. The variable is
whether the tool *substitutes* a person or *surfaces* one.

Issue 004 remains the emotional brief. Do not collapse this week into
loneliness, therapy, or sycophancy. The object here is **workplace
social capital**: who you still ask, who still asks you, and whether
the time AI saves is reinvested in a person or in another solo
document.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Matthew Lieberman** (UCLA) is the social-brain warrant, not an
  AI trial. In *Social* (2013) and earlier neuroleadership work with
  Naomi Eisenberger, he argued that the need to connect is a primary
  drive, and that workplace respect and belonging activate reward
  circuitry in ways organisations treat as optional [12][13]. The
  useful claim for this brief is modest: conversation is not a
  soft extra around cognition. It is one of the loads the brain is
  built to carry.
- **Bernt Bratsberg, Jennifer Harris, Vegard Skirbekk, Yaakov Stern,
  and colleagues** (*Psychology and Aging*, 2025; HUNT4 70+) linked
  registry employment from ages 30–65 to cognitive assessment at or
  after 70 in **9,248** Norwegians. Higher occupational social
  interaction (O*NET-derived) was associated with lower dementia
  (RRR **0.89** per SD, *p* = 0.003), lower MCI (RRR **0.88**,
  *p* < .001), and a **0.31**-point higher MoCA, after age, sex,
  education, and APOE-ε4 [3]. Observational. Not AI-specific. It
  tells you *which human practice* is at risk if the hallway ask
  dies — not that Copilot causes dementia.
- **Max Coleman, Meghan Roessler, and colleagues** (*Alzheimer’s &
  Dementia*, 2023) add the brain-reserve piece. A standard-deviation
  increase in occupational complexity *with people* was associated
  with a **0.14–0.19** SD rise in episodic memory, a **9–12%**
  reduction in the probability of MCI or dementia, and a
  **0.18–0.25** SD increase in a neuroimaging reserve gap — cognition
  holding up relative to hippocampal atrophy — after education [4].
  Again: midlife social complexity, not a chatbot RCT.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Christopher Dishop, Allen Brown, Ping-Ya Chao, Andrew Kuznetsov,
  and Anita Williams Woolley** (*Journal of Business and Psychology*,
  31 October 2025) isolate the relational cost of putting a model
  *between* two people. In a behavioural care-team simulation
  (Study 1, N = **371**), a teammate who disclosed ChatGPT while
  helping was rated less warm and less competent; warmth mediated
  lower felt obligation and less reciprocated help. A vignette
  replication (Study 2, N = **238**) held after controls for
  technology self-efficacy, readiness, and perceived usefulness. A
  student replication (N = **246**) had a randomisation imbalance;
  treat it as support, not the lead [5]. The finding is not “never
  use AI to help a colleague.” It is that disclosed, lightweight
  help spends less social currency than help that cost the helper
  something.
- **Susan Fiske’s warmth–competence map** is the mechanism Dishop
  uses: observers infer motive from effort [5]. A model-drafted
  “happy to help” note can look efficient and cold at once.
- **Granovetter (1973) and Burt (1992 / 2004)** remain the network
  grammar. Weak ties carry novelty; structural holes are where
  brokers sit [10][11]. A private assistant that answers from *your*
  files does not put you in a hole. A grounded directory that names
  the specialist might. That is the INSEAD pattern in older
  language.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Phanish Puranam and the Mannheim team** give the causal good
  news, with the caveats they wrote themselves. Team-level
  randomisation, three months, self-reported networks (in-degree
  from *others* is the more objective slice), one Central European
  tech-services firm, GPT-4 / GPT-4o RAG. Role contrasts
  (specialist vs generalist) are **not** causal — roles were not
  randomised, common support was thin. Hypothesis 2a (specialists
  gain more collaboration centrality) failed. The headline that
  survives: grounded GenAI increased human-human density rather
  than replacing it [1].
- **Yulin Yu, Yan Chen, Rui Hu, Siddharth Suri, and Scott Counts**
  (Microsoft / Microsoft Research / University of Arizona;
  arXiv:2608.15550, 24 August 2026; **preprint**) give the telemetry
  caution. Difference-in-differences on M365 traces after Copilot
  enablement: large relative shift toward Word / Excel / PowerPoint;
  mixed communication (more folder-switching, **−5.5%** reading,
  **−7%** mail-moving, fewer small-group threads). They cannot see
  prompt content or true time-on-task. Selection into early
  adoption remains a threat they mitigate with later-adopter
  matching, not a lottery [6].
- **Rudrajit Choudhuri, Christian Bird, Alice Ferng, and colleagues**
  (Oregon State / Microsoft; arXiv:2609.13786, September 2026;
  **preprint**) interviewed **22** knowledge workers and surveyed
  **1,534** (732 rating a human co-worker, 802 rating an AI). The
  BACI framework — Benevolence, Ability, Cooperativeness, Integrity
  — is useful here for one reason: workers disagreed over whether
  AI should have warmth, take initiative, or own outcomes. The
  etiquette taxonomy is the practitioner move: when AI-supported
  work is handed to a colleague, the *human* still owes
  preparation, disclosure, and responsibility [8]. That is a
  conversation, not a setting.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **The Workday Foundation / Hanover Index** is not a journal
  article. Name it as a large, vendor-commissioned survey of
  AI-using employees in seven countries, fielded March–April 2026.
  Gains are real in the same instrument: **62%** reported lower
  burnout risk, **86%** felt more productive, **64%** more confident
  about future roles. The connection numbers sit beside those
  gains, not instead of them. Gen Z is the tail the press release
  emphasises (time off for loneliness **20%** in that cohort; the
  “12 times more likely than Gen X to feel completely disconnected”
  claim is theirs, not independently audited here) [2].
- **AEA RCT Registry AEARCTR-0018768** (“Generative AI and the
  Social Fabric of Organizations,” last registered 13 July 2026) is
  a *planned* branch-level RCT in a Ugandan bank — 80 branches,
  customised GenAI for loan officers, help-seeking from peers and
  supervisors as an outcome. In development. Cite it as the next
  test of the INSEAD claim in a different industry and continent,
  not as a result [9].
- **A policy implication that travels.** If an organisation claims
  AI will “free people for higher-value collaboration,” measure the
  collaboration. Track advice ties, not only tickets closed. The
  INSEAD team already treated network position as a first-class
  outcome. Most deployments still do not.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Walk one question to a person.** Once this week, take a question
  the model already answered — a process, a judgement, a local
  “how we do this” — to the colleague who would have known. Ask
  what the model missed.
- **Use grounded tools as a directory, not a substitute.** If the
  assistant can name the specialist, send the specialist the
  question. That is the INSEAD knowledge-catalyst pattern [1].
- **Reinvest the saved minute.** If Copilot cut a retrieval or a
  first draft, spend one of those minutes on a live ask, a
  walk-by, or a two-person huddle — not a second solo document
  [6][2].
- **Help like a person.** When you use a model to help a
  colleague, add one sentence only you could have written, and
  say what you checked. Disclosed, effortless help spends
  warmth [5].
- **Handoff etiquette.** If AI-supported work reaches someone
  else, say that it did, what you verified, and what you still
  own. Recipients already expect that of a great co-worker [8].
- **Protect one non-transactional conversation.** A third of
  Workday respondents no longer have one in a given week [2].
  Put a name on the calendar or walk the long way to coffee.

### In personal life

- **Ask a person the local question.** How a family actually
  decides, how a neighbour solved the same problem, how a friend
  would hear the news: those are weak ties. A model can list
  options. It cannot be your acquaintance.
- **Do not outsource the thank-you.** Condolence and gratitude
  still have to cost you something, or they read like Dishop’s
  lightweight help.
- **Keep one standing conversation that is not a prompt.** A
  weekly call, a walk, a club. Occupational social interaction
  is the midlife version of the same reserve [3][4].
- **Issue 004 still applies at night.** If the chatbot is the
  confidant, route that to a human too. This week’s habit is
  the *daytime* ask.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** “AI at work isolates people. Every prompt is a
conversation you stole from a colleague.”

**Reality:** It depends which tool, and what you do with the
time. In the INSEAD field RCT, a grounded assistant *increased*
collaboration and knowledge-sharing ties [1]. In Microsoft
telemetry, a generic Copilot shifted the mix toward solo
documents and thinner small-group mail [6]. Workday’s survey
captures the felt deficit when advice, brainstorming, and even
companionship move into the chat [2]. Isolation is a *use
pattern*, not a property of the model. The habit is not
abstinence. It is refusing to let a fluent answer close a
question that still has a person on the other end.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **The INSEAD paper is a working paper**, one firm, three
  months, self-reported networks and self-reported project
  counts. In-degree from others is stronger than
  self-nominated out-degree. Role contrasts are correlational.
  Do not treat +7.77 as a universal “AI makes you more
  social” constant. Do treat the *possibility* — grounded
  tools can raise density — as the finding that kills the
  simple scare.
- **Workday is a vendor survey**, fielded by Hanover,
  published as a press release. No peer review, no public
  microdata, AI-using employees only, large enterprises
  only. The burnout *gains* in the same instrument are as
  real as the connection worries. Do not cite it as a
  causal “AI caused loneliness” paper.
- **Yu et al. is a preprint** on digital traces, not
  conversations. Actions ≠ minutes, and they cannot see
  which communication was redundant. The small-group email
  drop is the most socially meaningful slice they have.
- **Dishop is peer-reviewed** and still a lab / vignette
  design with disclosed ChatGPT. Hidden AI use, or help
  that was clearly edited, may land differently. Warmth is
  not the same as competence at the task.
- **HUNT and Coleman are observational**, decades of
  occupation, not 2026 copilots. They justify protecting
  social complexity at work as a cognitive-reserve
  behaviour. They do not measure GPT.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing here is a
  lesion, an IQ drop, or a dementia diagnosis caused by a
  chatbot. The evidence is about *patterns of use*: which
  conversations you still have, and which you only prompt.
