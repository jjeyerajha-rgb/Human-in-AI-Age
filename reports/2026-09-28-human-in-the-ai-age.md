## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 003 asked working adults to keep their hands on the work. This week is the
same problem one generation earlier, while the cortex is still being built.
Stanford’s SCALE Initiative reviewed **more than 800** papers on AI in K-12 and
found only **20** that met a high-quality causal bar. The pattern in those twenty
is now clear enough to act on: students do better *while they have the chatbot
open*, and the gain often **fails to transfer** once the chat is closed. Tools
that give **hints and step-by-step reasoning** hold up better than general-purpose
oracles that hand over the answer [1]. Common Sense Media’s 2026 census found
that **86%** of U.S. 9- to 17-year-olds already use AI, **85%** of those users
use it for homework, and **21%** do so every day — while **44%** have never had
a parent or teacher talk to them about using it safely [2].

This is not “AI damages the developing brain.” It is a **Safety Gap**: the
distance between a polished, AI-assisted product and the unassisted reasoning a
child can still do when the screen goes dark [3]. Frontal and parietal networks
that support executive function, inhibitory control, and schema construction
are still pruning and myelinating through adolescence [4, 5]. An oracle that
removes the struggle also removes the work those networks need.

**The single habit to start this week:** *ask for a hint, not the answer.*
Before you (or a child) paste a problem into a chatbot, set the rule in
advance: the model may ask a question, give a hint, or check a step. It may
not write the finished solution. Then close the chat and do **one item
unassisted** — a problem, a paragraph, an explanation in your own words. If
you cannot, the tool was doing the learning.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Homework used to leak the struggle into the house. You sat with a worksheet
until the stuck-ness produced a schema, or a parent sat with you and *did not*
give the answer. Generative AI collapsed that cost. A Year 10 student can now
upload a photo of a problem set and receive a fluent worked example in seconds.
The mark looks like competence. The cortex has not practised the retrieval.

SCALE’s 2026 review is the honest map of how little we know, and how much is
already being decided. There are **no high-quality causal studies of student AI
use in U.S. K-12 classrooms**. Most of the 20 causal papers are short, often a
single session, often postsecondary or international, and they measure
near-term performance more than durable skill [1]. Meanwhile the household
numbers have already moved. Among kids who use AI for homework, **16%** say it
is hard to start or finish work without it; among daily homework users that
rises to **28%** [2]. Australia’s under-16 social-media minimum age took effect
on **10 December 2025** — a policy signal that childhood digital environments
are now a legislative object — while general-purpose chatbots remain a tap
away on the same phones [6].

The personal stake is not only for parents. Adults who skip the struggle are
running a milder version of the same gap. Issue 003 covered apprenticeship at
work. This week is **developmental**: the window in which the skill is supposed
to form in the first place. If the product is graded and the process is
outsourced, the Safety Gap is the grade.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain
- **Jay Giedd, Nitin Gogtay, and colleagues’** longitudinal MRI work remains
  the developmental backbone. Gray-matter volume in frontal and parietal
  cortex peaks in late childhood and is then pruned; the prefrontal networks
  that support planning, inhibition, and working memory continue to reorganise
  into the twenties [4, 5]. That is not a reason to ban tools. It is a reason
  not to let a fluent oracle *stand in* for the generative work — testing,
  erring, retrieving — through which those networks are trained. **Sisi Tao
  (Education University of Hong Kong)** maps those MRI inflection points onto
  a five-tier GenAI framework in *AI, Brain and Child* (December 2025): zero
  direct child-facing use at ages 3–5; tightly teacher-mediated minutes in
  early primary; longer, more independent, still-capped use only in
  adolescence. The cut-offs are **proposed, not validated in a trial**. Treat
  them as a developmental *stance*: younger brains get more adult mediation,
  not more chatbot [7].
- **Lorna Uden and Gwo-Jen Hwang** published the **LEARN** framework
  (Lifelong Learning, Engagement, Active Processing, Reflection, Neuro-based
  Design) in the *Journal of Computers in Education* (28 July 2026). It is
  **conceptual**, not a new scan: neuroplasticity, effortful cognition, and
  metacognitive regulation are the reason AI should be a *scaffold* in
  problem-based learning, not a substitute for the assessment product. They
  are explicit that empirical tests of LEARN are still underway [8]. The
  useful claim for a household is smaller than the acronym: deep learning
  still requires a cognitive act the model cannot perform for the learner.
- **Hong Wang and Wenhui Shan (Jilin University)** name the practical
  failure mode in a April 2026 *Frontiers in Education* perspective: the
  **Safety Gap** — the epistemic discrepancy between AI-assisted output and
  the learner’s independently reproducible reasoning. For novices, whose
  schemas are still forming, an Oracle-style model (immediate complete
  answers) is the high-risk architecture; a Socratic tutor (hints, withheld
  solutions) and an adversarial agent (critique, lures) keep germane load in
  the task [3]. This is a **perspective**, not a trial. The diagram is a
  conjecture. The design distinction is the part that already has causal
  support in SCALE’s twenty [1].

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits
- **John Sweller’s** cognitive-load theory and **Elizabeth and Robert
  Bjork’s** desirable difficulties are the learning-science frame SCALE uses
  to read the mixed transfer results. AI can cut *extraneous* load
  (searching, formatting, decoding a badly written prompt). It can also cut
  *germane* load — the productive struggle that builds the schema [1, 9].
  Students prefer the easier tool. Long-term retention prefers the harder
  one. That preference gap is not new; chatbots made it instantaneous.
- **Manu Kapur’s** productive-failure work is the classroom version of the
  same idea: wrestling with a problem *before* instruction often beats being
  shown the method first [3]. The household translation is the hint rule.
  A parent or a model that jumps to the worked example is being kind and
  skipping the encoding.
- **Jenny Radesky (University of Michigan)** and Common Sense’s Youth AI
  Safety Institute have been the paediatric voice on *how* children actually
  meet these tools. The 2026 census is a survey, not a causal study: heavier
  AI use tracks with modestly lower self-rated happiness and more loneliness,
  and kids who already struggle to focus or persist are *more* likely to use
  AI weekly for schoolwork [2]. Direction is unknown. The design implication
  is not: the children most tempted to offload the hard bit are the ones who
  most need the struggle. Common Sense’s ChatGPT risk assessment (October
  2025) is blunt on a related, already-covered terrain from Issue 004: teens
  should not use chatbots as therapists. Study Mode and hint-first tools are
  the homework analogue of that boundary [10].

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers
- **Lily Fesler, J.P. Martinez, Chris Agnew, and Susanna Loeb (Stanford
  SCALE / AI Hub for Education)** did the field a favour by refusing to
  over-claim. Of 818 repository papers as of October 2025, 20 survived a
  What Works Clearinghouse-style causal screen. Headline: performance *with
  access* is real (math practice, programming, writing); independent
  assessment is mixed; pedagogical guardrails beat oracles; teacher-facing
  tools can save ~**25 minutes/week** of prep (Roy et al., NFER 2024) without
  a detectable drop in blind-rated lesson quality [1, 11]. They are equally
  clear about the holes: no U.S. K-12 student causal studies, almost no
  evidence on equity, wellness, or social development, and almost no
  longitudinal follow-up.
- **Rose Wang, Ana Ribeiro, Carly Robinson, Susanna Loeb, and Dora Demszky
  (Stanford)** ran **Tutor CoPilot**, a live-tutoring RCT in which AI
  advised *tutors*, not students. SCALE reports **900 tutors** and a **4
  percentage-point** lift in topic mastery, **7 pp** for less-experienced
  tutors and **9 pp** for lower-rated ones; tutors with the copilot asked
  more guiding questions and gave away fewer answers [1, 12]. The November
  2025 EdWorkingPapers version reports a somewhat smaller analysed sample
  (>700 tutors, >1,000 students). Either way, this is the *constructive*
  finding of the week: the valuable AI in a child’s learning life may sit
  on the *adult’s* shoulder, prompting the human to stay Socratic.
- **Matthias Stadler (LMU Munich)** and colleagues’ 2024 *Computers in
  Human Behavior* experiment is the adult-student warning that still
  generalises: twenty minutes with a general-purpose LLM to research a
  scientific issue produced **lower mental effort** and **weaker reasoning**
  than the same time with a search engine [13]. Ease is not a learning
  outcome. It is often the opposite signal.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy
- **Sisi Tao, Min Lan, Minjuan Wang, and Hui Li** scoped **22 empirical
  K-12 studies** in *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*
  (online Feb 2026; issue Jun 2026) and clustered reported risks into
  psychological wellbeing, intellectual agency (dependency, distorted
  self-assessment, eroded authorship), and ecology (governance, equity,
  privacy). Their mitigations match the habit: process over product; hints
  not solutions; AI literacy in the curriculum rather than a ban that
  children will route around [14]. This is a **scoping review of a small,
  early literature**, not a meta-analysis of effects.
- **The National Sleep Foundation** 16-expert panel (Hartstein, Hale, and
  colleagues; *Sleep Health*, 2024) is the developmental sleep anchor
  Issue 011 could not use for *adults*. The panel **did** reach consensus
  that screen use in general, and the *content* of pre-sleep screen use,
  impair sleep health in **children and adolescents**. They did **not**
  reach consensus on those claims for adults, nor that pre-sleep
  screen-emitted light is the mechanism [15]. Homework-chat at 11 p.m. is
  therefore a double hit: it skips the struggle *and* it is the age group
  for which the sleep evidence is strongest.
- **Australia’s Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age)**
  required designated platforms, from 10 December 2025, to take reasonable
  steps to prevent under-16s holding accounts. By mid-2026 the government
  was proposing to double maximum penalties and the eSafety Commissioner
  was investigating multiple platforms; independent reporting also found
  widespread circumvention [6]. The signal for this brief is not “bans
  work.” It is that childhood attention is now treated as a public good,
  while homework oracles remain unregulated in the same pocket.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **If you teach, manage juniors, or write training:** grade a *closed-book
  minute*. After any AI-supported draft or problem set, require one unassisted
  explanation (oral or handwritten). That is the Safety Gap test [3].
- **Put AI on the coach, not on the novice.** Tutor CoPilot’s useful result is
  that hints to the *human teacher* raised mastery; answers to the student are
  the failure mode [12]. Use AI to generate Socratic questions you will ask,
  not solutions the learner will paste.
- **Save the teacher’s time, spend the student’s.** Roy et al. found prep-time
  savings with no drop in rated lesson quality [11]. Reinvest the 25 minutes
  in the part AI cannot do: watching someone struggle and not rescuing them
  too soon.
- **Do not confuse enjoyment with encoding.** Students like the oracle. Stadler
  and SCALE both show that lower effort often travels with weaker reasoning
  [1, 13]. If a learning tool is described only as “engaging,” ask what
  happens when it is turned off.

### In personal life
- **The hint rule, said out loud, before the prompt.** “You may ask it for a
  hint or to check a step. You may not ask it to do the problem.” Then sit
  nearby. A rule the child cannot recite is not a rule.
- **One unassisted item every session.** Close the tab. Redo one question, or
  explain the method in two sentences without looking. If they cannot, the
  previous answers were the model’s.
- **Homework chat is a bedtime problem in this age group.** The National Sleep
  Foundation reached consensus on child and adolescent screens, especially
  stimulating pre-sleep *content* [15]. Park the chatbot with the other
  interactive screens 30–60 minutes before sleep (the Issue 011 habit, now
  with a developmental evidence base).
- **Have the safety conversation that 44% of kids have not had [2].** Not a
  lecture: when is a hint OK, when is an answer cheating, what to do if the
  model says something that feels wrong, and that it is not a friend (Issue
  004). **51%** have never been taught at school how to tell if AI information
  is trustworthy [2]. That lesson can happen at a kitchen table.
- **Age-gate the oracle, not just the minutes.** Tao’s tiers are unvalidated
  cut-offs [7]. The direction is still right: preschoolers should not have a
  private chatbot; primary use is adult-mediated; teens can have more access
  *with* a closed-book check. Personal devices are the main on-ramp (78% of
  kid users) [2]. School policies that ignore the phone will miss the work.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** “If the homework looks better with AI, the child is learning more.”

**Reality:** SCALE’s twenty causal studies are most consistent with a
**performance–learning split**. Access often raises the score on the task the
tool can see. Independent tests are mixed: sometimes up, sometimes flat,
sometimes down [1]. Bastani’s GPT Base condition is the cartoon version of
the same split (better homework, worse closed-book exam). The Safety Gap is
that cartoon named as a developmental risk [3]. A mark is not a schema. The
test that matters is the one the model is not invited to.

A second, related myth: “We should wait for U.S. classroom RCTs before
changing anything at home.” There are none yet [1]. The learning-science
mechanism (germane load, generation, productive failure) is older than
ChatGPT. Waiting for a perfect local trial while **21%** of 9- to 17-year-old
AI users do homework with a model daily [2] is itself a decision — to let
the oracle set the default.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **SCALE is an evidence *map*, not a mega-trial.** The 20 causal papers are a
  small, selected set; many are preprints; many are short lab tasks; none are
  U.S. K-12 student RCTs. Findings that transfer (hints > answers; access ≠
  mastery) are the ones SCALE argues rest on general learning science. Findings
  about exact effect sizes in a particular app will age out when the app
  changes [1].
- **Tutor CoPilot is a preprint / working paper**, and sample-size figures
  differ slightly across versions (SCALE’s 900/1,800 vs EdWorkingPapers’
  >700/>1,000). It tests AI as a *tutor aid* in live math tutoring of
  underserved K-12 students, not ChatGPT-for-homework. The +4 pp mastery
  effect is real in that setting; it is not a warrant for unsupervised
  student chatbots [12].
- **The Common Sense census is a cross-sectional survey.** 86% “use AI”
  includes search summaries, image tools, and chatbots. Homework association
  with focus problems and lower happiness is **not** causal. Kids who are
  already struggling may reach for the tool more [2].
- **Wang & Shan, Uden & Hwang, and Tao’s five-tier paper are conceptual.**
  Safety Gap, LEARN, and the minute-caps are frameworks. Do not treat 15 or
  30 minutes as a measured neurotoxic threshold [3, 7, 8].
- **Tao et al.’s scoping review has N = 22 studies.** It maps *reported*
  risks, not pooled effect sizes [14].
- **Bastani is reused as corroboration**, not as this week’s new result. Issue
  003 already covered it. Kosmyna’s EEG writing study and Kreijkes’
  note-taking RCT were covered in the pilot and Issue 006; they support the
  same “AI-only is worse than AI-plus-generation” pattern and are not
  re-litigated here.
- **This is literacy, not paediatric, educational, or legal advice.** A child
  in distress belongs with a trusted adult and, if needed, a clinician — not
  a chatbot (Issue 004). Sleep problems belong with a clinician too. School
  policy belongs with the school.
