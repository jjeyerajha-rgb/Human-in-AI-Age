## 1. Executive Summary

For four weeks we've looked at how AI affects the individual mind — cognitive reserve,
metacognition, the productive struggle, and emotional life. This week we widen the lens to
something that only becomes visible at scale: **the diversity of our ideas.** A growing and
strikingly consistent body of research finds a *creativity–diversity trade-off*. When you
reach for a chatbot at the start of a creative task, your output usually gets **better** —
more novel, more polished, more useful — but it also drifts toward **everyone else's**
output. In the landmark causal experiment (Doshi & Hauser, *Science Advances*, 2024),
writers given AI story ideas wrote stories judged more creative, yet those stories were
measurably **more similar to each other** than stories written by humans alone [1]. A 2025
meta-analysis of 28 studies (8,214 participants) puts hard numbers on it: humans + AI are a
bit *more* creative individually (Hedges' *g* ≈ +0.27), but idea **diversity drops sharply**
(*g* ≈ −0.86) [2]. The mechanism is old and human: **anchoring.** The first idea you see
becomes the gravity well your thinking orbits — and the AI hands everyone a very similar
first idea.

**The single habit to start this week:** *diverge before you converge.* Before you ask AI
for ideas, spend 3–5 minutes generating your own — a rough list, a bad first draft, your own
angle. Then use AI to stretch and pressure-test *your* thinking, not to replace the blank
page. When you do prompt for ideas, ask for **several deliberately different options** ("give
me five approaches that disagree with each other") rather than accepting the first, tidy
answer. Your individual work stays sharp; the pool of ideas stays wide.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The individual case for AI-assisted creativity is genuinely strong, and this brief is not
going to pretend otherwise. AI ideas measurably raise the floor: in the *Science Advances*
experiment, the writers who gained the most were the **least** creative to begin with —
access to five AI ideas lifted their stories' novelty by ~10.7% and enjoyment by ~22.6%,
effectively **equalizing** their scores with the naturally most creative writers [1]. If you
struggle with the blank page, AI is a real leveler. That is a good thing, and it is why
adoption is racing ahead in marketing, design, code, research, and classrooms.

The catch shows up one level up, in aggregate. What is individually rational becomes
collectively costly: if every writer, marketer, or student anchors on similar model outputs,
the *space* of ideas quietly narrows even as each person's work improves. Doshi & Hauser call
this a **social dilemma** — you're better off using AI, but if everyone does, "collective
novelty" shrinks [1]. And the effect isn't confined to the moment of co-creation: a 2026
systematic review finds signs that homogenization can **persist beyond the AI session** and
spill into later, unaided work [4]. This is the week's reframe: keeping your brain "actively
in use" isn't only about *effort*, it's about **divergence** — protecting your capacity, and
our shared capacity, to think differently. The reassuring finding, repeated across the
literature, is that this trade-off is largely a product of *how* we deploy AI (uniform
prompts, first-answer anchoring), not an iron law of the technology [5].

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Anchoring is the neural hook — and human ideas add more per head (Moon, Green &
  Kushlev, *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans*, Dec 2025, peer-reviewed).**
  This team — including Georgetown cognitive neuroscientist **Adam Green**, whose work on
  creative cognition featured in earlier issues — introduces a "**diversity growth rate**":
  how many genuinely new ideas each additional output contributes. Their finding is blunt:
  **each additional human-written essay adds more new ideas than each additional
  GPT-generated one**, and the **gap widens as you scale up** [3]. Notably, they tried to fix
  it from the machine side — tuning GPT's parameters and prompts to boost diversity — and it
  **did not close the gap** [3]. The brain-relevant lesson: the antidote to homogenization is
  not a cleverer model setting but *human variance* — many minds, each anchored on something
  different, still out-diversify a fleet of prompts.

- **Why the first idea wins.** The convergence effect is fundamentally the well-known
  cognitive phenomenon of **anchoring**: the first framing you encounter constrains the range
  of everything you generate afterward. Doshi & Hauser show writers' stories became ~5% more
  similar *to the AI idea itself* — they were, in the authors' words, "anchored to some
  extent on the generative AI idea presented to them" [1]. Seeing a fluent, plausible answer
  before you've formed your own suppresses the messier, more original directions your mind
  would otherwise explore.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **The creativity–diversity trade-off is a social dilemma (Doshi & Hauser, *Science
  Advances*, 2024, peer-reviewed).** In a pre-registered, two-phase online experiment,
  N = 293 writers wrote eight-sentence stories under three conditions (no AI, one AI idea,
  five AI ideas); N = 600 independent evaluators rated them blind [1]. AI access raised
  novelty (up to +8.1%) and usefulness (up to +9.0%), especially for less-creative writers —
  but AI-assisted stories clustered together, more similar both to each other and to the
  seed ideas [1]. Behaviourally, this is a trap of incentives: because AI-assisted work is
  rated higher, each individual is **rewarded for converging** — and the sameness only
  becomes visible in the aggregate, where no single person feels responsible for it.

- **A quiet ownership cost.** The same study surfaced a human signal worth noting: evaluators
  imposed an **ownership penalty of at least 25%** on stories written with AI help, and most
  wanted AI use **disclosed** — while still, on balance, considering AI-assisted writing a
  legitimate "creative act" [1]. People value provenance. Part of staying creatively "alive"
  is keeping enough of your own fingerprints on the work that it is recognizably yours.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Homogenization is a deployment choice, not destiny (Diverse-personas study,
  *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans*, 2026, peer-reviewed).** Researchers
  directly extended Doshi & Hauser and found the trade-off is **not inherent to the
  technology**: by prompting the model with **10 deliberately diverse "personas"** to
  generate a varied pool of 300 story plots, they *preserved* — and in the single-plot
  condition, hinted at *enhancing* — collective diversity versus a human-only baseline [5].
  Their conclusion is a practitioner's playbook: treat the model as a **configurable partner,
  not a static tool**; vary prompts, seed personas, and inputs; and beware **uniform
  deployment** and **over-standardization**, which are what actually cause the convergence [5].

- **Default settings converge; deliberate variation diverges.** Read together, the evidence
  points to concrete levers: single prompts, default settings, and "take the first answer"
  workflows push everyone toward the same attractor; **multiple contrasting prompts**,
  higher-variance sampling, and asking explicitly for *disagreement* pull outputs apart
  again [2][5]. The skill for the AI era isn't just prompting well — it's prompting for
  **breadth** before you optimize for polish.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **The trade-off replicates across 28 studies (Holzner, Maier & Feuerriegel, arXiv,
  May 2025, preprint).** A systematic review and meta-analysis (691 records screened →
  28 studies, 8,214 participants) found **no reliable difference** between human-only and
  AI-only creativity (*g* ≈ −0.05, n.s.), a **small positive boost** from human–AI
  collaboration (*g* ≈ +0.27, p = .036), and a **large, robust drop in idea diversity** when
  humans work with AI (*g* ≈ −0.86, 95% CI [−1.33, −0.40], p < .001) [2]. The diversity
  finding rests on relatively few studies but survived leave-one-out checks; strikingly, the
  reduction was **largest in academic samples** (*g* ≈ −1.26) — precisely the students and
  researchers we most want thinking divergently [2].

- **A second meta-analysis, and a warning about spillover (de Rooij & Biskjær, systematic
  review + meta-analysis, preprint, Apr 2026).** Pooling 19 studies and 61 effect sizes, this
  review confirms a **small but statistically significant** homogenization effect that is
  robust to publication-bias checks, **strongest in semantically constrained ideation tasks**
  (and weaker for open-ended divergent thinking) [4]. Two policy-relevant notes: the effect
  may **persist beyond the co-creative moment** into later work, and it is best understood not
  as "loss of creativity" but as a gradual, **scale-dependent reorganization** of diversity
  shaped by task design and workflows — which means schools and organizations can design
  *against* it [4].

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Diverge before you converge.** Spend the first few minutes generating your own rough
  ideas or a messy first draft *before* opening the chatbot. Anchor on your own thinking
  first; use AI to extend it, not to originate it [1].
- **Prompt for disagreement, not consensus.** Instead of "give me ideas," ask for "five
  genuinely different approaches that contradict each other," or assign the model distinct
  personas/perspectives. Deliberate variation is what breaks the convergence [5].
- **Don't take the first answer.** The first fluent output is the strongest anchor. Generate
  several, discard the obvious ones, and combine or push against them rather than polishing
  the default [2][5].
- **Vary the tools, not just the prompts.** If a whole team pipes the same brief through the
  same model with the same settings, expect same-y results. Rotate models, raise variance,
  and compare against a no-AI baseline for high-stakes creative work [3][5].
- **Keep your fingerprints on it.** Add your own examples, constraints, and voice so the
  final product is recognizably yours — and disclose AI use where it matters [1].

### In personal life

- **Protect a "no-AI first draft" habit.** For writing, art, planning a trip, or solving a
  personal problem, try your own version first. The struggle of the blank page is where your
  distinctive ideas live — outsourcing it too early quietly narrows them [3].
- **Use AI to broaden, then choose like a human.** Great use: "show me options I wouldn't
  have thought of." Then apply your own taste and judgment to pick — that final selection is
  the creative act the model can't do for you.
- **Watch for anchoring in everyday decisions.** From naming a pet to choosing a gift, notice
  when the AI's first suggestion has quietly become *the* option. Ask, "what would I have come
  up with on my own?" before you commit [1].
- **Cultivate non-AI inputs.** Read widely, talk to people who disagree with you, and collect
  odd sources. Diversity in equals diversity out — your originality is downstream of how
  varied your inputs are.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "AI makes people more creative, full stop — so leaning on it for ideas is pure
> upside."
> **Reality:** It depends on the level you look at. *Individually*, the evidence is genuinely
> encouraging: AI assistance modestly raises the creativity of your output, and helps
> less-confident creators the most [1][2]. But *collectively*, the same tool tends to make
> everyone's ideas **converge** — a large, replicated drop in idea diversity — because we all
> anchor on similar first suggestions [1][2][3]. Crucially, this is **not** evidence that AI
> "kills creativity," and it is **not** inevitable: when people deliberately vary their
> prompts, use diverse personas, or diverge before converging, the diversity loss can be
> avoided or even reversed [5]. The goal isn't to avoid AI — it's to use it in a way that adds
> to the pool of ideas instead of quietly shrinking it.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Two of the five sources are preprints.** The 28-study meta-analysis [2] and the de Rooij &
  Biskjær review [4] are not yet peer-reviewed. Doshi & Hauser [1] and the two *CHBAH* papers
  [3][5] are peer-reviewed, and the flagship experiment is causal and pre-registered — but see
  the scope limits below.
- **The diversity effect rests on fewer studies than the creativity effect.** In the Holzner
  et al. meta-analysis the diversity estimate came from just 6 observations across 4 studies
  (1,017 participants), with high heterogeneity — robust to leave-one-out checks, but a
  smaller evidence base than the headline *g* = −0.86 implies [2]. Effect sizes for
  homogenization are described as **small-to-moderate**, not overwhelming [4].
- **Mostly GPT-family models and short text tasks.** Much of this work uses GPT-3.5/GPT-4 on
  short-form writing or ideation (e.g., eight-sentence stories) with crowd participants [1][2].
  Whether the same patterns hold for newer reasoning models, images, code, or long-form
  professional work is not yet settled.
- **"Diversity" is a measured construct, not the whole of creativity.** Most studies quantify
  diversity via embedding/semantic distance between outputs. That captures something real, but
  it is a proxy — reasonable people can measure creative variety differently.
- **This is not anti-AI.** Every source here agrees AI can genuinely help individual
  creativity, and that the diversity cost is largely a *deployment* problem with practical
  fixes [1][2][3][4][5]. Treat the habits above as low-cost ways to keep both your own thinking
  and our shared idea-space wide.
