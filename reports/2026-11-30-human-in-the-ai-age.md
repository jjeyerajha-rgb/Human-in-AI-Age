## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 008 asked you to *leave one gap empty* — to protect
boredom and the default-mode network from a tool that
fills every idle second. Issue 010 asked you to *move to
think* — to treat aerobic work as hardware for cognition.
Issue 012 asked you to *stay after the answer* — to keep
attention from fragmenting at the desk. This week is none
of those. It is about the **environment that restocks
directed attention after the model has spent it**.

Prompting, verifying, rewriting, and arguing with a
chatbot are all *directed* attention: effortful, top-down,
easily fatigued. Stephen Kaplan called the opposite
**soft fascination** — the way trees, water, and slow
clouds hold the mind without asking it to steer. That is
Attention Restoration Theory (ART). A 2025 meta-analysis
of **80 studies and 273 cognitive outcomes** found that
nature beats non-nature for overall cognitive capacity,
that the reliable gains sit in **working memory and
attentional control**, and that the nature-versus-city
advantage peaks around **30 minutes**. The effects are
**small** and **heterogeneous**. That is the honest size
of the claim [1].

The AI-era twist is older than ChatGPT and more
practical than a wilderness retreat. In 2019, Bin Jiang,
Rose Schmillen, and William Sullivan sent **81** adults
into a 15-minute outdoor break after fatiguing
proofreading and subtraction. Four conditions: green or
barren, laptop or no laptop. Only **green space without
the laptop** restored attention. Green space *with* the
laptop was statistically the same as a parking lot [2].
You can take the model to the park. You just will not
get the restoration.

**The single habit to start this week:** *take the trees,
leave the phone.* After a stretch of AI-heavy work, step
into actual green space for **15–30 minutes** with the
phone in a bag, not in the hand. Do not prompt from the
bench. Soft fascination cannot compete with a fluent
answer.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The copilot did not invent mental fatigue. It
concentrated it. Directed attention is the same resource
you use to ignore a notification, hold a constraint,
check a citation, and decide whether the model's
confident paragraph is true. Issue 021 documented how
AI can become the day's operating system. Issue 012
documented how the hour after a turn still wants
another turn. Both leave the same residue: a prefrontal
system that has been steering for hours and has nowhere
to recover except another screen.

Nature is not "self-care" in the wellness-app sense.
It is a different *kind* of stimulation. Urban and
digital environments grab attention hard — horns,
badges, tokens, a sentence that looks finished — and
then demand more directed attention to stay on the
task. Natural environments grab it softly. The
executive system can stand down. Marc Berman, John
Jonides, and Stephen Kaplan showed the behavioural
version in 2008: after a 50-minute walk, backwards
digit-span rose from **7.90 to 9.40** in the Ann Arbor
arboretum and only from **7.90 to 8.40** downtown, in
**38** students, winter weather included [3]. In 2024,
Amy McDonnell and David Strayer put EEG on **92**
adults after a matched 40-minute nature or urban walk
and found an enhanced **error-related negativity** —
a neural index of executive control — only after the
trees [4]. The legs moved in both conditions. The
restoration did not.

This is not Issue 008 again (empty input, any room).
It is not Issue 010 (exercise as blood-flow
infrastructure). It is not Issue 012 (the minute after
a prompt). Those briefs asked *whether* you fill the
gap, *whether* the body moves, and *whether* you stay
in the document. This one asks **what the gap looks
at**. A chat on a park bench is still a chat.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Stephen Kaplan (University of Michigan)** named
  the mechanism in 1995: directed attention is
  limited; environments restore it when they offer
  *being away*, *extent*, *compatibility*, and **soft
  fascination** [5]. Rachel and Stephen Kaplan's
  earlier work is the source; the 1995 paper is the
  one the restoration literature still cites. The
  claim is not that nature is magic. It is that some
  scenes ask the executive system to work and some
  scenes let it rest.
- **Marc Berman (University of Chicago)** is the
  experimental through-line. The 2008 walk study with
  Jonides and Kaplan remains the cleanest
  within-subject demonstration: nature improved
  backwards digit-span more than a length-matched
  urban walk, and mood did **not** explain the
  cognitive gain [3]. In 2012 he extended it to
  **20** adults with major depression: after a
  rumination prime, a 50-minute nature walk beat an
  urban walk on memory span with a large interaction
  (ηp² = .53), about five times the healthy-sample
  effect [6]. In a November 2025 interview he
  restated the practical rule: ten mindful minutes
  of real nature beats an hour of scrolling; photos
  and soundscapes help **less** than the thing
  itself; you do not have to *like* the walk for
  working memory to move [7].
- **Amy McDonnell and David Strayer (University of
  Utah)**. *Scientific Reports*, 22 January 2024.
  Randomised **40-minute** arboretum versus medical-
  campus walk, **N = 92**, phones banned, distances
  matched. Nature walkers rated the walk more
  restorative and showed a larger post-walk
  **error-related negativity**. Behavioural executive
  control on the Attention Network Task improved
  over time in both groups; the **time × condition
  interaction on reaction time was not significant**
  [4]. Cite this as neural evidence that the walk
  changed executive monitoring. Do not cite it as a
  clean behavioural win. A companion 2024 trial in
  the same journal found **lower frontal-midline
  theta** after the nature walk and higher after the
  urban walk — urban walking kept asking the
  executive system to work [8].
- **Gregory Bratman (University of Washington) and
  colleagues.** *PNAS*, 2015. **90-minute** walk,
  nature versus a busy road. Nature reduced
  self-reported rumination (d = 0.34 in the nature
  arm) and **subgenual prefrontal** blood-oxygen
  signal — a region tied to brooding self-focus [9].
  This is affect and a neural correlate, not a
  working-memory test. It is why a green rest is not
  only "focus." It is also a way off the loop a
  chatbot is happy to keep spinning.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Bin Jiang, Rose Schmillen, and William Sullivan.**
  *Environment and Behavior*, 2019. This is the
  paper the AI workplace needed before it had a
  name. **81** adults, 17–35, four outdoor
  15-minute rests after a cognitive load. Setting
  (green vs barren) mattered. Laptop use mattered.
  The **interaction** mattered, F(1, 72) = 5.81,
  p = .019, ηp² = .08. Paired tests: only **green
  without laptop** moved attention (MD = 0.92
  trials, t(18) = 4.99, p < .001). The authors'
  sentence is the habit: it is not enough to go to
  a green space; you have to put the device aside
  *in* that space [2]. A 2026 chatbot on a sunny
  bench is the 2019 laptop.
- **Cameron Bell, Rebecca St George, Cynthia Honan,
  Lachlan Bell, Alex Jolly, and Allison Matthews
  (University of Tasmania).** *Journal of
  Environmental Psychology*, June 2025 (online 21
  May). The largest duration-aware ART meta to date:
  **273 outcomes, 80 studies**, correlated
  hierarchical effects. Nature beat non-nature
  overall. Of 11 cognitive domains, only **working
  memory** and **attentional control** showed
  reliable benefits. Duration moderated the
  difference **non-linearly**; the largest
  nature-versus-non-nature gap sits near **30
  minutes**. Effects were **small**; heterogeneity
  was **substantial**; pre-fatigued participants
  gained more [1]. An earlier 2018 meta by
  **Stevenson, Schilhab, and Bentsen** had already
  pointed at working memory, cognitive flexibility,
  and (less reliably) attentional control, with
  **low-to-moderate** effects and a hint that *real*
  nature beats images [10]. Bell 2025 is the update;
  Stevenson is the domain map. Treat both as
  "small, not zero."
- **MaryCarol Hunter, Mardie Gillespie, and
  Sophie-Ying Yu-Pu Chen (University of Michigan).**
  *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2019. **36** city
  dwellers took self-chosen "nature pills" (≥10
  minutes, ≥3 times a week, 8 weeks). Salivary
  cortisol fell **21.3% per hour** beyond the
  hormone's ordinary diurnal drop; the **most
  efficient** window was **20–30 minutes** [11].
  This is stress physiology, not digit-span. It
  rhymes with Bell's cognitive peak. It is not a
  reason to sit under a tree for three hours
  expecting a linear bonus.
- **Lee, Williams, Sargent, Williams, and Johnson.**
  *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 2015. A
  **40-second** view of a flowering green roof
  sustained attention better than a bare concrete
  roof in a lab micro-break [12]. Use this as the
  office fallback when you cannot leave the
  building — a window, not a feed — not as a
  substitute for Jiang's 15 minutes outside.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Cal Newport (Georgetown).** The practitioner
  version of ART does not mention Kaplan. It
  mentions a trail. In *On Quiet Creativity* he
  leaves the building, walks Georgetown's paths, and
  rearranges a result until it yields a new layer
  [13]. In his cognitive-fitness advice the
  instruction is stricter: several phoneless
  thinking walks a week, phone buried in a bag or
  left at home, attention pointed at **one**
  problem, then a journal line so the insight has
  to take a shape [14]. Issue 021 used Newport for
  the day's *architecture*. This week uses him for
  the walk's *contents*: no chat, one question,
  actual trees if you can get them.
- **Marc Berman**, again, as the scientist speaking
  to operators. The November 2025 conversation is
  the anti-wellness-app brief: fractal structure and
  gentle motion are easier for the visual system to
  compress than a city street or a token stream;
  **enjoyment is not required**; a cold Michigan
  walk still moved working memory; **the phone
  during the walk erases the spell** because it
  rehires directed attention [7]. That last clause
  is Jiang in plain speech.
- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton)** has spent two years
  telling knowledge workers to keep a human in the
  loop. The restoration addendum is simpler than a
  new prompt pattern: the loop needs a place that
  is not another model. Use the copilot at the
  desk. Recover the resource that makes the next
  loop intelligent **off** the glass.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Carina Fowler, Joan Luby, Theresa Bastain,
  Carrie Breton, and Deanna Barch (Washington
  University in St. Louis and collaborators).**
  *Environment International*, 2026. **N = 11,868**
  ABCD children, mean age 9.96. Census-tract **tree
  canopy plus park cover** (averaged; coefficients
  scaled per 10 percentage points) associated with
  better directed attention (β = 0.76, p < .001)
  and crystallized intelligence (β = 0.83,
  p < .001) after adjusting for deprivation, sex,
  ADHD, stimulants, sleep, perceived safety, media
  use, prenatal tobacco, **PM2.5**, and **physical
  activity**. PM2.5 **partially mediated** both
  links (indirect β = 0.06). Physical activity did
  **not** [15]. This is **cross-sectional**, not a
  walk RCT. It is the developmental public-health
  version of ART: trees and parks, not any strip of
  grass; safety and no-phone use are theoretically
  load-bearing and here were at least *measured*.
  WashU's November 2026 write-up of the paper is
  why this issue is timely [16].
- **Mathew White, Ian Alcock, James Grellier, and
  colleagues.** *Scientific Reports*, 2019.
  **N = 19,806**, nationally weighted English
  survey. Recreational nature contact of **≥120
  minutes a week** associated with better
  self-reported health (OR = 1.59 for 120–179
  minutes) and well-being (OR = 1.23), peaking
  around **200–300 minutes**, one long visit or
  several short ones [17]. This is **not** an
  attention test. It is a dose analog for people
  who ask "how much." Two hours a week of actual
  contact is a public-health floor, not a cognitive
  ceiling.
- **School and workplace design.** Fowler and Barch
  argue that ART's safety and no-device clauses
  were missing from earlier child-green-space
  papers — which is one reason those papers
  disagreed. Jiang is the adult workplace
  translation of the same clause. A campus "wellness
  walk" that is a walking meeting with Slack open
  is, on this evidence, an urban walk in green
  clothing.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Take the trees, leave the phone.** After a
  block of prompting, reviewing, or agent-wrangling,
  take **15–30 minutes** in actual green space —
  courtyard trees, a park, a river path. Phone in
  a bag. Do not "just check the draft." Jiang says
  the device cancels the setting [2]. Bell says
  the cognitive gap versus city exposure is largest
  near half an hour [1].
- **Do not count a walking meeting.** Talking,
  navigating traffic, and glancing at Slack are
  directed attention. McDonnell's urban walk moved
  the legs and still asked the executive system to
  work [8]. If the meeting needs to happen, have
  it. Do not file it under restoration.
- **Window before feed.** If you cannot leave the
  building, take the Lee micro-break: look at
  living plants or sky for under a minute, not a
  timeline [12]. Then stand up (Issue 010 still
  owns the movement snack). The window is a
  fallback, not the 20-minute dose.
- **Recover before the hard judgement.** ART is a
  *preload* for the work Issue 007 and Issue 015
  already asked you to keep: the load-bearing
  check, the decision you still own. A depleted
  verifier is a polite rubber stamp. Restore, then
  check.
- **Protect one outdoor block on AI-heavy days.**
  Issue 021 still owns *when* the model is allowed
  on the calendar. This week owns *where* the off
  shift happens. A cafeteria with a laptop is
  barren-with-device. A tree line without a phone
  is the condition that moved the score.

### In personal life

- **Two hours a week of actual contact, not
  scenery on glass.** White's survey is health and
  mood, not digit-span, but the floor is usable:
  **120 minutes** of recreational nature, split or
  lumped [17]. A nature documentary in bed is not
  the same resource.
- **Phoneless thinking walk, one question.**
  Newport's rule plus Berman's scene: one problem
  in the head, trees in the eyes, phone not in the
  hand [13][14][7]. After, write one line so the
  walk becomes a thought and not a vibe.
- **Do not rumination-prompt on a green bench.**
  Bratman found the walk itself lowered brooding
  [9]. A companion model that will discuss your
  loop for as long as you like is the opposite
  intervention. If you need a person, that is
  still Issue 004.
- **Safety first, taste optional.** Kaplan's
  *compatibility* and Fowler's covariate: you have
  to feel safe. You do not have to find the walk
  charming. Berman's January arboretum still
  worked [3][7][15].
- **Kids: parks and canopy, not a strip of grass
  plus a tablet.** Fowler's ABCD signal is tree
  cover and park cover, with media use measured
  because ART predicts a phone in the park will
  block the effect [15]. The family version of
  Jiang is the same sentence with a smaller
  device.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** Science has proved that nature "heals the
brain," that a green screensaver is basically a
forest, and that AI will fry your attention unless
you flee the city.

**Reality:** The best 2025 evidence is a **small**,
**messy** advantage for working memory and
attentional control after nature versus non-nature,
largest near **30 minutes**, bigger after fatigue
[1]. A 15-minute green break restores attention
only if the laptop stays closed [2]. A 40-minute
Utah walk changed an EEG marker of executive
control; the behavioural interaction was **not**
significant [4]. The authors of the 2025 meta, in
their own virtual-image study, got a **null** on
digit-span [18]. Neighbourhood trees correlate with
children's attention in 11,868 kids, partly through
cleaner air, and that design cannot prove that
*your* lunch walk caused *your* better afternoon
[15]. None of this is a lesion. None of it says
abandon the model. It says directed attention is a
budget, AI spends it, and the cheapest refill we
have evidence for is **real nature without a
screen**.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Bell 2025: small effects, huge heterogeneity.**
  Domain differences may not themselves be
  significant. Do not advertise a 30-minute park
  visit as a guaranteed IQ bump. Advertise it as
  the duration where the *average* nature-versus-
  city gap was largest.
- **Do not put Stevenson’s g values in Bell’s
  mouth.** Stevenson 2018 is the earlier domain
  map (working memory / flexibility / attentional
  control, low-to-moderate). Bell 2025 is the
  bigger, duration-aware update. We did not extract
  Bell’s per-domain g from the paywalled tables; we
  are not inventing them.
- **Jiang is a laptop, 15 minutes, N = 81, ages
  17–35.** It is not a ChatGPT trial. The
  generalisation we are willing to make is narrow:
  a device that rehires directed attention in a
  green setting behaves like the laptop. A phone
  with a chatbot is that device.
- **McDonnell & Strayer: neural yes, behavioural
  interaction no** on ANT executive RT. Sample
  mostly White and female, one Utah arboretum.
  Honest citation only.
- **Fowler 2026 is cross-sectional.** β = 0.76 is
  not "ten more trees made a child 0.76 points
  smarter this afternoon." PM2.5 mediation means
  air quality is part of the story, which ART did
  not need and did not rule out. Physical activity
  was **not** the mediator — that is the honest
  distinction from Issue 010, not a proof that
  walking is irrelevant.
- **Hunter N = 36; Berman 2008 N = 38; Berman 2012
  N = 20; Bratman ~38.** Classic, small, useful.
  They are not 2026 AI studies.
- **Bell’s own 2025 JoCN study (N = 40, images)
  found null working memory.** Virtual nature is a
  weak substitute. Nature-relatedness moderated
  some outcomes: restoration is not universal [18].
- **White 2019 is self-reported health, not
  cognition.** Use it as a weekly-contact floor,
  not as an attention result.
- **Access and safety are not evenly distributed.**
  A brief that says "go to the trees" without
  naming that is pretending. The fallback is the
  safest nearby green you can actually use, phone
  down; it is not a scolding.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing
  here is a drop in IQ, a lesion, or a reason to
  panic. The evidence is about *patterns of use*:
  whether the resource you spend on the model gets
  a setting that can refill it.
