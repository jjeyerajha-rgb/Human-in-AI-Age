## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 012 asked you to *stay after the answer* —
to remain in the destination file instead of bouncing
to Slack the moment the model returns. That brief was
about the **switch**. This week is about the
**architecture of the day**: whether AI is a tool you
open for a named job, or the operating system you
live inside. **Digital minimalism** — Cal Newport’s
name for it — is not abstinence. It is a tool diet.
You keep the tools that serve a value you can name,
and you give the rest a shift, not the day.

The newest field picture is uncomfortable and useful.
ActivTrak’s 2026 State of the Workplace read
**443 million hours** of digital activity across
**163,638** employees in **1,111** organisations.
Eighty percent now use AI at work; time inside AI
tools is up **eightfold**; the average company runs
**seven or more** AI products, up from two in 2023.
Among **10,584** people compared 180 days before and
after they adopted AI, email time rose **104%**,
chat and messaging **145%**, and business-management
tools **94%**. Focused, uninterrupted work fell about
**9%** — roughly **23 minutes** a day — while
non-users barely moved. Weekend work was up more
than **40%**. The workday got slightly shorter
(**−2%**) and much denser. That is not leisure
returned. It is more shallow motion [1][2].

A different kind of evidence says the same thing in
the lab. Grace Liu, Brian Christian, Tsvetomira
Dumbalska, Michiel Bakker, and Rachit Dubey ran a
series of randomised trials (**N = 1,222**) on
fractions and SAT-style reading. About **ten minutes**
of a helpful sidebar (GPT-5, answers on tap) raised
solve rates while it was open. When it closed, people
who had used it solved less and **skipped more**
than people who never had it — Cohen’s *d* around
**−0.42** on unassisted performance. The drop
concentrated in the **61%** who asked for the answer
directly [3]. Instant help trains the brain’s effort
calculator. The next hard minute looks optional.

**The single habit to start this week:** *give AI a
shift, not the day.* Pick one named window — 25 to
90 minutes — in which the model may draft, summarise,
or argue. Outside that window, the chat is closed.
Score the day by the work that shipped, not by how
many prompts felt fast.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The promise of workplace AI was time returned to
thinking. The 2026 telemetry says the opposite
pattern is winning: AI speeds the *wrong* tasks, so
the volume of those tasks expands until the calendar
is full of them. Cal Newport, writing in March 2026
about the ActivTrak numbers, called this a digital
productivity trap — the same trap email already
sprung. Sending a message was faster than a fax. The
day then became a furious exchange that felt busy
and produced less. Berkeley’s Aruna Ranganathan, in
the *Wall Street Journal* account of the same data,
put the mechanism in one line: “AI makes additional
tasks feel easy and accessible, creating a sense of
momentum” [2][4]. Momentum is not a scoreboard.

This is not Issue 012 again. That brief was the
**hour after a turn** — attention residue, 908 app
switches, stay in the file. It is not Issue 008
either: that one reserved empty gaps for the default
mode. It is not Issue 001’s “hard ten minutes first”
or Issue 003’s apprenticeship. Those were about *how*
you use the model on a task. This week is *whether
the model is allowed to be the weather of the day*.
Newport’s 2019 book already had the rule: new tools
are optional until they serve a value you would
defend without them [5]. In 2026 the optional tool
became an always-on colleague who never leaves the
room and never says no.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Amitai Shenhav, Matthew Botvinick, and Jonathan
  Cohen** (*Neuron*, 2013) gave the field a name for
  the calculation the brain runs before it bothers:
  the **expected value of control**. Dorsal anterior
  cingulate cortex weighs the payoff of a controlled
  process against how much control it would take and
  how costly that effort feels, then allocates
  (or withholds) prefrontal control [6]. A sidebar
  that answers in two seconds changes the
  equation. The controlled process — stay with the
  fraction, stay with the paragraph — looks expensive
  relative to a prompt. Liu and colleagues’ skip-rate
  jump is what that calculation looks like from the
  outside. This is not a lesion. It is a price
  update.
- **Wouter Kool, Joseph McGuire, Zev Rosen, and
  Matthew Botvinick** (*Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: General*, 2010) showed that people
  will take a longer path through a task graph to
  avoid a modest demand on control. Effort avoidance
  is the default, not a character flaw [7]. An
  always-open assistant is a permanent low-demand
  path. The brain will take it unless the day’s
  design makes the high-demand path the default for
  a while.
- **Michael Inzlicht, Amitai Shenhav, and
  Christopher Olivola** (*Trends in Cognitive
  Sciences*, 2018) added the other half: effort is
  costly *and* can be valued. People sometimes
  choose the harder route when it is the one that
  still feels like theirs [8]. Digital minimalism
  is a way of protecting that valuation. If every
  hard minute can be skipped, the “this is mine”
  signal never fires. Issue 009 covered ownership
  of output. This week is ownership of *effort*.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Liu, Christian, Dumbalska, Bakker, and Dubey**
  (2026 preprint, **N = 1,222** across RCTs) is
  this week’s causal AI paper. Experiment 1
  (analysed **N = 307**): after AI-on practice,
  unassisted solve rate **0.57** versus **0.73**
  in controls (*d* = **−0.42**); skip rate
  **0.20** versus **0.11** (*d* = **0.25**).
  Experiment 2 (**N = 585**) added a pretest and
  replicated the pattern; the harm sat in the
  **61%** who used the model for direct answers,
  not in the **27%** who asked for hints.
  Experiment 3 (**N = 168**) moved the same
  design to SAT reading: solve **0.76** versus
  **0.89** (*d* = **−0.42**); skip **0.08**
  versus **0.01** (*d* = **0.42**). The public
  significance statement is blunt: current systems
  are optimised for short-term helpfulness and
  never say no [3]. **Not yet peer-reviewed.**
  Treat the exact *d* as provisional. Treat the
  direction — help now, quit later — as the thing
  to design around.
- **ActivTrak Productivity Lab** (March 2026).
  Observational, vendor-collected, not a trial.
  The before/after slice (**N = 10,584**) is still
  the largest behavioural picture we have of what
  *adoption* does to a knowledge-work day: more
  email, more chat, more systems touched, less
  deep work, more weekend hours. Collaboration
  **+34%**, multitasking **+12%**, focus time at
  a three-year low [1]. Honest limit: people who
  adopt AI may already have been in intensifying
  jobs. The comparison to non-users helps; it does
  not randomise.
- **Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer,
  and Matthew Gentzkow** (*American Economic
  Review*, 2020). Not AI — Facebook. Still the
  cleanest large deactivation RCT in the digital-
  minimalism family: **2,743** users offered a
  price, **1,661** in the impact sample, four weeks
  off around the 2018 midterms, **>90%** compliance.
  Deactivation freed about **60 minutes** a day,
  raised a subjective-well-being index by
  **0.09 SD**, cut depression **0.09 SD**, and
  **80%** of the treatment group said the break
  was good for them. Afterward, Facebook use stayed
  **0.61 SD** lower [9]. The lesson we are allowed:
  a bounded fast from an ambient network is
  feasible, and people often like the life that
  appears in the gap. The lesson we are not:
  ChatGPT is Facebook.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Cal Newport (Georgetown)**. *Digital Minimalism*
  (2019) is the doctrine: start from values, add
  tools that serve them, decline the rest [5].
  The 2026 essays apply it to copilots. In “Why
  Hasn’t AI Made Work Easier?” and “Avoiding
  Digital Productivity Traps” (23 March 2026) he
  gives three operational rules. **Scoreboard:**
  count papers, shipped projects, or resolved
  cases — not prompts per hour. **Bottleneck:**
  put the model on the step that actually gates
  the scoreboard, not on the plot that was never
  the constraint. **Separate deep from shallow:**
  if AI accidentally multiplies admin, the damage
  stops at the admin block [4][10]. Newport has
  been a *voice* in Issues 011, 012, 015, 019,
  and 020. This is the first week he is the
  theme.
- **Xinyue Chen, Kunlin Ruan, Kexin Phyllis Ju,
  and Xu Wang** (*Proceedings of the ACM on
  Human-Computer Interaction*, 16 October 2025).
  Within-subjects note-taking, **N = 30**, three
  assistance levels during lecture videos.
  **Intermediate** AI (a live summary beside your
  own notes) produced the best post-test.
  **Automated** AI (structured notes done for you)
  produced the worst — and was the favourite,
  because it felt easy [11]. **Peer-reviewed.**
  Small N, lecture task. The preference/benefit
  split is the practical warning: the setup people
  like is the one that removes the work.
- **Ethan Mollick’s** working rule still applies,
  and it is a scheduling rule as much as a
  prompting rule: give the model a job
  description, not a desk in every meeting. A
  tool with hours is a colleague. A tool with no
  hours is an atmosphere.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Newport’s Slow Productivity** (2024) is the
  academic companion: do fewer things, work at a
  natural pace, obsess over quality. The ActivTrak
  pattern — more tools, more touches, less focus —
  is the failure mode that book was written
  against. Organisations that celebrate “AI
  adoption rate” as a KPI are scoring the
  opposite of the scoreboard.
- **Do not treat “human in the loop” as a time
  gift.** A human who is in the loop *all day*
  is not doing oversight. They are doing
  switchboard. The EU’s emerging right-to-
  disconnect debate and Australia’s under-16
  social-media restriction (noted in Issue 012)
  are blunt instruments. Adult knowledge work
  needs a finer one: **office hours for the
  model**.
- **Issue 012 still applies at the minute scale.**
  Castelo, Kushlev, Ward, Esterman, and Reiner
  (*PNAS Nexus*, 2025) showed that two weeks
  without mobile internet improved sustained
  attention (ITT *d_z* ≈ **0.23**) and well-being.
  That trial is not this week’s headline — it
  already carried Issue 012 — but it remains the
  causal proof that *constant connection* is a
  design choice, not a personality [12]. This
  week’s move is upstream: do not connect the
  workday to seven AI products and then try to
  stay after each answer.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Give AI a shift, not the day.** On the calendar,
  block one window (start with **50 minutes**)
  labelled with the actual job: “draft the
  appendix,” “steel-man the counterargument,”
  “summarise the three filings.” When the window
  ends, quit the chat. If you need it again, it
  waits for the next shift — the way a helpful
  colleague would.
- **Write tomorrow’s scoreboard tonight.** One
  line: the artefact or decision that would make
  the day count. Newport’s test: if the tool
  made the tasks faster but the scoreboard did
  not move, it was a trap [10].
- **Put the model on the bottleneck, not the
  busywork.** If the thing that actually gates
  your output is a conversation, a data source,
  or a judgement, that is where assistance
  belongs. Speeding the slides is optional.
- **Prefer intermediate help.** Chen et al.: a
  live scaffold beside *your* notes beat
  automated notes you did not write [11]. Liu
  et al.: hints did not carry the persistence
  penalty that “answer?” did [3]. Ask for
  structure, alternatives, or a check — not
  the finished object — inside the shift.
- **Cap the stack.** ActivTrak’s average
  organisation now runs **seven or more** AI
  tools [1]. Pick two for the next month. The
  third tab is usually a second atmosphere,
  not a second capability.
- **Issue 012 still owns the minute after the
  answer.** When the shift is on, stay in the
  destination file. This week owns the hours
  when the shift is *off*.

### In personal life

- **Give the chat office hours at home too.**
  Planning a trip, explaining a concept to a
  child, drafting a hard message: one sitting.
  Not an ambient companion between dinner and
  sleep. Issue 011 remains the night rule
  (*park the chat*). This week is the evening
  *before* the night.
- **Run a one-week tool audit, Newport-style.**
  List the AI and social apps you open without
  a named job. Drop one for seven days. Allcott
  is not a licence to claim ChatGPT will raise
  your happiness 0.09 SD. It is permission to
  treat a bounded fast as an experiment, not a
  personality transplant [9].
- **Protect one unassisted hour that is not
  work.** A walk, a page of a book with a
  spine, a conversation. The ActivTrak pattern
  at home is the same trap with different
  icons: more motion, less of the thing you
  would put on a life scoreboard.
- **Do not confuse momentum with rest.**
  Ranganathan’s “sense of momentum” is the
  feeling of extra tasks becoming easy [2].
  Rest is when nothing is being optimised.
  Issue 008’s empty gap still belongs on the
  calendar.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** If I adopt more AI, I will get my
evenings back and finally have time to think.

**Reality:** The largest behavioural dataset we
have says adoption *intensified* the day —
more mail, more chat, more systems, less deep
work, more weekend hours — while the workday
got only 2% shorter [1]. Lab RCTs add the
inner half: a brief dose of instant help
makes the next unassisted minute easier to
skip [3]. People also prefer the assistance
level that teaches them the least [11]. The
habit is not to quit AI. It is to stop
letting it be the climate. Give it a shift.
Score the work. Keep the rest of the day
human.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **ActivTrak is a vendor telemetry report,
  not a journal article.** Before/after
  adoption is not random assignment. Heavy
  AI users may already have been in
  accelerating roles. Definitions of “focus
  time” and “AI tool” are the company’s.
  Use it as a map of *what days look like*,
  not as a causal claim that Copilot stole
  23 minutes from your prefrontal cortex.
- **Liu et al. is a preprint.** Fraction
  problems and SAT items, Prolific samples,
  a sidebar that already knew the answer,
  skip buttons with no penalty. Effects
  appeared in **~10–15 minutes**. That is
  a warning about *acute* conditioning, not
  a measured year of deskilling. Do not
  quote *d* = −0.42 as a workplace IQ tax.
- **Chen et al. is N = 30**, lecture
  note-taking, one session per condition.
  Directionally consistent with Liu; not
  a field RCT of knowledge work.
- **Allcott et al. is Facebook in 2018,
  not ChatGPT in 2026.** Paid deactivation,
  US midterms, a sample younger and more
  educated than the average user. Cite it
  for the *feasibility* of a bounded fast,
  not as evidence that leaving Claude will
  raise your life satisfaction.
- **Shenhav, Kool, and Inzlicht are not
  about AI.** They justify treating effort
  allocation as a real neural and
  behavioural system that a zero-friction
  assistant can retune. They do not image
  a chatbot.
- **Castelo et al. and Ward’s 2017 “brain
  drain” already live in Issue 012.** Ward
  did not reliably replicate; 012 said so.
  Do not revive mere-presence as settled
  science. Do not claim AI damages the
  brain. Nothing here is a drop in IQ, a
  diagnosis, or a reason to panic. The
  evidence is about *patterns of use*:
  whether the model has a shift, or the
  day.
