## 1. Executive Summary

For the past seven weeks this brief has looked at the thinking you do *with* AI — the
critical thinking before you prompt, the memory you offload, the creativity you share, the
output you check. This week we look at something quieter and easier to miss: the thinking
that happens when you're doing **nothing at all**. The pause while a page loads. The wait in
line. The walk between meetings. The blank-page moment before you start. Boredom.

Those empty gaps used to be unavoidable. Now they are exactly what AI — and the phone it
lives on — is engineered to fill. There is always something to ask, generate, scroll, or
summarise. The problem is that the brain does not treat those gaps as wasted time. When you
stop feeding it external input, a system called the **default mode network (DMN)** switches
on and does real work: it replays and consolidates recent memories, connects distant ideas,
and lets the mind wander toward insight [2]. A 2025 peer-reviewed experiment found that
people who **mind-wandered more during a short break** produced measurably more creative
writing afterward — an effect specific to mind-wandering, not to deliberately grinding on the
problem [1]. In other words, some of your best thinking is scheduled for the moments you're
now most tempted to fill.

None of this means phones or AI "rot your brain" — the honest evidence, below, is more mixed
and more interesting than that [5][6]. But two things are well established: fragmenting your
attention has a real cost [3], and the brain uses undemanding downtime to consolidate and
create [1][2]. Put together, they point to a small, contrarian habit.

**The single habit to start this week:** *leave one gap empty, on purpose.* Pick one
recurring idle moment — the commute, the coffee queue, the first five minutes of a hard task,
the shower — and deliberately **do not fill it** with a screen or a prompt. Let yourself be
a little bored. You're not wasting the time; you're giving the back half of your brain the
quiet it needs to do the part of the job AI can't.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Two trends are colliding. The first is that our attention was already fragmented before AI
arrived. Gloria Mark's field research — measuring real people at real desks, not lab
subjects — found that the average time we spend on a single screen before switching has fallen
from about **2.5 minutes in 2004 to roughly 47 seconds** today, and that every switch carries
a **"switch cost"**: extra time to reorient, higher error rates, and measurably more stress
[3]. We are already living in short bursts.

The second trend is that generative AI removes the last bits of friction that used to create
natural pauses. You no longer stare at a blank page while your mind idles toward an angle —
you ask for a first draft. You no longer sit with a half-formed question on a walk — you dictate
it and get an answer before you reach the corner. Each of these is a genuine convenience. But
collectively they colonise the **incubation time** that creative work depends on. The
mechanism isn't mysterious or mystical: when external demands drop, the DMN comes online and
supports **spontaneous memory reactivation** — the brain quietly replaying and stabilising
what you recently learned, and abstracting the gist from the details [2]. Fill every gap with
new input and you keep interrupting that process before it finishes.

The reassuring framing, consistent with prior issues, is that this is about **the pattern of
use, not fixed harm**. The fix isn't ascetic abstinence; it's *reserving* a little empty time
the way you'd reserve time for sleep or exercise. And it's worth being precise about the
evidence: as we'll see, the direct claim that "filling breaks with your phone damages your
thinking" is only weakly supported [5][6], while the claim that "undemanding downtime helps
memory and creativity" is on much firmer ground [1][2]. That asymmetry is the whole basis for
the recommendation — protect the downtime because it *helps*, not because screens are poison.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **The "resting" brain isn't resting — it's consolidating (Joshi, Tompary & Kucyi,
  *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*, 2025; peer-reviewed review).** This synthesis
  bridges two literatures that were traditionally studied separately: **spontaneous thought**
  (mind-wandering during wakeful rest) and **memory consolidation** (the stabilisation of new
  memories over time). Their argument is that the DMN provides the "neural context" in which
  the hippocampus can replay recently learned information — via bursts called **sharp-wave
  ripples** — propagating it to the cortex for long-term storage [2]. The practical upshot:
  quiet, task-free moments are when the brain does the filing it can't do while you're
  taking in new material. Their headline bullets are blunt — *"spontaneous thought aids memory
  consolidation through DMN-supported reactivation"* and *"reactivation-linked DMN activity
  supports long-term memory stabilization"* [2]. Every gap you fill with fresh input is a gap
  the filing system doesn't get.

- **Mind-wandering during a break predicts *more* creativity afterward (McDaniel, Habibi &
  Kaplan, *Scientific Reports*, 2025; peer-reviewed, preregistered, N = 200).** Participants
  wrote a short story, took a 10-minute break doing one of several tasks, then wrote again.
  The type of break didn't reliably change creativity — but *regardless* of the assigned task,
  the people who **mind-wandered more** during the break showed greater within-person creative
  improvement (measured by semantic distance and corroborated by human and GPT-4 raters). The
  benefit was **specific to mind-wandering** and did **not** extend to deliberately, explicitly
  thinking about the story during the break [1]. This is a neat, honest result: it's not
  effortful problem-chewing that helps during a pause — it's letting the mind drift. Which is
  exactly the state a screen or a prompt short-circuits.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Avoiding boredom with a device tends to backfire (Tam, *Communications Psychology*, 2024;
  peer-reviewed).** This review makes the case that boredom is **rising** in the digital age
  and that our default coping strategy — reaching for a phone — is often self-defeating. From a
  "predictive-processing" view, boredom is a **signal** that your current activity lacks
  meaning or challenge; reflexively drowning that signal in stimulation means you never act on
  what it's telling you. The evidence she assembles is uncomfortable: people who habitually
  *avoid* boredom tend to become **more** prone to it, while those who use "approach" strategies
  (re-engaging, reframing) are bored less often [4]. The behavioural lesson isn't to seek out
  boredom for its own sake — it's to stop treating every dull moment as an emergency to be
  swiped away.

- **Reaching for the phone is mostly a self-initiated habit, not a response to alerts.** Tam
  cites first-person video research finding that **~89% of phone interactions were initiated by
  the user**, not by a notification — roughly one pickup every five minutes [4]. That matters
  for the fix: since most of the filling is *us*, not the device buzzing, the lever is a
  personal rule ("this gap stays empty"), not just turning off notifications.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **AI is being designed precisely to eliminate the pause.** The explicit product goal of a
  good assistant is to reduce "time to answer" and remove friction — to make sure you never sit
  with an unanswered question or a blank page. That is genuinely useful, and this brief is not
  anti-tool. But it's worth naming the trade-off clearly: the same friction that feels like
  waste (the blank page, the "hmm," the walk without input) is the raw material of incubation
  [1][2]. When you route every nascent thought straight to a model for a polished answer, you
  skip the messy, associative middle where the DMN would otherwise connect it to everything else
  you know. The engineering optimises for the *output*; the human value often lived in the
  *interval*.

- **Keep a "no-AI, no-input" slot, not just a no-AI task.** Earlier issues suggested doing some
  work by hand (a "No-AI Hour") to keep skills warm. This week's angle is subtler and
  complementary: protect time with **no input at all** — not AI, not a podcast, not a feed. The
  cognitive benefit here isn't from effortful practice; it's from the *absence* of external
  demand that lets spontaneous thought run [1][2]. A walk with headphones off does something a
  walk with a podcast cannot.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **The strongest evidence is for the *upside* of downtime, not the *harm* of filling it.**
  This is the intellectually honest core of the week. Experiments that put a phone in your
  break and looked for damage have mostly come up **empty**: a 2026 study in *Psychological
  Reports* found **no significant difference** in mind-wandering or subsequent task performance
  between breaks spent scrolling and breaks spent with your own thoughts [5]. And three
  correlational studies on creativity and smartphone use found **at most tiny** relationships
  between how much people use their phones and their divergent-thinking scores [6]. So the case
  for protecting downtime should *not* rest on "screens wreck cognition." It should rest on the
  positive finding that **undemanding, low-input rest supports consolidation and creative
  incubation** [1][2]. Protect the gap because of what the brain *does* in it — a much better
  footing than a moral panic about devices.

- **For educators and teams: build in incubation, don't just add tools.** The incubation
  literature has a clear design implication — inserting an undemanding break between drafting
  and revising can raise the quality of the result [1]. In a work or classroom culture racing to
  "AI-accelerate" every step, the counter-intuitive, evidence-based move is to deliberately
  **leave a gap** between generating and finalising, and to resist filling students' or workers'
  every idle minute with one more optimised input.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Leave a gap between generating and finalising.** For anything that matters, draft (with or
  without AI), then take a short, undemanding break — a walk, a stretch, a stare out the window —
  *before* you revise. Incubation measurably improves the second pass [1].
- **Protect a "no-input" block, not just a no-AI block.** Beyond doing some work by hand,
  schedule 20–30 minutes with no AI, no feed, no podcast — just the problem and your own mind.
  That's when associative, cross-domain thinking happens [1][2].
- **Don't fill the first five minutes of a hard task.** Resist opening the model the instant you
  sit down. Sit with the blank page long enough to form your own angle first; you'll prompt
  better *and* keep the thinking yours [1].
- **Batch, don't fragment.** Every switch has a switch cost in time, accuracy, and stress [3].
  Group AI-assisted tasks and defer low-priority pings so your attention stays on one thing long
  enough to get somewhere.
- **Treat "I'm bored, let me ask the AI" as a signal.** Boredom on a task can mean it lacks
  challenge or clarity — a cue to re-scope or re-engage, not just to escape into stimulation [4].

### In personal life

- **Reclaim one daily idle moment.** Commute, queue, walk, shower — pick one and keep it
  screen-free. Let your mind wander; that's a feature, not dead time [1][2][4].
- **Take walks without audio sometimes.** Headphones off, no podcast. A low-input walk creates
  the exact conditions — undemanding, self-generated thought — that predict creative insight [1].
- **Notice the reflexive pickup.** Most phone reaches are self-initiated, not prompted by an
  alert [4]. Just *noticing* the reflex before you act on it is enough to hand some gaps back to
  your own thoughts.
- **Let yourself be bored on purpose, occasionally.** You don't have to seek out tedium — but
  when a dull minute arrives, try sitting with it instead of swiping it away. Tolerating boredom
  tends to make you less prone to it, not more [4].
- **Keep it proportionate.** This isn't digital abstinence. Scroll, ask, and enjoy the tools —
  just ring-fence a little quiet so the brain gets its downtime.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "Downtime is wasted time — if there's a spare minute, I should fill it with
> something productive, like asking the AI one more thing."
> **Reality:** The brain treats undemanding downtime as *working* time. When external input
> drops, the default mode network comes online and supports the memory replay that consolidates
> what you've learned, and the mind-wandering that connects distant ideas into new ones
> [1][2]. A 2025 experiment found people who mind-wandered more during a break wrote more
> creatively afterward — and that deliberately *grinding* on the problem during the break did
> not help; drifting did [1]. Being honest about the flip side: the evidence that *filling*
> gaps with a phone actively harms cognition is weak and mixed [5][6], so this isn't a scare
> story about screens. It's the positive case — some of your most valuable thinking is
> scheduled for the moments you're now most tempted to fill, so it's worth leaving a few of
> them empty on purpose.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **The "harm" case is weak; lead with the "benefit" case.** The cleanest experiments looking
  for damage from filling breaks with a phone found **null results** [5], and correlational work
  finds at most tiny links between phone use and creativity [6]. Do not overstate: "AI/phones
  destroy focus" is not what these data show. The robust finding is the *positive* one —
  downtime and mind-wandering support consolidation and incubation [1][2].
- **Mind-wandering is not uniformly good.** The same drifting that helps creative incubation
  *hurts* when you need sustained, on-task attention (reading, driving, careful analysis), and a
  wandering mind is, on average, a slightly *less happy* one in in-the-moment studies. The claim
  here is narrow: undemanding **breaks** benefit from mind-wandering — not that you should let
  your mind wander through everything.
- **Effect sizes are modest and context-bound.** The incubation-creativity link was **small**,
  emerged mainly when people returned to the *same* problem after the break (not a new one), and
  came from a single online study (N = 200, Prolific) using a writing task [1]. Treat it as
  suggestive and directional, not a formula.
- **Reviews summarise, they don't prove.** Joshi et al. and Tam are **synthesis** pieces
  [2][4]; they marshal converging evidence and mechanisms (much of it neuroimaging and
  correlational) but are not themselves causal tests. The DMN-consolidation story, in
  particular, is an active, still-developing model.
- **Mark's attention-span figure is a real-world average, not a verdict on any individual.**
  The ~47-second number is a striking population trend from field measurement [3]; it doesn't
  mean any one person is incapable of sustained focus, and it predates today's AI tools.
- **Individual differences are large.** Benefits of downtime and costs of switching vary by
  person, task, and mood. Use these as defaults to experiment with, not universal laws.
