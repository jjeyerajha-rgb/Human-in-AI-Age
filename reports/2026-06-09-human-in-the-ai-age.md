## 1. Executive Summary

The fastest-growing risk of the AI era is not that machines think too well — it is
that, by quietly handing our thinking to them, **we practise our own less.** A
converging body of work in 2025–26 — an EEG study from MIT, a 666-person study in
*Societies*, a 319-worker survey from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, and the
practical guidance of figures like Cal Newport, Maryanne Wolf, and Ethan Mollick —
points to the same conclusion: **passive, uncritical AI use is associated with weaker
critical thinking and reduced cognitive engagement, while active, critical use is
not.** The mechanism has a name — *cognitive offloading* — and so does its compounding
cost: *cognitive debt.*

**The single most useful habit this week:** do your own thinking *first*. Write the
first rough draft, form your own answer, or sketch your own plan **before** you open
an AI tool — then use AI to challenge, extend, and pressure-test it. The studies are
consistent: people who think *then* prompt keep their cognitive engagement; people who
prompt *then* (maybe) think, lose it.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

AI has moved from a chat window you visit to an *ambient* layer inside your inbox,
your IDE, your browser, and your phone. The convenience is real and worth having. But
convenience is exactly the channel through which skills atrophy: every task you hand
over is a rep your brain doesn't do. Cal Newport's reframing for 2026 is blunt — his
worry is no longer whether you have *time* for deep work, but whether you *can still
do it at all* after a decade of attention erosion now accelerated by AI shortcuts.
([Cal Newport, *In Defense of Thinking*][cn1])

The good news from the same research: the damage is not in the *tool*, it is in the
*pattern of use*. Change the pattern and you keep the benefits without paying the
cognitive debt. This brief synthesises what leading neuroscientists, behavioural
scientists, AI researchers, and academics actually recommend.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Nataliya Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab)** — *Your Brain on ChatGPT* (June 2025).
  54 participants wrote essays under three conditions (Brain-only / Search engine /
  LLM) while wearing EEG caps. **Brain connectivity scaled *down* with the amount of
  external help**: brain-only writers showed the strongest, most distributed neural
  networks; LLM users the weakest, especially in alpha/theta bands tied to deep
  learning. In a 4th session, those switched *from* LLM *to* unaided writing showed
  lingering under-engagement (alpha/beta) — the authors' "cognitive debt." LLM users
  also reported low *ownership* of their essays and often **couldn't quote work they'd
  written minutes earlier.** *(Important: still a preprint, not peer-reviewed — see
  §7.)* ([MIT Media Lab][mit1]; [arXiv 2506.08872][mit2])

- **Maryanne Wolf (UCLA, cognitive neuroscientist)** — argues AI summarisation
  threatens the brain's **"deep-reading circuit"** that underpins inference, critical
  analysis, empathy, and reflection. Her prescription: protect daily, sustained
  reading of long, difficult texts — including texts you *could* have asked an AI to
  summarise — to keep that circuit strong. ([UCLA SEIS][wolf1])

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Michael Gerlich (SBS Swiss Business School)** — *AI Tools in Society*, *Societies*
  (Jan 2025), 666 participants, mixed methods. Found a **significant negative
  correlation between frequent AI use and critical-thinking scores, mediated by
  cognitive offloading** (the indirect effect via offloading was statistically
  significant). Younger users were more dependent and scored lower; higher education
  buffered but did **not eliminate** the effect. ([*Societies* 15(1):6][ger1];
  [PsyPost summary][ger2])

- **Cal Newport (Georgetown CS; author, *Deep Work*)** — calls for a **"cognitive
  fitness" revolution** to resist tools that degrade thinking. His five-part routine:
  (1) **read** 15–50 pages of hard material daily; (2) treat **writing as resistance
  training** — push through the first uncomfortable 10 minutes; (3) take **phone-free
  thinking walks**; (4) **leave your phone plugged in** in one spot at home rather than
  carrying it; (5) **learn a hard skill** (instrument, craft, sport) with measurable
  progress. On AI specifically: stop vague "just use AI" mandates and integrate it
  only where it makes you *smarter, not just busier.*
  ([Cal Newport / NYT][cn1]; [Deep Questions summary][cn2])

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton; author, *Co-Intelligence*)** — keep a **human in the loop
  at the stages that matter**, especially final decisions. Treat AI like a capable
  teammate you *manage*: give it a clear brief, set check-in/approval gates, define
  what "good" looks like, and verify outputs against your own expertise. His framing of
  the new scarce skill: *"knowing what to ask for"* and being able to recognise good
  work. Focus AI on what you *don't* want to do, and keep the human-defining work.
  ([Big Think — four principles][mol1]; [Mollick, *Management as AI superpower*][mol2])

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al. (Microsoft Research + Carnegie Mellon)** — CHI
  2025 survey of **319 knowledge workers / 936 real tasks**. Key result: **higher
  confidence in the AI predicts *less* critical thinking; higher confidence in
  *yourself* predicts *more*.** AI doesn't remove critical thinking so much as **shift
  it** — from generating work toward **verifying, integrating, and stewarding** it.
  Practical implication: build verification back into your workflow, and don't let
  trust in the tool replace your own judgment. ([Microsoft Research][ms1]; [CHI '25 /
  ACM][ms2])

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Think first, prompt second.** Draft your own answer/outline before opening any AI
  tool; then use AI to critique and extend it. (Counters the MIT "prompt-first"
  cognitive-debt pattern.)
- **Make AI argue against you.** Ask it for the strongest case *against* your view,
  the errors in its own answer, and its sources — then verify the sources yourself.
- **Keep a verification gate.** For anything consequential, treat AI output as a draft
  by a fast junior colleague: check facts, numbers, and logic before you ship.
- **Delegate like a manager, not a button-pusher.** Write a clear brief, set explicit
  check-in points, define "done," and stay accountable for the result (Mollick).
- **Protect one daily block of AI-free deep work** for the hardest thinking task.

### In personal life
- **Read something long and hard, on paper, most days** (Wolf, Newport). Don't
  outsource the books that change how you think.
- **Phone out of the bedroom; one charging spot at home.** Reduce ambient pulls on
  attention (Newport).
- **Take a phone-free walk** to let ideas form without input.
- **Do some everyday cognition by hand:** navigate without GPS occasionally, do mental
  arithmetic, recall before you search.
- **Notice "attachment," not just attention.** If an AI/app simulates a relationship
  (memory, persona, voice), add deliberate friction and time limits.

## 6. Keep-Your-Brain-On Routine (one page)

- **Daily:** 20–30 min deep reading (paper); one think-first task done before any AI;
  recall-before-search habit.
- **Weekly:** one phone-free thinking walk; one piece of real writing (journal,
  memo, post) drafted unaided; review one AI-assisted decision and ask "what did *I*
  actually contribute?"
- **Always-on guardrails:** verification gate for consequential AI output; phone in
  one spot at home; AI pointed at chores, not at your core judgment.

## 7. The Balanced View (what critics say)

- The **MIT EEG study is a preprint and has been criticised** for its small sample,
  heavy multiple-comparison testing, and the assumption that *lower* EEG connectivity
  means *worse* cognition rather than a *different* (possibly more efficient) strategy.
  The authors themselves caution against reading it as proof of "brain damage."
  ([MIT overview note][mit3])
- Correlational findings (Gerlich; the Microsoft survey is partly self-reported)
  **show association, not proof of causation** — reverse causation (people with weaker
  critical-thinking habits lean on AI more) is plausible.
- The *same* research points to an optimistic reading: **critical, active use shows no
  such decline**, and AI can deepen thinking when it shifts effort toward verification
  and synthesis. The problem is the *habit*, not the *tool*.
