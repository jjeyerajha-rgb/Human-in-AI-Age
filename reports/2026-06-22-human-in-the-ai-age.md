## 1. Executive Summary

The defining risk of the AI era is not that machines think too well — it is that, by
handing our thinking to them, **we practise our own thinking less.** A converging body
of recent work — an EEG study from the MIT Media Lab, a 666-person study in *Societies*,
a 319-worker survey from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, and a ~1,000-person
randomized trial from MIT and OpenAI — points to one conclusion: **passive, uncritical
AI use is associated with weaker critical thinking and reduced cognitive engagement;
active, critical use is not.** The mechanism has a name — *cognitive offloading* — and
so does its compounding cost — *cognitive debt.*

**The single habit to start this week:** *think first, prompt second.* Write your own
rough draft, form your own answer, or sketch your own plan **before** you open an AI
tool — then use AI to challenge, extend, and pressure-test it. The research is
consistent: people who think *then* prompt keep their cognitive engagement; people who
prompt *then* (maybe) think tend to lose it.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

AI has moved from a chat window you visit to an *ambient* layer inside your inbox, your
editor, your browser, and your phone. The convenience is real and worth having — but
convenience is exactly the channel through which skills quietly atrophy: every task you
fully hand over is a repetition your brain doesn't do.

The encouraging news from the same research is that the cost lives in the *pattern of
use*, not in the tool itself. Used as an oracle that hands you finished answers, AI is
linked to weaker engagement and recall. Used as a sparring partner that you direct,
interrogate, and verify, it can free attention for deeper synthesis. This brief
synthesizes what leading neuroscientists, behavioural scientists, AI researchers, and
academics actually recommend — and turns it into habits you can start now.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Nataliya Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab)** — *Your Brain on ChatGPT* (June 2025).
  54 participants wrote essays in three conditions (Brain-only / Search engine / LLM)
  while wearing EEG caps, across multiple sessions. **Brain connectivity scaled *down*
  with the amount of external help:** brain-only writers showed the strongest, most
  distributed networks; LLM users the weakest coupling (reported reductions in dDTF
  connectivity of up to ~55% versus baseline). In a later session, those moved *from*
  LLM *to* unaided writing showed lingering under-engagement (alpha/beta bands) — the
  authors' **"cognitive debt."** LLM users also reported low *ownership* of their essays
  and frequently **couldn't quote work they'd written minutes earlier.** Crucially, the
  reverse order — write unaided *first*, then bring in AI — showed *higher* connectivity
  and better recall. *(Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed — see §7.)* [1]

- **Maryanne Wolf (UCLA, cognitive neuroscientist)** — warns that over-relying on AI
  summarisation threatens the brain's **"deep-reading circuit"** that underpins
  inference, critical analysis, empathy, and reflection. Her prescription: protect
  daily, sustained reading of long, difficult texts — including texts you *could* have
  asked an AI to summarise — and teach young readers "not to over-rely on external
  information." [5]

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Michael Gerlich (SBS Swiss Business School)** — *AI Tools in Society*, *Societies*
  (Jan 2025), 666 participants, mixed methods. Found a **significant negative
  correlation between frequent AI use and critical-thinking scores, mediated by
  cognitive offloading.** Younger users (17–25) were the most dependent and scored
  lowest; higher education buffered the effect but did **not** eliminate it. The author
  is explicit that this is **correlation, not proven causation.** [2]

- **"Assisted thinking" vs. "cognitive offloading"** (*Psychology Today*, 2026) — a
  useful distinction for daily life: *offloading* hands the whole mental task to the
  tool (and can weaken memory); *assisted thinking* keeps your brain in charge of
  analysis and judgment while the tool handles the mechanical parts. Practical rule of
  thumb offered: on complex projects, keep AI to a *minority* of the cognitive work, and
  track which tasks you did independently. [6]

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton; author, *Co-Intelligence* / *Co-Existence*)** — keep a
  **human in the loop at the stages that matter**, especially final decisions. Treat AI
  like a capable but uneven teammate you *manage*: give it a clear brief, tell it what
  role to play, verify its output against your own expertise. He stresses the **"jagged
  frontier"** — AI is superhuman at some tasks and surprisingly bad at others, and you
  often can't predict which in advance — so *blanket trust* and *blanket dismissal* are
  both wrong. The new scarce skill is judgment: knowing when to refuse AI's help, when
  to verify, and when to hand over the keys. [7]

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al. (Microsoft Research + Carnegie Mellon)** — CHI 2025
  survey of **319 knowledge workers / 936 real tasks**. Key result: **higher confidence
  in the AI predicts *less* critical thinking; higher confidence in *yourself* predicts
  *more*** (at a higher perceived effort). GenAI doesn't remove critical thinking so
  much as **shift** it — from *generating* work toward **verifying, integrating, and
  stewarding** it. The antidote they identify is domain expertise: build verification
  back into the workflow, and don't let trust in the tool replace your own judgment. [3]

- **Fang, Liu, Phang et al. (MIT Media Lab + OpenAI)** — a four-week **randomized
  controlled trial (n≈981, >300k messages)** on chatbot use and wellbeing. Across
  modalities, **higher daily usage correlated with greater loneliness, emotional
  dependence, and problematic use, and *lower* real-world socialisation.** A small
  minority of "power users" accounted for most of the affective use. The lesson for
  "staying sane": treat AI as a tool, not a confidant — and protect human relationships
  as the primary source of emotional support. [4]

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Think first, prompt second.** Draft your own answer or outline *before* opening any
  AI tool; then use AI to critique and extend it. (Directly counters the MIT
  "prompt-first" cognitive-debt pattern.)
- **Make AI argue against you.** Ask for the strongest case *against* your view, the
  flaws in its own answer, and its sources — then verify the sources yourself.
- **Adopt a "no-AI first pass" for hard problems.** Sketch the solution unaided, then let
  AI refine or challenge it. It costs ~10–15 extra minutes and preserves the skill.
- **Verify, integrate, steward.** Treat your job as quality-controlling AI output against
  external sources and your own expertise — the critical-thinking work doesn't
  disappear, it moves here.
- **Keep one AI-silent block per day** for deep work, brainstorming, or writing from
  scratch, so your baseline skills don't quietly erode.

### In personal life
- **Protect a daily deep-read.** Read long, difficult material you *could* have
  summarised with AI, to keep the deep-reading circuit strong.
- **Keep AI out of the confidant seat.** Use it for logistics and drafting, not as your
  main source of emotional support; invest the freed time in real relationships.
- **Watch the "can I explain it?" test.** If you can't restate an AI answer in your own
  words, you've offloaded too much — redo that part yourself.
- **Do hard things unaided sometimes** — mental math, navigation, a first draft, a
  recipe — to keep the cognitive "muscles" in use.
- **Notice power-user creep.** If daily chatbot time is climbing and human contact is
  shrinking, that's the pattern the MIT/OpenAI trial flagged. Reset the balance.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "AI rots your brain."
> **Reality:** The evidence is about *patterns of use*, not permanent damage. The same
> MIT data show that writing unaided *first* and then using AI was associated with
> *higher* engagement and recall. The tool is not the problem; uncritical, prompt-first
> dependence is. Used as a sparring partner you direct and verify, AI can support deeper
> work.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- The **MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT"** study is a **preprint** with a **small sample
  (n=54; 18 in the key session 4)**, in a **narrow task** (essay writing). EEG
  connectivity is an indirect proxy for cognition. Treat it as suggestive, not settled.
- The **Gerlich** study reports **correlation, not causation**; reverse causation
  (lower critical thinkers prefer AI) and confounders are plausible. The author says so.
- The **Microsoft/CMU** study is **self-reported** survey data, with the usual recall
  and social-desirability limits.
- The **MIT/OpenAI** trial found *no significant effect of the assigned conditions*;
  the worse outcomes were among people who *chose* to use the chatbot more, so direction
  of causation is uncertain (lonelier people may simply use chatbots more).
- Net: the signal across very different methods is consistent enough to act on, but no
  single study here is definitive. Use the habits as low-risk hedges, not as proof.
