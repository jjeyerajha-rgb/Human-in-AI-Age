## 1. Executive Summary

The most important finding to land in 2026 is deceptively simple: **how you use AI, not
how often, decides what it does to your mind.** Theoretical neuroscientist Vivienne Ming
puts it bluntly — the danger is not the tool but *chronic substitution*, quietly handing
over the mental work that builds your **cognitive reserve** (the brain's lifelong buffer
against ageing and decline). When you stop doing the thinking because something will do
it for you, you stop building the reserve that protects you later. The converging
mechanism across this year's research has a name — **cognitive offloading** — and the
antidote also has one: **productive friction.**

**The single habit to start this week:** *do the hard 10 minutes first.* Before you open
any AI tool, spend ten minutes working the problem yourself — a rough draft, a first
guess, a sketch of the approach — *then* bring AI in to challenge, extend, and
pressure-test it. The friction is not a bug; it is the workout. People who think *then*
prompt keep their edge; people who prompt *then* (maybe) think slowly lose it.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

AI is no longer a place you visit; it is an *ambient* layer inside your inbox, editor,
browser, and phone, increasingly able to complete whole tasks on its own. The
convenience is real and worth having — but convenience is exactly the channel through
which skills atrophy, because every task you fully hand over is a repetition your brain
no longer does. This year the conversation matured from "does AI hurt thinking?" to a
sharper, more useful question: *which patterns of use protect the human, and which ones
hollow us out?*

The encouraging news from the same body of work is that the cost lives in the pattern,
not in the technology. Used as an oracle that hands you finished answers, AI is linked to
weaker engagement, recall, and critical thinking. Used as a sparring partner you direct,
interrogate, and verify, it can free attention for deeper synthesis — and may even raise
engagement when you do your own thinking first. This brief synthesizes what leading
neuroscientists, behavioural scientists, AI researchers, and academics actually
recommend, and turns it into habits you can start now.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Vivienne Ming (theoretical neuroscientist; chief scientist, The Possibility
  Institute; founder, Socos Labs)** — argues in 2026 that AI's deepest risk is not job
  loss but *passive use* that erodes **cognitive reserve**. "My worry is the cumulative
  impact of chronic substitution: when you stop doing the cognitive work because
  something will do it for you, you stop building the reserve that protects you later."
  Her memorable shorthand — **"GPT is the new GPS"** — recalls how satnav reliance is
  linked to weaker spatial memory; she fears the same for general reasoning, especially
  in the young, whose habits are still forming. Her rule: **"How you use AI, not how
  often, will determine its impact"** — use it *inquisitively*, challenge it, and keep
  your hippocampus and prefrontal networks in the game. She is careful that no biomarker
  study links AI use to dementia; today's data are correlational or short-term. [1][2]

- **Adam Green (professor of neuroscience, Georgetown; director, Laboratory for
  Relational Cognition)** — "There's plenty of evidence that if you are not doing as much
  of the actual thinking, then your capability to do that kind of thinking is going to
  atrophy." His prescription is **productive friction**: do things the slow way on
  purpose, sit with a hard problem before asking a bot, take notes by hand, and let AI
  *pressure-test* a view you formed yourself rather than supply the view. [3]

- **Maryanne Wolf (UCLA, cognitive neuroscientist)** — warns that over-relying on AI
  summarisation threatens the brain's **"deep-reading circuit"** that underpins
  inference, critical analysis, empathy, and reflection. Her prescription: protect daily,
  sustained reading of long, difficult texts — including ones you *could* have asked AI
  to summarise. [7]

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Michael Gerlich (SBS Swiss Business School)** — *AI Tools in Society*, *Societies*
  (Jan 2025), 666 participants, mixed methods. Found a **significant negative correlation
  between frequent AI use and critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive
  offloading.** Younger users (17–25) were the most dependent and scored lowest; higher
  education buffered the effect but did **not** eliminate it. Notably, the relationship
  looks **non-linear** — *moderate* use may be manageable, with costs accelerating beyond
  a point. The author is explicit that this is **correlation, not proven causation.** [4]

- **"Desirable difficulties" (Robert & Elizabeth Bjork, applied to AI use)** — decades of
  learning science show that effortful retrieval, generating an answer before seeing it
  (the **generation effect**), and spacing make learning *stick*. AI's frictionless
  answers remove exactly these difficulties. Practitioner protocols like the **"No-AI
  Hour"** operationalise this: a dedicated weekly (ideally 3–4×/week) block of working
  *without* AI to rebuild decomposition, retrieval, and judgment — the skills that erode
  fastest when AI hands you complete solutions. [8]

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton; author, *Co-Intelligence*)** — keep a **human in the loop at
  the stages that matter**, especially final decisions. Treat AI like a capable but
  uneven teammate you *manage*: give it a clear brief, tell it what role to play, and
  verify its output against your own expertise. He stresses the **"jagged frontier"** —
  AI is superhuman at some tasks and surprisingly bad at others, often unpredictably — so
  both blanket trust and blanket dismissal are wrong. The new scarce skill is *judgment*:
  knowing when to refuse AI's help, when to verify, and when to hand over the keys. [6]

- **"The Augmentation Trap" (arXiv, 2026)** — an economic model of AI use at work shows
  that productivity gains can be *front-loaded* while skills quietly erode, so a rational
  manager may adopt AI even when it lowers long-run output. Its sharpest warning: in
  tasks where AI output depends little on the worker's expertise, **experienced workers
  keep developing while novices can "deskill to zero,"** widening the gap over time. The
  lesson: deliberately protect skill-building for early-career people, don't let AI
  shortcut the apprenticeship. [5]

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch et al. (Microsoft Research + Carnegie Mellon)** — CHI 2025
  survey of **319 knowledge workers / 936 real tasks**. Key result: **higher confidence
  in the AI predicts *less* critical thinking; higher confidence in *yourself* predicts
  *more*.** GenAI doesn't remove critical thinking so much as **shift** it — from
  *generating* work toward **verifying, integrating, and stewarding** it. The antidote
  they identify is domain expertise: build verification back into the workflow. [9]

- **"Brainrot: Deskilling and Addiction are Overlooked AI Risks" (arXiv, 2026)** — argues
  that mainstream AI safety work has largely ignored *cognitive* harms — deskilling from
  offloading and dependence/attachment to AI systems. The authors call for treating these
  as first-class safety concerns, addressable through **AI-literacy information campaigns
  and sensible regulation**, not just model-level guardrails. A useful signal that
  "keeping humans sharp" is moving from op-ed territory into the safety research agenda. [10]

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Do the hard 10 minutes first.** Form your own rough answer, outline, or solution
  *before* opening any AI tool; then use AI to critique and extend it. Counters the
  "prompt-first" pattern and keeps your reasoning circuits in use.
- **Make AI argue against you.** Ask for the strongest case *against* your view, the
  flaws in its own answer, and its sources — then verify the sources yourself.
- **Run a weekly "No-AI Hour."** Pick one real task slightly below your comfort level and
  do it unaided to rebuild decomposition, retrieval, and judgment. 3–4×/week if you feel
  rusty.
- **Verify, integrate, steward.** Treat your job as quality-controlling AI output against
  external sources and your own expertise — the critical-thinking work doesn't disappear,
  it moves here.
- **Protect the apprenticeship.** If you manage juniors, deliberately reserve some
  skill-building tasks for them to do without AI, so they don't "deskill to zero."

### In personal life
- **Keep your internal GPS on.** Sometimes navigate, calculate, recall, and draft
  unaided — "GPT is the new GPS," and the route you never drive yourself is the one you
  never learn.
- **Protect a daily deep-read.** Read long, difficult material you *could* have
  summarised with AI, to keep the deep-reading circuit strong.
- **Use the "can I explain it?" test.** If you can't restate an AI answer in your own
  words, you've offloaded too much — redo that part yourself.
- **Take notes by hand and let AI quiz you.** Friction makes it stick; ask AI for
  flashcards rather than finished conclusions.
- **Keep AI out of the confidant seat.** Use it for logistics and drafting, not as your
  main source of emotional support; invest the freed time in real relationships.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "Using AI will rot your brain / give you dementia."
> **Reality:** No study links AI use to dementia pathology, and today's cognitive data
> are correlational or short-term. The real, evidence-based concern is *chronic
> substitution* — habitually outsourcing the mental effort that builds cognitive reserve.
> The fix is not abstinence but *pattern*: use AI inquisitively, do the hard part
> yourself sometimes, and the same tool that could dull you can instead free attention
> for deeper work.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Ming's warning is expert commentary, not a new dataset.** She explicitly notes no
  biomarker study links AI use to dementia, and most current evidence is correlational or
  short-term. Treat it as a well-reasoned hypothesis to act on cautiously, not a proven
  causal chain.
- **The Gerlich study is correlational** and **non-linear**; reverse causation (weaker
  critical thinkers prefer AI) and confounders are plausible. The author says so.
- **"The Augmentation Trap" and "Brainrot" are preprints** — a theoretical model and a
  position paper, respectively. They frame risks and incentives well but do not, by
  themselves, measure real-world skill loss.
- **The Microsoft/CMU study is self-reported** survey data, with the usual recall and
  social-desirability limits.
- **Net:** the signal across very different methods and disciplines is consistent enough
  to act on, but no single source here is definitive. Use the habits as low-risk hedges
  that also happen to make you better at your work — not as proof of harm.
