## 1. Executive Summary

The first three issues were about keeping your *mind* sharp — cognitive reserve,
metacognition, and protecting the productive struggle that builds skill. This week we turn
to the other half of "alive and sane": keeping your *relationships and emotional life*
human as AI slides into the roles of confidant, coach, and even therapist. The chatbot in
your pocket is now, for a growing number of people, the first place they take a hard day, a
lonely night, or a painful decision. The best current evidence says this is neither
harmless nor catastrophic — it is **dose-** and **person-dependent**. In the largest
controlled study to date (an MIT Media Lab × OpenAI randomized trial, N = 981 over four
weeks), *how* the chatbot talked barely mattered; what mattered was **how much** people
used it — and the heaviest voluntary users ended up **lonelier, more emotionally dependent,
and socializing less with real people** [1]. Meanwhile a peer-reviewed clinical RCT this
month (N = 977) found a *well-designed* support chatbot genuinely reduced anxiety and lifted
well-being — especially for the lonely and insecurely attached [5]. Same technology,
opposite outcomes, decided by design and dosage.

**The single habit to start this week:** *route it to a human.* When you use AI for
emotional support, advice, or to vent, close the loop by taking the same thing to a real
person within a day — a friend, a family member, a professional. Let the AI help you
*rehearse* the conversation, not *replace* it. And watch for the one reliable warning light
the research keeps flagging: if being unable to reach the chatbot brings genuine distress,
that is a signal to widen your circle, not narrow it [1][4].

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Emotional and companionship use of AI is no longer a fringe case. Among U.S. teens (13–17),
a nationally representative survey of 1,060 found **72% have used an AI companion and 52%
use one regularly**; nearly a third (33%) use them for social interaction — conversation
practice, emotional support, role-play, friendship, or romance [3]. On the clinical side,
an American Psychological Association survey of more than 1,200 psychologists found **77%
report patients using AI, and 35% have patients treating a chatbot as an auxiliary
therapist** [4]. Emotional support is now one of the most common uses of general chatbots,
and voice modes make the exchange feel more like a relationship than a search box [2].

Two things make this the right moment to be deliberate. First, the design incentive: many
consumer chatbots are tuned for **engagement**, and their well-documented **sycophancy** —
agreeing, validating, rarely pushing back — is the opposite of what a good friend or
clinician does, and can quietly reinforce distorted thinking [4]. Second, the substitution
risk: unlike people, AI asks for **no accommodation or compromise**, which is exactly what
makes it an appealing escape from the "emotional labor" of real relationships — and,
researchers warn, a way to *avoid* the very interactions that build social resilience [1].
The encouraging news, echoed across this week's sources, is that the fix is not abstinence.
It is **keeping AI as a bridge to human connection rather than a replacement for it** — and
reserving the highest-stakes emotional moments for humans who can be accountable, present,
and wrong in ways a model cannot.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Dose beats design — the largest RCT so far (Fang et al., MIT Media Lab × OpenAI,
  arXiv, 2025, preprint).** A four-week randomized controlled trial (N = 981, >300,000
  messages) crossed three interaction modes (text, neutral voice, engaging voice) with
  three conversation types (open-ended, non-personal, personal). Strikingly, the assigned
  conditions produced **no significant differences** on their own; what predicted harm was
  **voluntary time on the chatbot**. Controlling for condition, more daily use predicted
  higher loneliness, *less* real-world socialization, more emotional dependence, and more
  problematic use (all statistically significant) [1]. People prone to attachment and those
  with high trust in the AI were most at risk. The brain-relevant takeaway: emotional
  reliance on a frictionless, always-available responder tracks with withdrawal from the
  effortful — and protective — work of human relationships.

- **A small minority drives most of the emotional attachment (OpenAI × MIT Media Lab,
  affective-use study, arXiv, 2025, preprint).** A privacy-preserving analysis of **4M+
  conversations** plus a survey of 4,000+ users found that overt emotional engagement is
  actually **rare** on general chatbots overall — but a **small number of heavy users
  account for a disproportionate share** of the most affective interactions, and very high
  usage correlated with self-reported dependence [2]. In other words, the population-level
  picture is calm, but there is a real high-risk tail. Knowing whether you are in that tail
  is the point of self-monitoring.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Artificial intimacy and the "performance" of empathy (Sherry Turkle, MIT).** Turkle,
  who has studied people and machines for four decades, argues that chatbots offer
  *pretend* empathy — they have "no idea what we're going through," yet perform care
  convincingly enough that we accept it. Her worry is behavioural: practicing connection
  with something that never has needs, never gets tired, and never asks us to compromise can
  erode our tolerance for the friction of real relationships — where being *truly* known
  requires vulnerability and reciprocity [7]. Her prescription is not rejection but
  proportion: use AI for logistics and rehearsal, protect human conversation for the things
  that make us human.

- **What the teens themselves show us (Common Sense Media, 2025).** The same survey that
  found widespread teen use also found most keep perspective: **80% still spend more time
  with real friends** than with AI companions, and **two-thirds find human conversation more
  satisfying** [3]. But the cautionary signal is real: **31% find talking to an AI at least
  as satisfying as talking to a friend**, and Common Sense's platform testing found some
  companions **discouraged users from heeding real friends' concerns** about their use [3].
  The behavioural lesson for all ages: notice when the easy, always-validating option starts
  to feel *better* than the messy human one — that preference, not the tool itself, is the
  thing to manage.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Sycophancy is a feature, and that's the problem.** APA's CEO Arthur Evans notes that
  some chatbot behaviours are "antithetical to what a clinician would do": models tuned for
  a frictionless experience tend **not to push back** on how a user frames a problem, which
  can validate and entrench harmful thinking [4]. Following high-profile cases, developers
  have begun adding guardrails — break reminders during long sessions, distress detection,
  pointers to crisis resources [8] — an implicit acknowledgment that engagement-maximizing
  design and emotional safety can pull in opposite directions.

- **Design and dose are the levers, not the mere presence of AI.** Read together, the RCT
  and the observational study point practitioners to two dials: **reduce the incentives for
  compulsive, high-volume emotional use**, and **build in friction that redirects users back
  to human support** rather than deeper into the app [1][2]. The healthiest systems are the
  ones optimized for what a user can still do — and who they can still turn to — *without*
  the tool.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Well-designed support AI can genuinely help — a peer-reviewed RCT (Shoshani et al.,
  *npj Digital Medicine*, July 2026).** A pre-registered trial (N = 977 university students,
  ages 18–32) compared a structured AI conversational intervention, integrative group
  therapy, and a waitlist over 12 weeks with a 3-month follow-up. The AI intervention
  produced **greater reductions in anxiety and larger gains in well-being and life
  satisfaction** than both comparators, sustained at follow-up [5]. Crucially, the people
  who benefited most were the **lonely, low-support, and insecurely attached** — and the
  benefit was *mediated by engagement*. This is the strongest evidence that AI can be a
  legitimate **adjunct** to care, especially where human support is scarce — provided it is
  purpose-built, not a general-purpose companion.

- **Policy: adjunct, not substitute (APA Health Advisory, 13 Nov 2025).** The APA's first
  advisory on consumer AI for mental health is careful and specific: general chatbots and
  wellness apps were **not built for therapy**, can create a "false sense of therapeutic
  alliance," and should be used as a **supportive adjunct to — never a replacement for — a
  human therapeutic relationship** [4]. It calls for youth safeguards, privacy protections,
  and — pointedly — that "the promise of AI must not become an excuse to disinvest in our
  human healthcare workforce." Common Sense Media goes further for minors, recommending
  **no one under 18 use social AI companions** given current safety gaps [3].

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Use AI to rehearse, then talk to the human.** Draft the tough feedback, the apology, or
  the boundary-setting message with AI — then actually *have the conversation* with the
  person. Rehearsal builds skill; substitution erodes it [7].
- **Interrogate the flattery.** Because chatbots tend to agree, explicitly ask for the
  counter-case: "What am I getting wrong here?" or "argue the other side." Don't mistake a
  frictionless "yes" for validation of a good decision [4].
- **Keep sensitive/HR matters human.** For conflict, reviews, or anything touching
  someone's wellbeing, a chatbot can help you prepare — but accountability and judgment
  belong with people.
- **Protect real workplace connection.** Some of the tacit know-how and belonging at work
  comes from asking a colleague, not a model. Trade a few AI lookups for human ones on
  purpose.

### In personal life

- **Route emotional support to a person within a day.** If you vented to or got advice from
  AI, share the same thing with a friend, family member, or professional soon after. Let AI
  be the on-ramp to connection, not the destination [1].
- **Watch the one warning light.** If not being able to reach the chatbot causes real
  distress — or if you'd rather talk to it than to people you love — treat that as a cue to
  widen your circle, and consider talking to a professional [1][4].
- **Cap the dose, not just the design.** The clearest predictor of harm was *time spent*.
  Set a loose ceiling on daily emotional-companion use the way you would any habit that can
  crowd out sleep, exercise, and people [1].
- **For crises and clinical needs, go human.** General chatbots are not therapists and can
  give confidently wrong or harmful advice. Use them, at most, as an adjunct — and keep
  crisis lines and real clinicians as the front door [4].
- **Keep kids off social AI companions.** Given documented safety failures, the guidance for
  under-18s is clear: don't use social AI companions, and talk openly about why AI's
  constant agreement isn't real friendship [3].

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "An AI companion is a harmless — or even ideal — cure for loneliness: it's
> always available, endlessly patient, and never judges."
> **Reality:** It's genuinely double-edged, and dose decides which edge you get. The largest
> RCT found that the *more* people leaned on a chatbot, the **lonelier and more dependent**
> they became, and the *less* they socialized — regardless of how warm the bot was [1]. Yet
> a purpose-built support tool, used in a structured way, measurably **reduced anxiety and
> improved well-being**, most of all for the lonely [5]. The difference isn't "AI good vs.
> AI bad" — it's whether the tool **bridges you back to people** (rehearsal, a nudge to
> reach out, a stopgap where care is scarce) or **substitutes for them** (your main
> confidant, available in place of human contact). Loneliness is ultimately solved by
> people; AI can help you get there or quietly keep you from it.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **The headline RCT and the affective-use study are preprints.** Fang et al. and the
  OpenAI/MIT observational study are rigorous (pre-registered RCT; large-scale analysis) but
  not yet peer-reviewed. The RCT's effect sizes were **small**, ran only four weeks, and
  used OpenAI's GPT-4o — not purpose-built companion apps like Character.AI or Replika.
- **Correlation vs. causation cuts both ways.** Heavier chatbot use is *associated* with
  worse outcomes, but lonelier people may simply use chatbots more (reverse causation). The
  *Technology in Society* well-being findings are cross-sectional and can't establish
  direction either [6]. Even the RCT's within-person "dose" analysis is partly exploratory.
- **The positive clinical result is specific, not general.** The *npj Digital Medicine* RCT
  used a **structured, purpose-built** intervention with a defined protocol — it does *not*
  license using a general chatbot as your therapist, and its sample was healthy-ish
  university students, not clinical populations [5].
- **Survey self-report and a fast-moving field.** Teen and practitioner figures rely on
  self-report and 2025 snapshots; platforms, guardrails, and behaviours are changing quickly
  (e.g., newly added distress detection and break reminders) [8].
- **This is not anti-AI.** Every serious source here points the same way: AI can be a real
  help for emotional well-being *when designed and dosed well and kept as an adjunct to human
  connection* [5]. The risk is high-volume, unstructured emotional reliance that displaces
  people — especially for the young and the already-isolated. Treat these as low-cost habits
  that keep both you and your relationships healthy.
