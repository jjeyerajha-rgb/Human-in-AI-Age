## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 009 asked you to *draft it before you delegate it* — protecting
ownership, self-efficacy, and the felt sense that the work is *yours*.
Issue 006 asked you to *generate before you store* — protecting memory
encoding. This week is the missing middle: **writing as thinking**. The
point of writing, for most of the work that still has to be done by a
human, is not a clean page. It is the moment when a claim you did not
have when you sat down becomes a sentence you can defend.

That is not a slogan. It is a cognitive-process claim with a 2026
classroom test. Xinran Chen, in an eight-week quasi-experiment with
**168** Chinese undergraduates writing English academic essays, compared
no-AI writing, **bounded** AI support (questions, alternatives, language
help — no complete paragraphs or ready-made arguments), and **open** AI
collaboration. While the tool was on, open collaboration produced the
best-looking essays (aggregated mean **4.02** vs **3.83** bounded vs
**3.57** no-AI). At Week 8, with the tool *removed*, the ordering
reversed. Bounded support led open collaboration on independent writing
quality (**+0.27**), higher-order thinking (**+0.35**), argument depth
(**+0.42**), and revision quality (**+0.39**). Open users asked nearly
twice as many prompts per task (**13.92** vs **7.58**), incorporated
**43%** of AI text versus **19%**, and generated only **56%** of the
final words themselves versus **78%**. The largest offloading gaps were
not grammar. They were **ideas** and **reasoning**. Chen’s own honesty
move: six intact classes, wild-cluster *p* values sitting on the
0.050–0.063 boundary, a bundled “boundary + compulsory reflection”
treatment. Treat it as a classroom pattern, not a verdict [1].

Peter Dalsgaard, writing for the European Association of Cognitive
Ergonomics in September 2026, names what the pattern costs. When writers
shift from generating text to evaluating and selecting model text, they
leave a **generation condition** and enter something closer to a reading
or response condition. He calls the missing property **cognitive
fidelity**: the degree to which the process still requires the writer to
originate, articulate, and reformulate rather than receive the emerging
prose [2]. That is this week’s object.

**The single habit to start this week:** *write until you surprise
yourself.* Before you ask a model for a draft, outline, or “make this
professional,” write one messy paragraph that contains a claim you did
not have when you sat down. Keep that sentence. Then the model may
polish, outline, or argue against it. Origination stays on your side.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Inbox copilots, slide generators, and “write this email” buttons have
made a finished page cheaper than a thought. That is useful for the
ceremonial document nobody will read. It is a poor trade for the
documents that are supposed to change what *you* know: the strategy
memo, the paper, the difficult message, the note that decides what you
believe. Linda Flower and John Hayes described writing in 1981 as a
recursive cognitive process — planning, translating, reviewing — not a
linear dump of pre-formed ideas [3]. David Galbraith later split the
work further: a **knowledge-transforming** process that arranges what
you already know for a reader, and a **knowledge-constituting** process
in which formulating language *synthesises* content you did not retrieve
as a finished idea [4]. The second process is the one a first-draft
button can skip.

Chen’s process log is the behavioural map of that skip. Open
collaboration did not merely polish. Students asked the model to
elaborate ideas (**19.8%** of prompts) and develop arguments (**18.5%**)
more often than the bounded group, and **24%** of their adoptions were
minimally transformed. Learner-led revision fell from **55%** (bounded)
to **27%** (open); AI-dominant revision rose from **11%** to **34%** [1].
The supported product improved. The independent thinker did not keep
pace. Salomon, Perkins, and Globerson already warned that effects *with*
a technology are not effects *of* it after the tool is gone [5].
Generative AI made that warning ordinary office equipment.

This is not “stop using writing tools.” Surface help — grammar,
vocabulary, a second pair of eyes on tone — can free working memory for
the claim. Chen’s bounded condition *beat* no-AI at Week 8. The question
is which layer you hand over. Hand over spelling. Keep origination.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Ronald Kellogg** (*Journal of Writing Research*, 2008) is the
  working-memory account. Expert writing is not transcription. It is
  **knowledge-crafting**: holding the author’s intended content, the text
  so far, and a model of the reader in working memory at once, while the
  central executive coordinates planning, sentence generation, and
  review. That orchestration takes more than two decades of practice to
  become fluent. The bottleneck is executive attention. Anything that
  automates the basic processes can *free* that attention — or, if it
  also automates the claim, leave the executive with nothing to
  coordinate but selection [6]. Inner speech, in Kellogg’s earlier
  model, is one of the transient stores that lets a writer hold a phrase
  while shaping the next. A model that supplies the phrase skips that
  loop.
- **Nataliya Kosmyna, Pattie Maes, and colleagues** (MIT Media Lab;
  arXiv:2506.08872, June 2025; **preprint**) remain the EEG
  corroboration, not this week’s headline. Fifty-four people wrote essays
  with an LLM, a search engine, or no tools; eighteen returned for a
  fourth session that swapped conditions. Brain-only writers showed the
  strongest, most distributed connectivity; LLM users the weakest. LLM
  users were also worst at quoting their own essays, and ownership
  ratings were lowest in that group. The useful Session-4 result, already
  used as a sequence finding in the pilot issue, is still the one to
  keep: people who wrote unaided *first* and then used the model showed
  more engagement than people who had been LLM-first [7]. Do not upgrade
  a 54-person preprint into “AI damages the brain.” It is a connectivity
  and recall pattern during a lab essay task.
- **Chen’s layer map** is the educational neuroscience-adjacent claim
  without the scanner. Idea and reasoning offloading (adopting claims,
  warrants, evidence readings, counterarguments) showed the largest
  bounded–open gaps (Hedges’ *g* = **2.14** and **2.22**). Surface
  offloading was smaller (*g* = **0.95**) [1]. That gradient is the
  practical neuro point: the operations you stop practising are the ones
  that later fail the no-AI hour.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf** (*Journal of Experimental
  Psychology: Human Learning and Memory*, 1978) named the **generation
  effect**: material you generate is remembered better than material you
  read. Issue 006 used that fact for notes and storage. This week uses it
  for prose. Dalsgaard’s argument is that AI-assisted writing often moves
  the writer from a generation condition into a read-and-select
  condition, which may lack the cognitive properties that made writing an
  epistemic tool in the first place [2][8].
- **Bereiter and Scardamalia** (1987) distinguished **knowledge-telling**
  (retrieving and dumping what you already know) from
  **knowledge-transforming** (using the writing problem to reorganise
  knowledge). Most first-draft buttons are excellent knowledge-tellers.
  They are indifferent to whether *you* transformed anything [9].
- **Galbraith and Baaijen** (*Educational Psychologist*, 2018) add the
  discovery piece. Increases in a writer’s own understanding after
  writing tracked two separable behaviours in keystroke work: global
  revision of structure, and spontaneous, disposition-guided sentence
  production — the messy paragraph in which the next sentence is not
  fully planned. Explicit outlining can improve the *text* and still
  block the implicit synthesis that constitutes new knowledge [4]. A
  model that hands you a tidy outline plus fluent paragraphs can raise
  product quality while starving both discovery routes.
- **Farrokhnia, Latifi, Papadopoulos, and colleagues**
  (*International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education*,
  10 February 2026; N = **70** fifth-semester students writing
  argumentative essays in Persian) compared teacher feedback with
  ChatGPT-4o feedback under zero-shot versus chain-of-thought prompts.
  CoT produced *higher-rated* feedback than both zero-shot and the
  teacher. That quality did **not** produce significantly better
  revisions. Teacher feedback, rated lower, yielded comparable gains in
  essay quality. Students improved in every arm; the between-group
  revision contrast was null. Feedback that looks better is not the same
  as thinking that got done. Uptake is the behaviour [10].

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Peter Dalsgaard** (Aarhus University; ECCE 2026, ACM) offers the
  design criterion. **Cognitive fidelity** has three dimensions:
  **origination** (did the writer generate the idea?), **articulation**
  (did they put it into words?), **reformulation** (did they recast it?).
  Tools should be scored on whether they require those operations, not
  only on whether the output is good, fast, or original-looking [2]. A
  “write this for me” control scores near zero on all three. A “here are
  three objections to *my* paragraph” control scores high on
  reformulation and leaves origination intact.
- **Ethan Mollick** (Wharton) is not a writing scientist, but he is one
  of the practitioners who still treats drafting as thinking. In
  *Co-Intelligence* and later notes he has been explicit that he writes
  chapter and post drafts himself, using models for feedback, unsticking,
  and reference-checking rather than for the first pass — because the
  first pass is where he finds out what he thinks, and because
  AI-authored first drafts anchor the rest of the work [11][12]. That is
  a workflow, not a randomised trial. It matches Chen’s bounded pattern
  and Dalsgaard’s origination test.
- **Richard S. Lewis** (*Looking for Trouble*, Springer, 2026) puts the
  practitioner’s felt shift in older language. Socrates worried that
  writing would give *readers* words without the author’s knowledge. AI
  writing tools risk giving *authors* words without knowledge. Selection
  is not generation. Lewis is a philosopher of technology, not an
  experimentalist; he leans on Kosmyna more heavily than the preprint
  can bear. Keep the distinction, drop the scare [13].

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Flower and Hayes** (1981) remain the process model the 2026 tools
  should be designed against: writing as goal-directed thinking with
  recursive planning, translating, and reviewing, not as transcription of
  a prior mental essay [3]. Yoshida, Kobayashi, Tateno, and Chen
  (arXiv:2608.30424, 31 August 2026; **preprint**, N = 21 across two
  within-subjects studies) try to build that model into a proactive
  co-writer. Early evidence that inferring *which cognitive process* the
  writer is in can raise engagement with suggestions. Small N, not a
  learning trial, useful as a design direction: support the process you
  are actually in, instead of replacing it [14].
- **Soderstrom and Bjork** (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*,
  2015) supply the assessment rule Chen used: performance during practice
  is not learning. Open collaboration won the homework. Bounded support
  won the closed-book hour [15][1].
- **L2 caution, and a policy implication.** Chen’s sample wrote in a
  second language. Surface offloading may be more defensible there than
  for a native-language strategy memo. The policy move that travels is
  still the same: assess at least one artefact without the tool if the
  course, job, or decision claims to certify *the person’s* judgement.
  That is not a ban. It is a measurement choice.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Originate the claim.** For any memo, update, or analysis that is
  supposed to be *your* judgement, write one paragraph that includes a
  sentence you could not have predicted ten minutes earlier. Then ask the
  model to tighten, outline, or steel-man it.
- **Bound the tool like Chen’s better arm.** Ask for questions,
  alternative outlines, “what is weak here?”, and language cleanup. Do
  not ask for the thesis, the full argument, the counterargument, or a
  rewrite of the whole page.
- **Keep a self-generated share.** If more than about a third of the
  sentences originated in the model, you are in Chen’s open-collaboration
  zone. Rewrite one paragraph from memory with the chat closed.
- **Separate polish from thought.** Grammar, tone, and slide layout are
  fair game. Warrants, evidence readings, and the decision sentence are
  not — unless you can restate them unaided.
- **Assess the person, not the copilot, at least once.** If a review, a
  hire, or a go/no-go depends on someone’s judgement, look at one
  artefact they produced without the model, or ask them to reconstruct
  the argument on the spot.

### In personal life

- **Journal before you prompt.** One messy paragraph about the day, the
  decision, or the feeling, *then* optional summarising or reframing.
- **Letters that matter.** Condolence, apology, love, a hard boundary:
  originate the uncomfortable sentence yourself. The model may check
  tone. It should not invent the feeling.
- **Keep the surprising sentence.** If nothing in the draft surprised
  you, you probably evaluated someone else’s text. Add one claim that
  cost you something to write.
- **Read your own words back.** If you cannot quote a sentence you
  “wrote” this afternoon, you are in Kosmyna’s recall pattern. That is a
  signal to generate the next one.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

**Myth:** “If I edit the AI draft thoroughly, I still did the thinking.
Writing is rewriting.”

**Reality:** Rewriting *your* sentences is reformulation — one of
Dalsgaard’s three fidelity dimensions, and one of Galbraith’s discovery
routes. Rewriting *the model’s* sentences is often evaluation and
selection. Lewis’s distinction is the right one: generating a claim and
approving a claim are not the same cognitive act [13][2]. Chen’s open
group revised; they just revised less of their own text, spent fewer
active drafting minutes (**40** vs **48**), and arrived at Week 8 with
thinner independent arguments [1]. Editing is real work. It is not a
substitute for origination. The habit is not “never edit a model.” It is
“do not let the first thought on the page be a thought you never had.”

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Chen is quasi-experimental.** Condition was assigned to six intact
  classes, not 168 people. Wild-cluster *p* values for the Week 8
  bounded–open contrasts sit between **0.050 and 0.063**. The author
  correctly refuses a clean causal boast. Bounded support bundled
  delegation limits *with* compulsory reflection; those cannot be
  separated. All writing was L2 English by Chinese undergraduates in one
  university, March–April 2026, GPT-5.3 in ordinary chat. Do not treat
  the means as a workplace RCT. Do treat the *divergence* — best product
  with the tool, better thinker without it — as the pattern to watch.
- **Open collaboration really did help the supported product.** Burying
  that would be a scare campaign. If the only goal is a fluent page
  tonight, open use won. This brief is about the competence that remains
  on Monday.
- **Kosmyna is a preprint**, N = 54 / 18, already the spine of the June
  2026 pilot. Connectivity is not “brain damage.” Quote-failure is a
  recall and ownership finding on a lab essay. Use it as corroboration
  of sequence (struggle, then tool), not as this week’s proof.
- **Dalsgaard is a framework paper**, not a trial. Cognitive fidelity is
  a useful scoring rubric. It is not a measured effect size.
- **Farrokhnia et al.** is a one-shot essay in Persian with a
  structural rubric. The abstract’s “graduate students” does not match
  the method section’s fifth-semester bachelor’s sample; we follow the
  method. The null on revision quality is the finding, not a hidden
  “AI makes you revise less” count. They did not code uptake at the
  accept / adapt / reject grain they themselves recommend for follow-up.
- **Galbraith’s discovery evidence** predates ChatGPT. It tells you
  *which human processes* are at risk; it does not measure GPT.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing here is a lesion, a
  longitudinal IQ drop, or a permanent change. The evidence is about
  *patterns of use*: which operations you still perform (originate,
  articulate, reformulate), and which you only approve.
