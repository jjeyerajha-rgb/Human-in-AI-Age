## 1. Executive Summary

Earlier issues asked what AI does to your *thinking* — your critical judgment, memory,
creativity, attention. This week asks something more personal: what AI does to your
**sense of yourself as the author of your work**. Not "can you still do the task?" but
"do you still feel it's *yours*, do you still believe *you* could do it, and do you still
care?"

That trio has names in psychology: **psychological ownership** (the felt sense that "this is
mine"), **self-efficacy** (the belief that "I can do this on my own"), and **intrinsic
motivation** (wanting to do it for its own sake). A newly published, **preregistered
experiment** in *Scientific Reports* put these under a controlled test. People did realistic
work tasks in one of three ways: no AI, **passive AI** (copy-and-paste the model's output), or
**active collaboration** (write your own draft first, *then* use AI to refine it). Passive use
measurably lowered self-efficacy, ownership, and the sense that the work was meaningful — and
strikingly, the dents in self-efficacy and meaning **lingered even after people went back to
working without AI**. Active collaboration showed **no such cost**: those participants looked
just like the people who used no AI at all [1].

Around that spine sits a fast-growing 2026 literature reaching the same conclusion from
different angles: in a study of university students, **AI *dependency* predicted "learned
helplessness"** — the belief that one's own effort doesn't drive outcomes — which in turn
predicted lower motivation [2]. In a co-creation experiment, higher AI **automation** eroded
people's felt ownership and their "sense of agency," with the most **skilled** participants
losing the most [3]. And — importantly for honesty — a separate experiment found AI support
*raised* motivation and confidence, especially for weaker students, when it was used as a
tutor rather than a ghostwriter [5]. The common thread is not *whether* you use AI; it's
**whether your own effort still visibly drives the result**.

**The single habit to start this week:** *draft it before you delegate it.* For anything you
want to feel is yours — an email that matters, a plan, a piece of analysis, a design —
write your own rough version *first*, then hand it to AI to sharpen. That one sequence
(what the experiment called **"First Human, Then AI"**) was enough to protect ownership,
confidence, and meaning while keeping almost all of the speed [1].

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The productivity case for AI is settled enough that many organisations now push staff to
"use AI for everything." The quiet risk in that instruction is that the *fastest* way to use
AI — paste the prompt, take the output — is also the mode that most erodes the human's
psychological connection to the work [1]. You can hit your numbers and, task by task,
stop feeling like the author of your own job.

Why care, if the output is fine? Because self-efficacy, ownership, and meaning aren't luxuries —
they're the fuel for persistence, learning, and initiative. When people stop believing their
effort is what produces good outcomes, motivation and independent problem-solving decline; that
is the textbook definition of **learned helplessness**, and 2026 survey data now links it
specifically to *AI dependency* [2]. The mechanism is intuitive: if a satisfactory result
appears every time you delegate, the felt connection between *your* effort and the *outcome*
quietly dissolves. Do that across enough tasks and the belief generalises — "it wasn't me, it
was the tool" — which is corrosive to motivation in a way a single shortcut never is [2].

The reassuring, non-alarmist framing — consistent with every prior issue — is that **this is
about the pattern of use, not fixed harm, and the remedy is nearly free.** The same studies
that flag passive use as costly find that a small change in *sequence and stance* removes most
of the cost [1][3], and that people who use AI as a scaffold rather than a substitute can come
away *more* motivated, not less [5]. You don't have to choose between speed and ownership. You
mostly have to choose the order you do things in.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **The felt "sense of agency" has real cognitive machinery — and automation dampens it.** Your
  brain builds a *sense of agency* — the feeling of "I did that" — partly from the match between
  the effort/action you invest and the outcome you observe. A 2026 co-creation experiment
  (He, *Frontiers in Psychology*; N = 162) varied how much of a music task the AI automated and
  measured participants' **state sense of agency**. Higher automation **reduced** felt effort
  (subjective task load), which reduced **psychological ownership**, which reduced the **sense of
  agency** — a clean serial chain [3]. When a finished result appears "with one click," the
  invested-effort cue the brain uses to tag work as *self-produced* is missing, and the mind
  shifts from "active constructor to passive responder" [3]. The lesson isn't to add busywork;
  it's that *some* felt effort is what makes an outcome register as yours.

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Passive use feels great in the moment — and that's the trap.** In the *Scientific Reports*
  experiment, copy-and-paste AI use **initially boosted enjoyment and satisfaction** — the work
  was easy and the output was polished. But those good feelings **reversed** once people went
  back to unaided work, while the deficits in self-efficacy and meaning persisted [1]. The
  behavioural takeaway: the in-the-moment pleasantness of "just let the AI do it" is a poor guide
  to whether it's good for you. Judge a workflow by how you feel about your abilities *afterward*,
  not by how smooth it felt at the time.

- **Skill doesn't protect you — it can raise the stakes.** Counter-intuitively, the *experts* in
  the co-creation study lost **more** ownership and agency under high automation than novices
  did [3]. For a beginner, automation fills a gap; for a skilled person, having the machine take
  over the core creative act sidelines the very ability that defines them. If you're good at
  something, guard the parts of it you most identify with — don't hand those over first.

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **The workflow *is* the intervention: "First Human, Then AI."** The single most useful
  operational finding this week is that **sequence matters**. Draft-first-then-refine preserved
  self-efficacy, ownership, and meaning at levels statistically indistinguishable from using no
  AI at all — while presumably keeping most of the time savings [1]. This is a concrete design
  rule for individuals and teams: default your AI workflows to *augment* (refine my work) rather
  than *automate* (produce the work), and make "show me your own draft first" a normal
  expectation rather than a productivity sin.

- **"Dependency" is not the same as "frequent use" — aim your worry correctly.** The learned-
  helplessness study is careful on this point: it's not *how often* you use AI that predicts harm,
  but **dependency** — a felt inability to do the task without it [2]. Frequent, fluent,
  *scaffolded* use can be fine or even beneficial [5]. The thing to monitor in yourself is not
  your usage count; it's whether you'd still feel capable if the tool vanished tomorrow.

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **The honest counter-evidence: used as a tutor, AI can *raise* motivation.** A 2026 quasi-
  experiment (Shao & Wang, *Frontiers in Psychology*; N = 200 STEM students) found that students
  given AI-assisted writing support reported **higher** intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy
  than those with traditional support — with the **largest gains for lower-achieving students**
  [5]. This is the crucial nuance that keeps this brief from becoming a scare story: AI is not
  inherently demotivating. When it functions as a patient, non-judgmental coach that helps you
  *do* the work, it can build confidence; the damage in [1] and [2] comes specifically from AI
  *replacing* the work.

- **What separates the two paths is a motivational one — and it's teachable.** A large SDT-based
  study of students (Fang & Feng, *Frontiers in Psychology*; N = 750) found that the difference
  between "**deep processing**" (prompting well, checking, revising, selectively using output)
  and "**over-reliance**" (copying uncritically) is driven by intrinsic motivation and by two
  basic psychological needs — feeling **competent** and feeling **autonomous** [4]. The policy
  implication mirrors the learned-helplessness paper's: rather than *banning* AI, build
  **AI literacy** and design tasks that keep human judgment in the loop; literacy measurably
  **buffered** the dependency-to-helplessness pathway [2][4].

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Draft it before you delegate it.** For anything you want to own, write your own rough
  version first, then use AI to refine. This "First Human, Then AI" order protected ownership,
  confidence, and meaning at no-AI levels in a controlled experiment [1].
- **Default your AI to *refine*, not *produce*.** Ask "improve/critique/tighten this" more than
  "write this from scratch." Augmentation preserves the felt link between your effort and the
  result; wholesale automation severs it [1][3].
- **Guard the part you most identify with.** If you're skilled at something, don't hand the core
  creative or analytical act over first — that's exactly where experts lose the most ownership
  and agency [3]. Automate the periphery; keep the centre.
- **Run the "if the tool vanished" test.** Once in a while, do a familiar task without AI. If
  that feels genuinely hard now, you've drifted from *use* toward *dependency* — the pattern
  that predicts helplessness and lost motivation [2]. Re-warm the skill.
- **Judge workflows by the after-feeling, not the in-the-moment ease.** Passive use feels great
  while you're doing it and worse afterward [1]. Ask whether a habit leaves you feeling *more* or
  *less* capable a day later.

### In personal life

- **Keep authorship of things that carry your name.** A wedding toast, a note to a friend, a
  personal decision — start these yourself. Let AI polish, not originate, so the result still
  reads (and feels) like you [1].
- **Use AI as a tutor, not a ghostwriter, when you're learning.** When the goal is to get
  *better* at something, have AI explain, quiz, and coach you through your own attempt — that's
  the mode that *raises* confidence and motivation, especially for beginners [5].
- **Protect your kids' (and your own) sense of "I did that."** For homework and hobbies, keep the
  human doing the load-bearing thinking; use AI for feedback and scaffolding. Competence and
  autonomy are what turn AI use into deep learning rather than over-reliance [2][4].
- **Build AI literacy, not AI avoidance.** Understanding what a model is good and bad at is what
  lets heavy users *keep* their sense of agency. Literacy buffered the slide into helplessness in
  the data [2][4]; abstinence isn't the goal, informed use is.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** "As long as the output is good, it doesn't matter whether I wrote it or the AI did —
> the result is what counts."
> **Reality:** The *result* isn't the only thing that changes. A preregistered experiment found
> that producing work by copy-pasting AI output lowered people's **belief in their own ability**,
> their sense that the work was **theirs**, and how **meaningful** they found it — and the hits to
> ability-belief and meaning **outlasted the task**, still showing up when people later worked
> unaided [1]. Those aren't soft extras: they're the psychological basis of motivation and
> persistence, and their erosion is linked to **learned helplessness** [2]. The fix isn't to
> avoid AI — used as a coach it can even *raise* confidence [5] — it's to keep your own effort
> visibly in the loop by drafting before you delegate [1].

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **One experiment is a strong signal, not the last word.** The *Scientific Reports* study is
  preregistered and experimental (its main strength), but the primary sample is N = 269 doing
  short (~10-minute) occupation-style writing tasks on Prolific, and a chunk of participants had
  to be excluded for failing the manipulation check [1]. "Lingering" effects were measured over
  one follow-up task, not weeks or months — treat "persistence" as *within-session*, not proof of
  long-term change.
- **The learned-helplessness result is correlational.** Cui & Khan is a **cross-sectional
  survey** (N = 457, single country) analysed with SEM [2]. It shows a coherent, theory-consistent
  pattern, but it **cannot establish causation or direction** — less motivated or more helpless
  students might also lean on AI more. Read the βs as associations, not effects of an intervention.
- **The evidence points both ways, on purpose.** The same body of work that flags passive use as
  costly [1][2][3] also shows AI *raising* motivation and confidence when used as a tutor [5], and
  identifies conditions (competence, autonomy, literacy) under which heavy use stays healthy
  [2][4]. This is not "AI harms motivation." It's "**automating** the work harms it; **augmenting**
  your own work needn't."
- **Constructs are self-reported and culturally situated.** Ownership, agency, meaning, and
  motivation are measured with questionnaires, and several studies here draw on Chinese
  university samples [2][4][5]; magnitudes may not transfer directly to other settings or to
  seasoned professionals.
- **"Draft first" has real costs sometimes.** For low-stakes, high-volume, or genuinely
  commoditised tasks, insisting on a human-first draft may not be worth it. The habit is meant
  for work you want to *own* or *learn from* — not a universal tax on every keystroke.
