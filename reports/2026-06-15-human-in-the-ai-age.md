## 1. Executive Summary

The defining risk of the AI era isn't that the machines think too well — it's that, by
quietly handing our thinking to them, **we practise our own less.** A year of
converging research — an EEG study from MIT, a 666-person survey in *Societies*, a
319-worker study from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, and a 2026
neuroscience framework in *npj Artificial Intelligence* — keeps pointing at the same
distinction: **passive, uncritical AI use is associated with weaker engagement and
critical thinking, while active, critical use is not.** The mechanism has a name —
*cognitive offloading* — and the leading worry has one too: *cognitive debt.*

But the same body of work carries a hopeful corollary, and it's the most important
thing to internalise this week: **the harm is in the *pattern of use*, not the tool.**
Neuroscientists now argue the very same AI tool can push your brain in *opposite*
directions depending on whether you consume its output passively or engage with it
actively. Wharton's Ethan Mollick gives the failure mode a blunt name — **"cognitive
surrender"** — and the fix is equally blunt.

**The single most useful habit this week:** *do your own thinking first.* Form your own
answer, draft, or plan **before** you open an AI tool — then use AI to challenge,
extend, and pressure-test it. People who think *then* prompt keep their cognitive
engagement; people who prompt *then* (maybe) think tend to lose it.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Rossi et al. (2026), *npj Artificial Intelligence* — the "3R principle."** Argues
  neuroplasticity is *activity- and time-dependent* ("use it or lose it"), so
  interaction style matters at the synaptic level. Their guideline: treat AI output as
  a **Result** (raw data, not truth), convert it into a **Response** (interpreted and
  evaluated within your own values), and retain **Responsibility** (judgment and
  accountability that *cannot* be outsourced). ([npj AI][r3r])
- **Nataliya Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab) — *Your Brain on ChatGPT* (June 2025).**
  54 participants wrote essays under three conditions (Brain-only / Search / LLM) under
  EEG. Brain connectivity **scaled down with the amount of external help**; LLM users
  showed the weakest networks, reported the lowest *ownership* of their essays, and
  often **couldn't quote work they'd written minutes earlier.** A 4th session suggested
  lingering under-engagement — the authors' "cognitive debt." *The authors themselves
  ask readers not to call this "brain damage"; it is a preliminary preprint (see §5 &
  §7).* ([MIT Media Lab][mit])
- **Practising neuroscientists urge caution, not panic.** In TIME (May 2026),
  researchers note that mental skills behave somewhat like muscles — *"if you always
  offload one particular skill to AI, you're likely to become deskilled with regards to
  that specific task"* — while adding we may gain *new* skills (like critically
  evaluating AI output) at the same time. ([TIME][time])

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Michael Gerlich (SBS Swiss Business School), *Societies* (Jan 2025), n=666.** Found
  a **strong negative correlation (r ≈ −0.75) between cognitive offloading and
  critical-thinking scores,** with AI use driving offloading (r ≈ 0.72). Younger users
  were more dependent and scored lower; higher education buffered but did **not
  eliminate** the effect. (Cross-sectional — correlation, not causation.) ([via ICLR
  2026 synthesis][iclr])
- **Beth Kanter — the "Brain Engagement Meter."** A 5-level scale for cognitive
  engagement during an AI session. Levels 1–2 are "cognitive surrender" (AI runs, your
  brain is in a jar); Levels 3–4 are the **active matching zone** — you bring your own
  thinking and use AI to extend it. The value is *in-the-moment* awareness, while you
  can still course-correct. ([Beth Kanter][bk])

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton; *Co-Intelligence*) — *Choosing to Stay Human.*** Names the
  failure mode **"cognitive surrender"**: people stop thinking and let the AI run *even
  when it's wrong.* His prescription is about *intent*, not abstinence: make a
  **conscious choice** about each task rather than reflexive dependence (or reflexive
  avoidance). His recurring line: *"keep asking what to hand over and what to keep for
  ourselves… and don't expect anyone, including the AI, to answer that for us."*
  Treat AI as a **teammate you manage** — clear brief, approval gates, and verify
  against your own expertise. ([Mollick][moll])
- **Wharton Executive Education (May 2026) — "Tri-System Theory."** Not all deference
  to AI is harmful; in well-defined tasks where AI is simply more accurate, deferring
  can be rational. The skill is **knowing when deference is right and when a decision
  needs human judgment** — a risk that's "invisible until the moment it matters most."
  ([Wharton][whar])

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Lee, Tankelevitch et al. (Microsoft Research + Carnegie Mellon), CHI 2025, n=319
  knowledge workers / 936 tasks.** Key result: **higher confidence in the AI predicts
  *less* critical thinking; higher confidence in *yourself* predicts *more.*** AI tends
  to **shift** cognition from *generating* work toward *verifying and integrating* it —
  so deliberately build verification back into your workflow. (Measures self-reported
  effort, not raw capability — see §5.) ([via ICLR 2026 synthesis][iclr])
- **EDUCAUSE Review (Dec 2025) — "The Paradox of AI Assistance: Better Results, Worse
  Thinking."** Warns of **"metacognitive laziness"** — passively accepting output
  rather than evaluating it — and that GenAI can interrupt reflection by removing the
  productive "mental unrest" that drives inquiry, especially for younger users.
  ([EDUCAUSE][educause])

## 5. The Counter-View

A weekly brief that only quoted the scary headlines would be doing exactly the passive,
uncritical thing it warns against. So, the honest caveats:

- **The flagship MIT study is a preprint with real limits.** ~9 participants per cell
  in the key 4th-session finding; thousands of EEG comparisons (a multiple-comparisons
  hazard); and EEG is a coarse proxy — "closer to microphones outside a stadium than a
  readout of thoughts." Crucially, **lower neural connectivity means *different*, not
  necessarily *worse*,** cognition. ([Comment paper][crit]; [skeptical review][skeptic])
- **Self-report ≠ capability.** The Microsoft/CMU study measures *perceived* effort and
  critical thinking, not measured ability.
- **Correlation ≠ causation.** The Gerlich study is cross-sectional: maybe weaker
  critical thinkers simply lean on AI more.
- **AI can also *build* skill.** The strongest causal studies on *scaffolded* AI
  tutoring show sizable *positive* learning effects. The tool isn't destiny — the design
  and the habit are.

The throughline: *be appropriately concerned, not apocalyptic.* The robust, repeatable
finding across all of it is the **active-vs-passive distinction**, not any single
dramatic number.

## 6. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work
- **Think first, prompt second.** Draft your own outline/answer before opening any AI
  tool; then use AI to critique and extend it. (Counters the "prompt-first" debt pattern.)
- **Make AI argue against you.** Ask for the strongest case *against* your view, the
  errors in its own answer, and its sources — then verify the sources yourself.
- **Keep a human at the decision gate.** Especially in high-stakes domains (health,
  law, finance, people decisions), treat AI as a draft, never the verdict.
- **Run the 3R check.** Is this a *Result* (raw output), a *Response* (you've judged
  it), and have you kept *Responsibility* for it? If not, slow down.
- **Notice your "Brain Engagement" level mid-task.** If you've slipped to "AI runs, I
  rubber-stamp," stop and re-engage.

### In personal life
- **Protect a daily "no-AI" thinking rep.** Read something long and hard, write a few
  paragraphs unaided, or work a problem on paper — keep the deep-reading and reasoning
  circuits in use.
- **Don't outsource memory you actually need.** Offloading a one-off fact is fine;
  routinely offloading the things that make up your judgment is the trap.
- **Pick what to keep human on purpose.** Decide which tasks are about the *output*
  (delegate freely) and which are about *you getting better* (do them yourself).
- **Mind the kids and the juniors.** Effects skew larger for younger/early-career
  users; model "AI as sparring partner," not "AI as answer vending machine."

## 7. Caveats & How to Read This

This is an automated weekly synthesis, not peer-reviewed advice. Several headline
findings come from **preprints and self-report studies**; where that's the case it's
flagged above. Treat dramatic single statistics with suspicion and weight the
**direction** of the evidence (active engagement good; passive surrender risky) over any
one number. Sources are listed below so you can — fittingly — **check the work
