## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 008 asked you to *leave one gap empty* — a
stretch of no input so the default-mode network can
wander. Issue 014 asked children (and the adults who
teach them) to *ask for a hint, not the answer*.
Issue 024 asked you to *retrieve it cold* after a
fluent page already exists. This week is none of
those. It is about the **question itself**: the
information gap you can feel *before* anyone, human
or model, closes it.

George Loewenstein named the mechanism in 1994.
Curiosity is not a personality garnish. It is
**cognitively induced deprivation** — the aversive,
attention-grabbing state that starts when you notice
the difference between what you know and what you
want to know [1]. Min Jeong Kang, George Loewenstein,
Colin Camerer and colleagues later showed that this
state has a sweet spot. Curiosity is an inverted U
against how sure you are: it peaks when your
confidence that you know the answer is around
**0.50**, and it tracks *P*(1 − *P*) at *r* = .44.
People will spend tokens, and wait extra seconds, to
close a gap they have already felt [2].

Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman, and Charan Ranganath
put a clock on that feeling. In the scanner, **19**
people waited **14 seconds** for trivia answers they
were or were not curious about. High-curiosity
answers were recalled at **70.6%** versus **54.1%**
for low-curiosity ones. The dopaminergic midbrain
and nucleus accumbens fired during *anticipation*,
not at the answer. A day later, in a separate
behavioural group, the same split was **45.9%**
versus **28.1%**. Faces flashed in the waiting
window were remembered better too. The encoding
window is the gap [3].

A copilot that answers in the first turn skips that
window. You still get the model. The habit is that
the question stays yours long enough to be a
question.

**The single habit to start this week:** *Sit with
the question.* Before you paste, write the question
in your own words and a one-line guess. Then prompt.
If you use the model, tell it to **ask, not dump**
on the first turn. You close the gap. It does not
close it for you.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

Instant answers are now the default product. The
interface is built to collapse Loewenstein’s gap in
under a second. That is useful when the question is
a lookup. It is costly when the question is how you
learn, decide, or notice what you do not know.
Kang’s subjects were most curious when they were
neither blank nor sure. A model that always
completes the sentence never lets you occupy that
middle.

2026 evidence says the *structure of the turn*
decides whether curiosity survives the chat. In a
controlled tutoring study, curiosity-oriented
linguistic interventions — novelty, complexity,
conflict, uncertainty, in the spirit of Daniel
Berlyne — produced up to **2.4×** more conversational
turns under the same time budget, with large gains
in learner questioning and agency, even when
tutor-side “instructional quality” did not improve.
Same models. Different questions [4]. Across four
public chat corpora, Michigan NLP found that
curiosity-oriented model questions are still the
minority (**17–28%** of the questions models ask).
When they do appear, the next human turn is more
epistemically marked — especially after reflective
and exploratory questions. Participants still prefer
a direct answer. They also say the curiosity
question is what would make them keep thinking [5].

This is not Issue 008 again (empty input, any room).
It is not Issue 014 (hint versus answer while a
novice is still inside a problem). It is not Issue
024 (reconstruct a page that already exists). Those
briefs asked what to do with idle time, with a
homework item, or with a recap. This one asks
whether you ever **feel the gap** before someone
fills it.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Matthias J. Gruber, Bernard D. Gelman, and
  Charan Ranganath (UC Davis).** *Neuron*, 22 October
  2014 (online 2 October). **N = 19** in the
  scanner; a one-day behavioural replication
  *t*(26). Trivia questions, 14 s of anticipation, a
  neutral face in the middle of the wait. High- vs
  low-curiosity recall **70.6%** vs **54.1%**,
  *t*(17) = 5.64, *p* < .001. Nucleus accumbens and
  substantia nigra / ventral tegmental area rose
  with curiosity *before* the answer. Connectivity
  between midbrain and hippocampus during that wait
  predicted how well people later recognised the
  irrelevant faces. A day later: **45.9%** vs
  **28.1%**, *t*(26) = 11.11. The state that encodes
  is the wait, not the reveal [3].
- **Min Jeong Kang, Ming Hsu, Ian Krajbich, George
  Loewenstein, Samuel McClure, Joseph Tao-yi Wang,
  and Colin Camerer.** *Psychological Science*,
  August 2009. Experiment 1, **N = 19** Caltech
  students, fMRI. Curiosity tracked caudate (reward
  anticipation), lateral prefrontal cortex, and
  parahippocampal gyrus when the question appeared.
  Curiosity was an inverted U against confidence,
  peaking near *P* = .50; *r* = .44 with
  *P*(1 − *P*), *p* < .0005. Experiment 2,
  **N = 16** (twelve returned 11–16 days later):
  among items first guessed *wrong*, high- vs
  low-curiosity recall *d* = 1.11. Experiment 3,
  **N = 30**: people spent tokens or waiting time
  for answers they were more curious about; +1 SD
  curiosity bought about **3.7 extra seconds** of
  waiting. Information is treated like a reward
  when the gap is open [2].
- **Celeste Kidd and Benjamin Hayden.** *Neuron*
  (review), 4 November 2015. The field’s map:
  curiosity is not one circuit and not just
  “wanting facts.” It includes information as a
  reward, exploration under uncertainty, and
  developmental foraging. The practical warning is
  the same as Gruber’s: if you never occupy
  uncertainty, you never run the circuitry that
  makes uncertainty useful [6].

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **George Loewenstein (Carnegie Mellon).**
  *Psychological Bulletin*, 1994. Information-gap
  theory. Curiosity is deprivation induced by
  attention to a missing piece. It is intense,
  transient, a little impulsive, and often slightly
  disappointing when satisfied — which is why a
  chatbot that closes every gap on contact is so
  good at ending the episode and so poor at
  starting the next one [1].
- **Jordan Litman.** *Cognition and Emotion*, 2005
  (I/D model). Two flavours. **I-type** (interest):
  take-it-or-leave-it pleasure in something new,
  low “wanting,” high “liking.” **D-type**
  (deprivation): an uncomfortable need to know, high
  wanting *and* liking, more persistence. Instant
  answers mainly cancel D-type. I-type can survive
  a browse. The work question you actually need
  answered is usually D-type. Sit with that one [7].
- **Daniel E. Berlyne.** *Conflict, Arousal, and
  Curiosity*, 1960. Collative variables — novelty,
  complexity, conflict, uncertainty — are what make
  a stimulus worth inspecting. CurioBot’s 2026
  operators are this list with an API [8].
- **Michelene Chi, Nicholas de Leeuw, Mei-Hung
  Chiu, and Christian LaVancher.** *Cognitive
  Science*, 1994. Prompted self-explanation while
  studying a physics text produced deeper, more
  accurate mental models than reading the same
  text. The question you generate is not
  decoration. It is the encoding operation [9].
- **Lashkar Hussain, Muhammad Jawwad, Muhammad
  Meiwan Ashraf, and Javed Mengal.** *Journal of
  Global Social Transformation*, 24 October 2026.
  Cross-sectional survey, **N = 301** Pakistani
  university students, convenience sample, Google
  Forms. Generative-AI use correlated with
  cognitive offloading (*r* = .584, *p* < .001).
  Offloading predicted *lower* epistemic curiosity
  (*B* = −0.218). Direct path from AI use to
  curiosity was *positive* (*B* = 0.341); the
  indirect path through offloading was negative
  (*B* = −0.115, 95% CI −0.205 to −0.037).
  Inconsistent mediation: the same tool can invite
  questions or replace them, depending on whether
  you offload the thinking. Treat as association,
  not a trial [10].

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Gevindu Ganganath, Pasindu Bolonghege, Qianru
  Lyu, Pradeep Varakantham, and Thivya Kandappu
  (Singapore Management University / CMU).**
  arXiv:2606.22349, 21 June 2026. **Preprint.**
  CurioBot: Berlyne operators as inference-time
  tutoring. **N = 45** students, **270**
  conversations, 3 model families × 3 domains × 3
  complexities, time on task fixed. Curiosity
  modulation produced up to **2.4×** more turns.
  Learner-side scores rose across models (*p* <
  .005): exploratory questioning +21–32%,
  observable curiosity +26–35%, conversational
  agency +30–34%, productive struggle +44–47%.
  Gains held when Gemini’s tutor-side quality
  *dropped*. Curiosity is not the same variable as
  “the tutor explained well” [4].
- **Angana Borah, Rachana Ramchandar, Inderjeet
  Nair, and Rada Mihalcea (University of
  Michigan).** arXiv:2610.33950, 24 October 2026
  (v2 5 November). **Preprint.** Four in-the-wild
  corpora (WildChat, OASST1, LMSYS-Chat-1M,
  ShareGPT). Curiosity-oriented questions
  **17–28%** of model questions. Next-turn
  epistemic markers rose most after
  reflective-metacognitive (+0.243), then
  counterfactual (+0.230) and exploratory
  (+0.220); causal-explanatory least (+0.151).
  Prolific study: **50** people, **230** items.
  Direct answers win preference and naturalness.
  Curiosity questions win “this would make me keep
  thinking,” especially in advice, creative, and
  research contexts. People want the dump. They
  explore after the question [5].
- **Nakyung Kim and Yong Gu Ji (Yonsei).**
  *International Journal of Human-Computer
  Studies*, 210, article 103771, 2026 (online 19
  February; issue March). **Peer-reviewed.**
  **N = 36**, 2 × 2 within-subject: progressive
  disclosure of the model’s answer × high vs low
  user prompt initiative, on unfamiliar but
  relatable topics meant to elicit curiosity.
  Progressive disclosure and high initiative both
  raised perceived knowledge change, engagement,
  germane load, and exploratory prompting. Dumping
  the whole answer at once is the opposite of
  sitting with the question [11].
- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton).** *One Useful Thing*.
  27 November 2022: specific curiosity — the itch
  to explain a puzzle — predicts next-day
  creativity; mysteries are a practice, not a
  personality [12]. 5 April 2023: use the model as
  a *thinking companion* that challenges you, not
  a decider [13]. A reader’s tactic that has aged
  well: “Before you act on these instructions, ask
  me clarifying questions.” That is this week’s
  prompt, one sentence [13].

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Farhan Ali (Nanyang Technological University)
  and Yehong Yang (Beijing Normal University).**
  AIED 2026, Seoul, 29 June 2026. *Lecture Notes in
  Computer Science* (Springer). **Peer-reviewed
  conference paper.** **N = 378** students in
  Grades 5–8, two-week unit, random assignment to
  regular instruction or a classroom chatbot.
  Outcome: a written assessment *without* GenAI.
  Heterogeneous treatment effects by type of
  curiosity and by prior achievement; interaction
  patterns mediated some of the effect. The authors’
  conclusion is the one this series will not
  overclaim: GenAI can **widen** existing gaps
  depending on who is already curious and how they
  talk to the bot. Exact per-profile *d* values
  were not in the public abstract; direction
  only [14].
- **Arthur Graesser and colleagues.** AutoTutor and
  the question-asking literature: learning
  conversations work when the system keeps a
  knowledge gap open long enough for the learner to
  generate an explanation, not when it lectures.
  Issue 014 used this family for K-12 *hints*.
  This week uses it for adult *questions*: the
  model’s job is to return the gap to you [15].
- **Classroom translation.** Ali and Yang’s
  inequality warning plus CurioBot’s 2.4× turns
  plus Kim and Ji’s initiative effect is one
  policy sentence: do not give students (or staff)
  an answer oracle and call it inquiry. Give them
  a turn structure that requires a question of
  their own.

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Sit with the question.** Before the paste,
  write one sentence: *what I actually want to
  know*, and one guess. Gruber’s 14 seconds are
  not a ritual number. They are a reminder that
  the wait is the encoding. Kang says the guess
  is part of the gap.
- **First turn: questions only.** “Do not answer
  yet. Ask me the three questions that would
  change your answer. Wait.” Mollick’s reader
  tactic. Borah: reflective and exploratory
  questions, not “would you like me to
  continue?”
- **Progressive disclosure, high initiative.**
  Kim and Ji: do not take the whole dump. Ask
  for the next piece, then the next. You keep
  prompt initiative; the model does not narrate
  the topic.
- **Protect D-type questions.** The uncomfortable
  “I should know this and I do not” is Litman’s
  deprivation curiosity. Do not soothe it with
  a summary. Name the missing piece. Then search
  or prompt.
- **Do not outsource the question to the model
  and call it curiosity.** CurioBot raised
  *learner* questions. A bot that asks you
  questions you then ignore is theatre. Answer
  its question in a sentence of your own before
  you let it proceed.

### In personal life

- **One un-Googled hour of a puzzle.** Mollick’s
  specific curiosity: sit with a mystery (a
  gadget, a recipe that failed, a news claim)
  long enough to generate *your* question. Then
  look it up. The lookup is allowed. The skipped
  itch is not.
- **Read until you have a question, then stop.**
  Chi: self-explanation beats more text. Close
  the article. Write “why does…?” Then open the
  model or the rest of the page.
- **For kids, keep the gap visible.** Issue 014
  still owns homework hints. This week’s family
  version is: the child states the question and
  a guess *out loud* before anyone — parent or
  chatbot — talks. Ali and Yang: the already-curious
  child and the not-yet-curious child will not
  get the same thing from the same bot.
- **When the answer arrives, ask one more.**
  Loewenstein: curiosity dies when the gap
  closes. Leave one related question unanswered
  on purpose. That is not Issue 008’s empty
  room. It is a named next gap.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** Using AI kills curiosity. Curious
> people do not need tools, and tools make
> everyone incurious. Science has shown that
> chatbots damage the brain’s reward system.
>
> **Reality:** Curiosity is a *state* you can
> occupy or skip. Gruber and Kang measured
> that state in a trivia wait, not a lesion.
> CurioBot and Kim & Ji show the same model
> can host more questions or fewer, depending
> on how the turn is built. Hussain’s survey
> found a *positive* direct link from AI use
> to curiosity and a *negative* indirect link
> through offloading — inconsistent mediation,
> not a morality play. Borah’s raters still
> prefer the dump. Preferring the dump is the
> habit to notice, not proof of brain damage.
> None of this is a reason to throw the tool
> out. It is a reason not to let the first
> token close a gap you have not felt.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Gruber N = 19 in the scanner** (and a
  separate one-day behavioural group). Trivia
  and incidental faces, not strategy memos.
  The 14 s wait is a design, not a dose. Do
  not advertise “wait fourteen seconds or you
  will not remember.” Advertise: the
  anticipatory state is doing work the answer
  does not redo.
- **Kang’s memory *d* = 1.11** is high- vs
  low-curiosity items that were *first guessed
  wrong*, in twelve returning Caltech students.
  Small, selected, not an AI study.
- **CurioBot is a preprint.** Learner-side
  scores are rubric ratings (including
  LLM-as-judge dimensions). Time was matched;
  learning *tests* were not the primary
  claim. “2.4× more turns” is exploration,
  not grades.
- **Borah / Mihalcea is a preprint.** 17–28%
  is a range across corpora, not a universal
  rate. Next-turn “epistemic markers” are
  linguistic, not fMRI. The Prolific study is
  preference, not a learning outcome.
- **Kim & Ji N = 36**, subjective knowledge
  change and germane load. Direction is
  enough; we did not invent *F* statistics
  from a paywalled table.
- **Ali & Yang** is peer-reviewed at AIED
  2026. We have the design (N = 378, two
  weeks, unassisted written test) and the
  heterogeneity conclusion. We do not have
  per-cell *d*. Do not invent them.
- **Hussain et al.** is a convenience-sample
  cross-section in a little-cited journal.
  Self-report, one country, no random
  assignment. Useful as a *pattern* (AI can
  correlate with both more curiosity and more
  offloading). Not a causal headline. Issue
  006 already owns offloading-as-forgetting;
  this paper is cited only for the curiosity
  path.
- **Loewenstein, Litman, Berlyne, Chi, and
  Kidd/Hayden are not AI studies.** The leap
  we are willing to take is narrow: a gap you
  never feel is not curiosity, and a first-turn
  dump is a closed gap.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.**
  Nothing here is a drop in IQ, a dopaminergic
  lesion, or a reason to panic. The evidence
  is about patterns of use: whether you occupy
  the question or skip it.
