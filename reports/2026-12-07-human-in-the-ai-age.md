## 1. Executive Summary

Issue 006 asked you to *generate before you store* —
to produce the note yourself so the model does not
become an external brain that your hippocampus treats
as a cue to forget. Issue 003 asked you to *keep your
hands on the work* — to struggle during first
production. Issue 014 asked children (and the adults
who teach them) to *ask for a hint, not the answer*.
This week is none of those. It is about what happens
**after the material already exists**: the meeting
recap, the model’s summary, last week’s draft. The
default review is to open it and read. That feels like
knowing. It is not.

Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke named the
corrective in 2006: the **testing effect**. Students
who studied a prose passage and then took a recall
test remembered **56%** a week later; students who
used the same time to restudy remembered **42%**.
When the comparison was three tests versus three
extra studies, the week-later scores were **61%**
versus **40%**. Immediate tests went the other way —
restudy looked better at five minutes — which is why
the fluency of an AI recap is such a trap. You feel
finished. A week later you are not [1].

The 2026 twist is that the recap is now fluent by
default, and the tool that wrote it will happily write
it again. In a Penn State applied-statistics course,
**89** undergraduates all got the same adaptive
AI-assisted pretest, the same instruction, and the
same baseline. Over the next seven weeks they were
randomly assigned to adaptive spaced retrieval with
the AI, fixed spaced retrieval with the AI, or
learner-directed AI study — the ordinary “ask it
anything” chat. Adaptive retrieval beat open chat on
the end-of-semester posttest (**78.19** vs **67.28**,
*d* = 0.92). Open chat also produced the least
effort. Same model. Same number of sessions. The
structure of the turn decided whether the follow-up
was learning or rereading with extra tokens [2].

You still get the model. The habit is that review
happens **cold**.

**The single habit to start this week:** *Retrieve it
cold.* After a briefing, a draft, or an AI summary,
close the pane. Write or say the skeleton from
memory — claims, numbers, the decision. Then open
and check. If you use the model later, tell it to
**ask, wait, and grade**. Never to answer for you.

## 2. Why This Matters Now

The copilot did not invent rereading. It industrialised
it. A finished-looking page is now cheaper than a
question. Re-opening the chat feels like review because
the sentences are smooth and the argument is already
in the right order. That is the **illusion of fluency**:
ease of processing is mistaken for knowledge in
storage. Karpicke and Janell Blunt showed the
academic version in *Science* in 2011. Students
predicted that restudying and concept-mapping would
beat retrieval. On a test one week later, retrieval
scored **0.67** against **0.45** for concept mapping
—*d* = 1.50, about a 50% relative gain, including on
inference questions. The students’ judgements of
learning pointed at the wrong activity [3].

Jonathan Tullis and Hosain Heshmati brought that
illusion into the ChatGPT year. In a 2026 Lilly
Conference study, students read class passages, then
either reread them or answered ChatGPT-generated
questions with feedback. Students believed they had
learned more from rereading. On the delayed test they
had learned more from the questions [4]. The model
can instantiate retrieval. It can also destroy it. The
same lab’s CUES seminar this month asked what
happens when AI *answers* the practice questions for
you, writes the summaries, or generates the quizzes.
Those are not one intervention. They are three. Only
the third is this week’s habit.

This is not Issue 006 again (encode by generating
the note). It is not Issue 003 (struggle while you
first make the work). It is not Issue 014 (hint versus
answer while a novice is still in the problem). Those
briefs asked *how the first trace gets into the head*.
This one asks *how you keep it there once a fluent
page exists*. Rereading the model’s recap is restudy.
Closing it is a test.

## 3. Voices From the Field

### 3.1 Neuroscience & the Brain

- **Carola Wiklund-Hörnqvist, Lars Nyberg, and
  colleagues (Umeå).** *Brain and Behavior*, January
  2021 (online 22 October 2020). **N = 50** upper
  secondary students learned Swahili–Swedish pairs
  half by restudy, half by retrieval with feedback, six
  repetitions each. One week later, in the scanner,
  retrieval-practised items were recalled at **0.53**
  versus **0.36** for studied items, *t*(49) = 8.61,
  *d* = 1.22. Posterior hippocampus activity scaled
  linearly with how many times an item had been
  successfully retrieved during learning. Anterior
  hippocampus activity showed up for items retrieved
  many times, not few — a candidate signature of
  gist across episodes, not just a stronger copy of
  one. The testing effect is not a classroom slogan.
  It is a hippocampal practice schedule [5].
- **Linnea Karlsson Wirebring, Carola
  Wiklund-Hörnqvist, Lars Nyberg, and colleagues.**
  *Journal of Neuroscience*, 1 July 2015. **N = 23**
  adults (five discarded for empty conditions) studied
  60 Swahili–Swedish pairs, were tested three times
  in the scanner on day 1, and once on day 7.
  Successful later memory tracked **lower** pattern
  similarity in right superior parietal cortex across
  the day-1 tests, and a dropping dorsolateral
  prefrontal signal for items that would still be
  remembered a week later. Repeated retrieval does
  not stamp the same trace harder. It **varies and
  elaborates** the representation, and it cheapens
  later control. Repeated study, in other work, tends
  to do the opposite: more similar traces [6].
- **James Antony, Catarina Ferreira, Kenneth
  Norman, and Maria Wimber.** *Trends in Cognitive
  Sciences*, 2017. The theoretical hinge: retrieval is
  a **fast route to consolidation**. Offline sleep
  (Issue 011) still owns the slow overnight pass.
  Online retrieval does a related job in minutes —
  reactivating associates, integrating them with what
  you already know, differentiating them from
  competitors — so the memory is less dependent on
  a replay of the original page. An AI recap you
  never reconstruct never runs that pass [7].

### 3.2 Behavioural Science & Habits

- **Henry L. Roediger III and Jeffrey D. Karpicke
  (Washington University in St. Louis).**
  *Psychological Science*, 2006. Experiment 1,
  **N = 120**, 2 × 3: one restudy versus one test,
  final recall at 5 minutes, 2 days, or 1 week.
  Restudy won immediately (**81%** vs **75%**,
  *d* = 0.52). Testing won at 2 days (**68%** vs
  **54%**, *d* = 0.95) and at 1 week (**56%** vs
  **42%**, *d* = 0.83). Experiment 2 compared
  study-study-study-study with study-test-test-test:
  at one week, **40%** versus **61%**. No feedback
  on the practice tests. The gain is not
  re-exposure [1].
- **Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt (Purdue).**
  *Science*, 20 January 2011. Retrieval beat
  elaborative concept mapping on verbatim *and*
  inference questions a week later (Experiment 1
  key contrast *d* = 1.50). Experiment 2, **N = 120**,
  within-subject, even when the final test *was* a
  concept map. Students predicted the reverse.
  Concept mapping is not the enemy. Treating a
  finished map — or a finished chatbot page — as
  proof of knowledge is [3].
- **John Dunlosky, Katherine Rawson, Elizabeth
  Marsh, Mitchell Nathan, and Daniel Willingham.**
  *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*,
  2013. Ten popular study techniques. Only two
  earned **high utility**: practice testing and
  distributed practice. Rereading, highlighting, and
  summarising — the moves a copilot now performs
  for you — were low. The monograph is still the
  adult field guide [8].
- **Christopher Rowland.** *Psychological Bulletin*,
  2014. Testing versus restudy: **61** studies, **159**
  effects, *g* = **0.50**. Recall tests beat recognition.
  Effortful production is doing work that a
  multiple-choice glance, and a chatbot answer,
  skip [9].
- **Olusola Adesope, Dominic Trevisan, and
  Narayankripa Sundararajan.** *Review of
  Educational Research*, 2017. **118** articles, **272**
  effects. Practice testing versus nontesting
  conditions: *g* = **0.61** overall; versus restudy,
  about **0.51**. The restudy comparison is the one
  that matches “I will just read the recap again” [10].
- **Jonathan Tullis and Di Zhang.** *Journal of
  Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 2 September
  2025. Two experiments, **N = 69** each. Retrieval
  and generating mnemonics both beat restudy on
  memory and transfer at 5 minutes and at 2 days.
  Retrieval took about **half as long** as mnemonic
  generation. Students still rated mnemonics as more
  effective. Efficiency is not what the mind rewards.
  Fluency is [11].
- **Robert and Elizabeth Bjork.** Desirable
  difficulties: the conditions that slow performance
  now (spacing, testing, variation) are often the
  ones that improve the memory later. A chatbot
  that removes the difficulty removes the
  difficulty’s benefit. That is not a reason to make
  work unpleasant. It is a reason not to outsource
  the retrieval [12].

### 3.3 AI Practitioners & Researchers

- **Mahir Akgun (Penn State) and Sacip Toker
  (Atilim).** arXiv:2606.22328, 2026. **Preprint.**
  Upper-division applied statistics, multiple linear
  regression in a cybersecurity frame. Common
  adaptive AI pretest, then seven weeks of
  structured follow-up. **N = 89** (G1 = 27, G2 = 28,
  G3 = 34). MANCOVA on posttest plus logged
  effort: Wilks’ Λ = .664, *F*(4, 168) = 9.56,
  *p* < .001, ηp² = .185. Posttest ANCOVA
  *F*(2, 85) = 6.24, *p* = .003, ηp² = .128.
  Adaptive retrieval **78.19** (SE 2.37) versus
  learner-directed AI **67.28** (SE 2.12), *d* = 0.92,
  *p* = .003. Fixed retrieval **74.55** sat in between
  and did not reliably beat either neighbour after
  correction. Effort: G1 *M* = 0.85 versus G3
  *M* = 0.49, *d* = 1.33. Session count was matched;
  exchange length did not differ. The ordinary
  “use AI to study” chat was the weakest of the
  three legal uses of the same agent [2].
- **Jonathan Tullis and Hosain Heshmati
  (University of Arizona).** Lilly Conference Texas,
  2026. Students studied four class passages, then
  reread or took ChatGPT questions with feedback.
  Delayed multiple-choice favoured the questions;
  judgements of learning favoured rereading. Exact
  *N* and percentages were not in the public
  abstract — treat the direction as the finding, not
  a *d*. Tullis’s 10 November 2026 CUES seminar
  (*AI as Shortcut or Tutor?*) is the lab’s own
  warning: generating questions, writing summaries,
  and answering questions for the student are
  different treatments. This week keeps the first
  and refuses the third [4].
- **Ethan Mollick (Wharton).** 17 March 2023,
  “Using AI to make teaching easier.” Strategy 3
  was not a better explainer. It was a **low-stakes
  quiz generator**: diagnostic items, no
  all-of-the-above, an answer key after the attempt.
  In 2025–26 he has kept saying the same thing
  more sharply: unconstrained chatbots give the
  *illusion of learning* because they provide
  answers; tutor-conditioned models help when they
  force an attempt. Issue 014 used the K-12 hint
  literature. This week uses Mollick for the adult
  review loop: the model may write the quiz. You
  write the answer [13].

### 3.4 Academia, Education & Policy

- **Chunliang Yang, Liang Luo, Miguel Vadillo,
  Rong Yu, and David Shanks.** *Psychological
  Bulletin*, 2021. Classroom quizzing, not word-pair
  labs: **222** independent studies, **48,478**
  students, overall *g* = **0.499**. Medium, not
  magic. Moderators include the control strategy,
  feedback, and whether the quiz matches what will
  later be asked. A workplace translation is allowed
  only at that size: a cold recap of yesterday’s
  decision beats re-reading the deck, on average,
  in the same moderate way a quiz beats restudy [14].
- **Pooja Agarwal, Ludmila Nunes, and Janell
  Blunt.** *Educational Psychology Review*, 2021.
  Applied school and classroom retrieval: **50**
  experiments in the systematic review (Visible
  Learning’s extract: 37 studies / 5,374 students /
  *g* = 0.66 in one slice). The consistent finding is
  not “more tests.” It is retrieval as a learning
  event with modest stakes [15].
- **Shana Carpenter, Steven Pan, and Andrew
  Butler.** *Nature Reviews Psychology*, 2022. The
  current map of spacing plus retrieval for people
  who have to remember something they will not
  have open. Akgun’s seven-week design is this
  review with an LLM in the loop. Learner-directed
  chat is not spacing-plus-retrieval just because
  the sessions are on a calendar [16].

## 5. Do This Now — Practical Checklist

### At work

- **Retrieve it cold.** After the model (or a colleague)
  produces a recap you will need later — a decision,
  a design, three numbers from a meeting — close
  the pane. Write the skeleton from memory. Then
  open and patch. Akgun says seven weeks of open
  chat is not a substitute for that move. Roediger
  says the feeling that restudy worked is a five-minute
  result.
- **Ask the model to quiz, wait, and grade.** Paste
  the source. “Ask me one short-answer question at
  a time. Do not reveal the answer until I attempt.
  Then score and correct.” Tullis’s delayed-test
  condition. Mollick’s 2023 quiz prompt. If it answers
  first, you are restudying.
- **Do not restudy the deck before the call.** The
  five-minute test in 2006 rewarded rereading. The
  week-later test did not. For anything that has to
  survive until Thursday, close the slides and name
  the argument.
- **One cold pass on load-bearing work.** Issue 007
  still owns checking the number while you can see
  it. This week owns yesterday’s number with the
  sheet closed. If you cannot reconstruct it, you
  cannot notice when the next model run drifts.
- **Space the retrieval, don’t binge the chat.**
  Carpenter, Pan, and Butler still own the calendar.
  Three short cold recalls beat one long reread of
  the same summary. Akgun matched session count
  and still found structure inside the session.

### In personal life

- **Close the explanation, then teach it back.** A
  Wikipedia page or a chatbot primer is restudy.
  After it, write five lines as if to a smart friend.
  Then ask the model to mark the gaps. Karpicke
  and Blunt: even a concept map you copy while
  looking is weaker than retrieval.
- **Flashcards beat saved chats.** A thread you can
  search is Issue 006’s archive. A card you cannot
  answer is this week’s test. Photograph the
  handwritten card later if you need filing.
- **Use JOLs as a warning light.** “That was easy, I
  have it” is the 2011 prediction error. Add a cold
  recall when fluency is high, not when it is low.
- **Quizzes for kids, answers for nobody.** Issue 014
  still owns homework-with-hints. This week’s family
  version is the same sentence with a delay: tonight
  the model may ask; it may not narrate the chapter
  again.

## 6. Myth vs. Reality

> **Myth:** Science has proved that AI destroys memory,
> that any use of a chatbot during study is cheating
> your hippocampus, and that more rereading will
> fix it if you just concentrate.
>
> **Reality:** The testing effect is one of the most
> replicated findings in memory research: about half
> a standard deviation against restudy in Rowland’s
> meta, about the same in Yang’s classroom meta,
> larger in some labs, smaller in others. Students
> (and knowledge workers) systematically prefer the
> worse activity because it feels like learning.
> 2026 evidence says the *same model* can host
> retrieval or host restudy. Akgun’s open-chat arm
> underperformed structured retrieval with no extra
> sessions and no extra tokens required. Tullis’s
> students thought rereading had worked. None of
> this is a lesion. None of it says abandon the
> model. It says fluency is not retention, and a
> recap you never reconstruct is restudy with better
> typography.

## 7. Caveats & Honest Limitations

- **Akgun & Toker is a preprint** in one
  upper-division statistics course. Effort was coded
  from logs (Levene’s test failed for that outcome).
  G2 versus G3 on the posttest was *d* = 0.57,
  *p* = .071 after correction — directionally
  consistent, not a second independent win. Do not
  advertise “AI quizzes raise grades by ten points.”
  Advertise: open-ended AI study was the weakest
  follow-up after a shared pretest.
- **Tullis & Heshmati 2026 is a poster.** Direction
  only until a paper reports *N*, *d*, and the delay.
  We did not invent percentages.
- **Heshmati posted de-identified data** (Zenodo,
  29 October 2026) from two experiments on using
  ChatGPT *to answer* practice questions. We did
  not re-analyse those sheets. The title is a
  hypothesis the lab is testing, not a result we
  will quote.
- **Barcaui 2025 (N = 120, 57.5% vs 68.5%,
  *d* = 0.68)** already headlined Issue 006. It is
  unrestricted ChatGPT versus traditional study, not
  a retrieval manipulation. Cite it only as
  corroboration that unguided AI study can flatten
  retention. Drop-out to *t*(83) means 85 took the
  surprise test.
- **Wiklund-Hörnqvist N = 50; Karlsson Wirebring
  N = 23 (18 in main imaging).** Word pairs, not
  strategy memos. They tell you retrieval recruits
  hippocampus and varies parietal representations.
  They do not tell you ChatGPT shrinks those
  regions.
- **Karpicke & Blunt and Roediger & Karpicke are
  educational passages, not AI studies.** The leap
  we are willing to take is narrow: a fluent page
  you restudy is the restudy condition. A closed
  pane is the test.
- **Metas average heterogeneous designs.** Rowland
  *g* = 0.50 and Yang *g* = 0.499 are medium.
  Immediate tests can reverse the effect. Feedback,
  delay, and test format all move the estimate.
- **Do not claim AI damages the brain.** Nothing
  here is a drop in IQ, a lesion, or a reason to
  panic. The evidence is about patterns of use:
  whether review is a reconstruction or a reread.
